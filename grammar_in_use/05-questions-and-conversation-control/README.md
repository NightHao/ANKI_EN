# Chapter 5 — Questions and conversation control

## Use it when

You want to start a real exchange, check that you understood, sound considerate, or keep an IELTS answer moving without turning it into a speech. Good questions use the normal question form; good conversation control also gives the other person an easy way to answer.

## Core contrast

In a direct question, put an auxiliary before the subject:

> **Do** you work from home?  
> **Have** you finished it?  
> **Why did** they cancel the trip?

If there is no auxiliary, use `do / does / did` and the base verb:

> What **do** you usually cook?  
> Where **did** you put the receipt?

But when the question word is itself the subject, do not add `do` or change the normal subject–verb order:

> Who called you?  
> What happened after the meeting?

Compare:

> Who did you call? *(who = object)*  
> Who called you? *(who = subject)*

For an embedded question after `Do you know`, `Could you tell me`, or `I was wondering`, use statement order. It is polite and also essential in longer speaking turns.

> Could you tell me **where the station is**?  
> I am not sure **why she left early**.

Not: `Could you tell me where is the station?`

## Natural examples

Open questions invite detail:

> What do you enjoy most about your neighbourhood?  
> How did you get interested in photography?

Choice questions narrow the topic without sounding abrupt:

> Do you prefer working early, or are you more productive at night?  
> Is the issue the price, or is it the delivery time?

Use short questions to show you are following:

> “I changed jobs last month.” “Oh, **did you**? How is it going?”  
> “The train was cancelled.” “**Was it**? What did you do?”

Question tags usually check shared expectations, not seek a completely new fact. A falling voice sounds fairly sure; a rising voice asks for confirmation.

> This is the right platform, **isn’t it**?  
> You have met Hana before, **haven’t you**?  
> They did not reply, **did they**?

The tag normally reverses the polarity: positive statement + negative tag, or negative statement + positive tag. With `I am`, the usual tag is `aren’t I?`

> I am next, aren’t I?

For IELTS, use questions as bridges rather than as memorised fillers:

> I would say public transport has improved. **The bigger question is whether it is reliable outside the city centre.**  
> I have not lived abroad, but **if I had the chance, I would ask local people how they found the adjustment.**

That lets you introduce a contrast, qualification, or example naturally.

## Common failure

Do not use question order inside an indirect question:

> Wrong: Do you know what time does it start?  
> Better: Do you know what time it starts?

Do not add `do` when `who` or `what` is the subject:

> Wrong: Who did call you? *(unless you are strongly emphasising “who”)*  
> Better: Who called you?

Avoid a tag when you actually need information. `You know the answer, don’t you?` can sound like pressure. Prefer `Do you know the answer?` when you do not know.

## Say it yourself

1. Turn this into a polite embedded question: `Where can I charge my phone?`
2. Ask about the subject, then the object, in this idea: `Mina recommended a podcast.`
3. Add a natural tag: `The shop closes at six, ...?`
4. In a two-minute answer about technology, use one direct question to involve the listener and one embedded question to qualify your opinion.
