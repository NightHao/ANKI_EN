# Chapter 7 — Nouns, articles, and quantity

## Use it when

You introduce a thing, refer back to it, make a general statement, or say how much or how many. These small choices make spoken English sound clear because they tell the listener whether you mean one example, a known thing, or things in general.

## Core contrast

Use `a / an` with one singular countable noun when the listener does not yet know which one:

> I bought **a** lamp for my desk.  
> She is **an** architect.

Use `the` when the listener can identify the thing from the situation, earlier conversation, or a following detail:

> The lamp is much brighter than I expected.  
> Could you close the window?  
> I liked the café we tried on Sunday.

Use no article for general plural countable nouns and general uncountable nouns:

> **Dogs** need regular exercise.  
> **Music** helps me focus.

Compare the meaning:

> I need **a coffee**. *(one drink)*  
> I drink **coffee** every morning. *(the drink generally)*  
> The coffee in this office is surprisingly good. *(a particular coffee)*

Countable nouns can be singular or plural: `idea / ideas`, `ticket / tickets`. Uncountable nouns usually do not take `a/an` or a plural `-s`: `advice`, `information`, `equipment`, `furniture`, `research`. Give them a container or measure:

> a piece of advice; some information; two items of equipment; a bit of research

Use `some` in positive statements when the exact amount is not important, and `any` especially in negatives and ordinary questions:

> I need some help.  
> Do you have any questions?  
> We did not have any time.

But use `some` in an offer or request when you expect or invite “yes”:

> Would you like some tea?  
> Could I have some water?

## Natural examples

For quantity, match the noun type:

> There are **a few** seats left. *(some; probably enough)*  
> There are **few** seats left. *(not many; a problem)*

> We have **a little** time. *(some time remains)*  
> We have **little** time. *(almost none)*

Use `many / few` with countable plurals and `much / little` with uncountable nouns. In casual positive speech, `a lot of` is often more natural than `much`:

> I have a lot of emails today.  
> How much luggage are you taking?  
> How many languages do you speak?

`Each` looks at members one by one; `every` treats a whole group as a routine or rule:

> Each guest received a name tag.  
> I call my parents every Sunday.

Use `another` for one more or a different singular item, and `other` with plural or uncountable nouns:

> Could I have another minute?  
> What other options do we have?

## Common failure

Singular countable nouns normally need a determiner:

> Wrong: I bought new phone.  
> Better: I bought a new phone.  
> Better: I bought my new phone yesterday.

Do not pluralise an uncountable noun just to mean “many”:

> Wrong: She gave me many advices.  
> Better: She gave me a lot of advice.  
> Better: She gave me several useful pieces of advice.

Do not add `the` merely because a noun feels important. Say `I like travelling` for the activity generally, but `the travel arrangements` for a particular set of arrangements.

## Say it yourself

1. Describe something you bought recently: introduce it with `a/an`, then refer to it with `the`.
2. Make one general statement and one specific statement about food, transport, or music.
3. Correct: `I need informations about other country.`
4. Answer an IELTS-style question about a busy week using `a few`, `a little`, and `a lot of` accurately.
