# Chapter 3 — Modals and attitude

## Use it when

You want to avoid sounding too absolute. Modals let you show ability, possibility, obligation, advice, permission, and your attitude toward an idea—the difference between “This is true” and “This might be true.”

## Core contrast

Use **can** for general ability or practical possibility: “I can focus better in the morning.” Use **could** for past general ability, a more tentative possibility, or a softer request: “Could you repeat that?” For one successful past event, prefer *was/were able to* or *managed to*: “I managed to finish before the deadline.”

Use **must** for the speaker’s strong conclusion or requirement: “You must be exhausted after that flight”; “I must remember to reply.” Use **have to** for an external rule or necessity: “I have to show my ID at reception.” In everyday speech, *have to* is often more natural for duties.

Negatives change sharply. **Mustn’t** means prohibition: “You mustn’t park here.” **Don’t have to / don’t need to** means no necessity: “You don’t have to come early.” It does not mean you are forbidden.

Use **should** for advice or an expected outcome, not an order: “You should take breaks”; “The parcel should arrive tomorrow.” Use **might/may/could** when a result is possible but uncertain. *Might* often sounds the least committed: “It might be worth trying.”

To judge the past, use modal + **have + past participle**: “She must have missed the bus” (strong deduction); “I should have started earlier” (regret/advice about the past); “They might have misunderstood” (uncertain explanation).

## Natural examples

- “I can usually understand different accents, but I could struggle if someone speaks very quickly.”
- “In my job, I have to respond quickly, although I don’t have to be online all evening.”
- “You mustn’t share the password, but you can reset it yourself.”
- “People should be allowed to work flexibly when the role permits it.”
- “The restaurant might be busy, so we could reserve a table.”
- “I should have asked more questions instead of pretending I understood.”

In IELTS, modals make claims measured and credible: “Technology can make learning more accessible, but it may also distract students.” That balanced contrast is stronger than an overconfident universal statement.

## Common failure

**Confusing lack of necessity with prohibition.**

> “You mustn’t wear a suit” when you mean it is optional.

Say: “You don’t have to wear a suit.” Say *mustn’t* only if it is not allowed.

**Adding *to* after a modal.**

> “I can to speak English.”

Say: “I can speak English.” After a modal, use the base verb: *can go, should study, might help*.

**Using *must* for every strong opinion.** “People must exercise” can sound like a rule. “People should exercise” is normally better advice.

## Say it yourself

1. Give two rules at work or school: one required and one forbidden.
2. Give one piece of advice to a visitor to your city, then soften it politely.
3. Explain two possible reasons a friend is late: one strong deduction and one uncertain guess.
4. Describe one thing you should have done differently last year, without overexplaining it.
