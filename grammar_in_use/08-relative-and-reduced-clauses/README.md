# Chapter 8 — Relative and reduced clauses

## Use it when

You need to identify a person or thing, add a useful detail, or make a noun phrase more compact in speech. A full relative clause is the safe default. A reduced clause is useful when its shorter form keeps the meaning clear. For fuller reference and clause-building checks, see [the cheat sheet](../../cheat-sheet.md).

## Core contrast

A **subject relative** tells us what the noun does. Keep the relative word:

> The neighbour **who lives upstairs** is a nurse.  
> I need an app **that tracks expenses**.

`Who/that` is the subject of `lives/tracks`; deleting it would leave no subject.

An **object relative** tells us what happens to the noun. You may omit `who`, `that`, or `which` in a defining clause:

> The neighbour **who I met yesterday** is a nurse.  
> The neighbour **I met yesterday** is a nurse.  
> The film **that we watched** was excellent.

Here, `I` and `we` are already the subjects. Do not omit the relative word in a non-defining comment, after a fronted preposition, or with `whose`:

> My neighbour, who I met yesterday, is a nurse.  
> The colleague to whom I wrote replied quickly.  
> The student whose laptop disappeared was upset.

## Natural examples

Use a full clause when it gives time, a modal, a negative, or a separate event you want the listener to notice:

> I spoke to the person **who had organised the event**.  
> We need a route **that does not involve two buses**.  
> The package **that must be signed for** is at reception.

Reduction makes a noun phrase shorter. Choose the form from the noun’s role.

> people **waiting** outside *(people who are waiting: active `V-ing`)*  
> the photos **taken** at the festival *(photos that were taken: passive past participle)*  
> the bridge **being repaired** *(bridge that is being repaired: ongoing passive)*

The contrast is about voice, not simply whether the action is “present” or “past”:

> a customer **asking** for help  
> a customer **asked** to wait  
> a customer **being asked** to wait

Use an infinitival relative for an available task, purpose, expected action, or an order/ranking:

> She was the first person **to arrive**.  
> There are several forms **to complete**.  
> I need someone **to talk to**.

`Noun + to-infinitive` is especially natural in phrases such as `something to eat`, `a place to sit`, and `time to think`. The understood actor may be the speaker, listener, or contextually obvious person:

> Do you have anything to read on the flight?  
> We need a quiet place to work.

## Common failure

Reduction can remove information. `The staff working on Monday` does not tell us whether they *are working now*, *usually work*, or *will work*. `The form to be signed` may express an expected task, but it does not automatically preserve `must be signed` or `has to be signed`.

Keep the full clause when tense, aspect, obligation, negation, or the relationship between two events matters:

> Safer: The documents that were being checked when the alarm rang were damaged.  
> Safer: I need a colleague who can speak Japanese.

Do not reduce every passive clause. A past participle can describe a completed/resulting state, while `being + past participle` highlights a process:

> the road closed after the flood  
> the road being closed for repairs

Also avoid a reduced form if it could attach to the wrong noun or make the listener work too hard. In spontaneous IELTS speaking, one accurate full relative clause is better than a compressed phrase whose timing is unclear.

## Say it yourself

1. Describe a colleague using one subject relative and one object relative; omit the object relative word once.
2. Expand these: `the guests waiting outside`; `the reports being reviewed`.
3. Say what you would pack for a trip using `something to ...` and `a place to ...`.
4. Describe a local change. Use a full relative clause for the important time or modal meaning, then explain why reducing it would lose that meaning.
