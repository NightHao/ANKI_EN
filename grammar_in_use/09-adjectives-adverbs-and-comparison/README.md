# Chapter 9 — Adjectives, adverbs, and comparison

## Use it when

You want to describe an experience precisely: what something was like, how an action happened, or how two choices differ. In IELTS Speaking, comparison is useful for extending an answer; in ordinary conversation, it helps you sound specific rather than dramatic.

## Core contrast

An **adjective** describes a noun or follows a linking verb: *a noisy café; the café was noisy*. An **adverb** usually describes an action, an adjective, or another adverb: *We left quietly; surprisingly cheap; very slowly.*

Do not assume every word ending in *-ly* is an adverb. *Friendly, lively,* and *likely* are adjectives. Some common adverbs have no *-ly*: *fast, hard, late, early.* Compare *a hard exam* with *I worked hard*. But *hardly* means “almost not”: *I hardly slept.*

For a simple comparison, use **-er / more**: *quieter, more reliable*. Use **than** for the reference point: *My current commute is shorter than my old one.* For a clear difference, add *much, far, a lot, slightly,* or *a bit*: *Online meetings are slightly less tiring when they are well run.* Do not say *more easier* or *very better*.

Use **as … as** for equality and **not as/so … as** for a weaker quality: *The new flat is not as central as the old one, but it is much brighter.* Superlatives normally need **the**: *the most useful advice I received.* After a superlative, a present perfect can make the time frame natural: *It’s the best concert I’ve been to this year.*

## Natural examples

> I used to choose the cheapest option, but now I care more about whether it will last.

> The neighbourhood feels safer at night because the streets are better lit.

> I speak more slowly when I explain a technical idea, especially to someone new to it.

> My brother is the more patient one; I tend to make decisions faster.

> It was a surprisingly relaxing trip. We did almost nothing, which was exactly what we needed.

> The further you live from the centre, the more important a reliable bus service becomes.

That last pattern, **the + comparative, the + comparative**, describes a linked change. It is useful when it expresses a real observation, not as a prepared “advanced” sentence.

## Common failure

**Wrong:** *I was very boring during the meeting.*  
**Better:** *I was very bored during the meeting.*

*Boring* describes the thing that creates the feeling; *bored* describes the person who feels it. The same distinction appears in *interesting/interested, confusing/confused,* and *exhausting/exhausted*.

**Wrong:** *This phone is more cheaper than mine.*  
**Better:** *This phone is cheaper than mine.* / *This phone is much cheaper than mine.*

Choose one comparison form, then add a degree word only if you mean it. Also keep the comparison logical: *The rent is higher than last year’s* is clearer than comparing rent with a whole year.

## Say it yourself

Choose two places you know. Speak for 45 seconds:

- Give one similarity with *as … as*.
- Give two differences, one small and one large.
- Add one reason with *because* or *so*.
- Finish with a personal judgement: *Overall, I’d choose … because …*

Then describe a recent difficult or enjoyable experience. Include one *-ed/-ing* adjective pair correctly: *I was exhausted, but the result was satisfying.*
