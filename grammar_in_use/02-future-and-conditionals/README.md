# Chapter 2 — Future and conditionals

## Use it when

You are talking about what is arranged, intended, expected, imagined, or dependent on another event. These forms help an answer move beyond “yes/no” into reasons and consequences.

## Core contrast

Use the **present continuous** for a personal arrangement with some reality behind it: “I’m meeting my cousin on Saturday.” Use **going to** for an intention or a prediction based on present evidence: “I’m going to apply after I update my portfolio”; “Look at those clouds—it’s going to rain.” Use **will** for a decision made while speaking, a neutral prediction, willingness, or an offer: “I’ll send you the link tonight.”

For schedules and time clauses, English often uses a present form even for the future: “The train leaves at 7:10”; “I’ll call you when I get home.” Do not say *when I will get home* in this ordinary time-clause meaning.

Conditionals are meaning patterns, not a test of how many forms you can name.

- **Real/open possibility:** “If I have time tonight, I’ll cook.”
- **Unreal present or unlikely future:** “If I had more time, I’d cook more.”
- **Unreal past:** “If I’d left earlier, I would have caught the train.”

The past form in the second pattern signals distance from reality, not past time. “If I were you” is the most conventional advice form, though “was” is common in informal speech.

## Natural examples

- “I’m flying to Singapore next month; the tickets are already booked.”
- “I think remote work will remain common, but offices won’t disappear completely.”
- “I’m going to cut down on takeaway food because I’ve spent too much recently.”
- “If the weather is decent, we’ll go cycling. Otherwise, we’ll find a café.”
- “If I could redesign my city, I’d add more shaded walking routes.”
- “If I had known the queue would be that long, I would have booked online.”

For a nuanced IELTS answer, combine a conditional with a reason: “If public transport were cheaper, more people would use it, especially students and older residents.” This is useful range because it expresses a real idea, not because it is an “advanced grammar” trick.

## Common failure

**Putting *will* after *if* for a normal condition.**

> “If it will rain, we’ll stay inside.”

Say: “If it rains, we’ll stay inside.” Use *will* in an if-clause only when willingness matters: “If you’ll wait a moment, I’ll check.”

**Mixing the time logic.**

> “If I would have more money, I will travel.”

Choose the meaning: “If I have more money, I’ll travel” (possible future), or “If I had more money, I’d travel” (not true now).

**Calling every plan *going to*.** “I’m going to meet Ana” may be fine, but “I’m meeting Ana at six” makes the fixed arrangement clearer.

## Say it yourself

1. Give one arrangement, one intention, and one instant decision about next week.
2. Predict a change in your work, city, or study life. Add evidence for one prediction.
3. Finish these naturally: “If I get a free evening, …”; “If I had a free month, …”; “If I’d prepared better, …”
4. Answer: “Would you move abroad?” Use one conditional and one realistic detail.
