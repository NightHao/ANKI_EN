# Chapter 1 — Time and tense

## Use it when

You need to show whether something is a habit, happening around now, finished, or still connected to now. In speaking, tense is not decoration: it tells the listener how to place your experience.

## Core contrast

**Present simple** is for a stable fact, routine, or regular arrangement: “I usually walk to work.” **Present continuous** is for an activity in progress, a temporary situation, or a developing change: “I’m walking more lately because the weather is good.” Do not use the continuous form for every present meaning; *I’m knowing* and *I’m believing* are normally wrong.

**Past simple** closes a time period: “I lived in Kaohsiung in 2022.” **Present perfect** connects a past event or period to now: “I’ve lived in Kaohsiung, so I know the food scene well.” The crucial question is not “Is it recent?” but “Is the past time finished and named?” Say *I saw it yesterday*, not *I’ve seen it yesterday*.

Use **present perfect continuous** for an activity or situation viewed as continuing or recently producing a result: “I’ve been preparing for this exam for two months.” Use the simple perfect when the result, number, or completion matters: “I’ve written three practice answers.”

**Past continuous** supplies the background or an interrupted activity: “I was cooking when my friend called.” **Past perfect** is useful only when you need to move further back than a past reference point: “By the time I arrived, the film had started.” In a simple sequence, past simple is clearer: “I arrived and the film started.”

## Natural examples

- “I work from home most days, but this week I’m commuting because my internet is unreliable.”
- “I’ve tried learning Spanish twice. The first time, I gave up after a month.”
- “I’ve been listening to more podcasts recently, mainly on my way to the gym.”
- “When I was at university, I stayed up far too late. I’m much more careful now.”
- “I had already applied when I heard the role was being filled internally.”

For IELTS, make the timeline explicit with small anchors: *these days, over the last few years, when I was younger, since then, by the time*. That sounds more controlled than adding unusual tenses without a reason.

## Common failure

**Using present perfect with a finished time.**

> “I’ve graduated in 2023.”

Say: “I graduated in 2023.” A year is a completed, named period. But: “I’ve graduated, so I’m looking for a full-time job” is possible because the present consequence is central.

**Forgetting that some verbs describe states.**

> “I’m understanding why public transport matters now.”

Prefer: “I understand why public transport matters now.” However, some state verbs can become continuous when the meaning changes: “I’m having lunch” (an activity), not “I’m having a car” (possession).

**Overusing past perfect.** It does not make a story more advanced. Use it to prevent confusion, then return to the simple past.

## Say it yourself

Answer each aloud in two or three sentences.

1. Describe a normal weekday, then one temporary difference this week. Use present simple + continuous.
2. Describe one experience that still affects your view today, then say exactly when it happened. Use present perfect + past simple.
3. Explain something you have been working on recently and give one measurable result.
4. Tell a short story using “I was ___ when ___” and, only if needed, “had already ___.”

Record yourself once. Check that every tense choice helps the listener locate the time; replace any tense used merely because it sounds more formal.
