# `EN::Patterns`

Production-frame cards for natural American English. Each level-two heading is
a card Front (exactly as shown in Anki); the text below it is the Back.
Related alternatives that do the same job share one card, separated by ` · `.
Rules: [`BACK_LAYOUT.md`](BACK_LAYOUT.md), [`../CARD_AUTHORING.md`](../CARD_AUTHORING.md), and
[`PATTERN_FRAME_SELECTION.md`](PATTERN_FRAME_SELECTION.md). After editing, run
`python3 cards/tools/build_import.py`.

## `I think/believe/feel (that) + clause · I do not think/believe + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

State a personal judgment.

### `I think/believe/feel (that) + clause`

State your judgment; think/believe overlap, while feel can emphasize a personal impression. Believe can sound more considered, but none of these words establishes an objective certainty level.

- I think the simpler design is easier to maintain.
- I believe the simpler design is easier to maintain.
- I feel that the team needs more uninterrupted time.

### `I do not think/believe + clause`

Give a negative judgment politely, or say you withhold belief.

- I do not think the deadline is realistic.
- I do not believe the estimate includes testing.

## `In my opinion/view, + clause · From my perspective/point of view, + clause · Personally, + clause · As far as I am concerned, + clause · If you ask me, + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Mark a view as your own.

### `In my opinion/view, + clause`

Mark a judgment as your opinion; opinion and view are close alternatives. These expressions mark perspective, not different levels of certainty. Use in my opinion, but from my point of view.

- In my opinion, the shorter commute is worth the higher rent.
- In my view, the shorter commute is worth the higher rent.

### `From my perspective/point of view, + clause`

Frame the same kind of judgment from your particular standpoint.

- From my perspective, the main problem is the lack of clear ownership.
- From my point of view, the main problem is the lack of clear ownership.

### `Personally, + clause`

Mark your own preference or judgment.

- Personally, I would rather have flexible hours than a private office.

### `As far as I am concerned, + clause`

State your position; this can sound firm when setting a boundary.

- As far as I am concerned, the matter is settled.

### `If you ask me, + clause`

Offer a conversational, often frank opinion.

- If you ask me, that meeting could have been an email.

## `I would say + clause · I guess/suppose/imagine + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Offer a tentative judgment.

### `I would say + clause`

Soften an assessment rather than presenting it as certain.

- I would say the trial was successful, although we still have some bugs to fix.

### `I guess/suppose/imagine + clause`

Guess/suppose can sound hesitant; imagine can infer someone else's experience. Imagine often infers a situation you have not experienced yourself; tone allows substantial overlap.

- I guess we could postpone it.
- I suppose we could postpone it. — Either can signal hesitant acceptance.
- I imagine working across three time zones gets exhausting.

## `I bet/I'll bet + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Express an informal confident expectation or inference, often as a friendly reaction. A real wager is unnecessary. This is conversational; use a more neutral judgment when presenting evidence in formal writing.

### `I bet/I'll bet + clause`

Express an informal confident expectation or inference, often as a friendly reaction. A real wager is unnecessary. This is conversational; use a more neutral judgment when presenting evidence in formal writing.

- I bet the earlier flight will be cheaper.
- I'll bet the earlier flight will be cheaper.
- You had three interviews today? I bet you're exhausted.

## `In my experience, + clause · As far as I know, + clause · From what I have heard, + clause · Apparently, + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Identify the basis or limits of a claim.

### `In my experience, + clause`

Ground the claim in your firsthand experience.

- In my experience, written decisions reduce misunderstandings across time zones.

### `As far as I know, + clause`

Limit the claim to your knowledge or observations; you may be missing information.

- As far as I know, the office is still open on Fridays.
- As far as I can tell, the rollout succeeded; the error rate is back to normal.

### `From what I have heard, + clause`

Attribute the information to something you heard.

- From what I have heard, the new manager is very approachable.

### `Apparently, + clause`

Present something as apparently true from reports or evidence, without guaranteeing it.

- Apparently, the venue has changed; I just received a new invitation.

## `It seems/appears that + clause · It seems/looks/sounds/feels like + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Present an impression rather than a confirmed fact.

### `It seems/appears that + clause`

Present an impression with a that-clause; appears often sounds more formal.

- It seems that the update helped.
- It appears that the update helped.

### `It seems/looks/sounds/feels like + clause`

Present an impression with a like-clause; looks/sounds can express an inference too.

- It seems like we have enough time.
- It looks like we have enough time.
- It sounds like the client wants a smaller first release.
- It feels like we are solving the same problem twice.

## `look/sound/feel/taste/smell + adjective · look/sound/feel/taste/smell like + noun`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Describe a perceived quality with an adjective; use like before a thing you compare it with.

### `look/sound/feel/taste/smell + adjective`

Put an adjective after the perception verb to describe the subject's quality. Use tastes/smells good for a pleasant quality; well normally describes how an action is performed.

- The interface looks clean, the proposal sounds reasonable, and the controls feel responsive.
- The sauce tastes smoky, and the kitchen smells wonderful.

### `look/sound/feel/taste/smell like + noun`

Put a noun after like to say what the subject resembles.

- It looks like a charger, sounds like a fan, and feels like rubber.
- The soup tastes like coconut, and the room smells like fresh paint.

## `What does X look like? · How does X look?`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Ask about appearance; descriptions and evaluations can overlap.

### `What does X look like?`

Ask for a description or resemblance.

- What does the new office look like? — It has large windows and an open kitchen.

### `How does X look?`

Ask about appearance or invite an evaluation; the answers can overlap with the first question.

- How does the new office look? — Bright and welcoming.

## `I assume/presume + clause · Assuming (that) + clause, main clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Accept something as true without confirmation, or state a working premise.

### `I assume/presume + clause`

Accept a premise without confirmation; presume often sounds more formal.

- I assume the meeting is still at three.
- I presume the meeting is still at three.
- For this estimate, let's assume everyone is available for two hours.

### `Assuming (that) + clause, main clause`

State the premise your conclusion depends on.

- Assuming the requirements stay the same, we can finish by Friday.

## `I suspect + clause · I have a feeling (that) + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Present an unconfirmed belief or intuition.

### `I suspect + clause`

Offer an unconfirmed belief or possible explanation; wrongdoing is not required.

- I suspect the cache is involved, but we need more evidence.

### `I have a feeling (that) + clause`

Present an intuition more explicitly.

- I have a feeling that she would prefer a quieter restaurant.

## `I am sure/certain/convinced/confident (that) + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

State a strong belief or assurance. Sure and certain are close alternatives; convinced stresses that you hold a belief firmly. Confident often stresses assurance about an outcome or ability. Context allows substantial overlap; the wording does not prove the claim. Pretty sure softens sure: you feel fairly confident while leaving room for doubt.

### `I am sure/certain/convinced/confident (that) + clause`

State a strong belief or assurance. Sure and certain are close alternatives; convinced stresses that you hold a belief firmly. Confident often stresses assurance about an outcome or ability. Context allows substantial overlap; the wording does not prove the claim. Pretty sure softens sure: you feel fairly confident while leaving room for doubt.

- I am sure this is the right address.
- I am certain this is the right address.
- I am pretty sure this is the right address.
- I am convinced that the old process needs to change.
- I am confident that we can finish the migration this month.

## `I realize/understand (that) + clause · I can see that + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Acknowledge or recognize a situation.

### `I realize/understand (that) + clause`

Acknowledge a situation; realize often highlights becoming aware of it.

- I realize that the deadline is tight, but we still need to check the results.
- I understand that you need an answer today.

### `I can see that + clause`

Signal recognition of a situation or reasoning, without necessarily agreeing.

- I can see that the current process is causing frustration.

## `I am not sure whether/if + clause · I have no idea + wh-/whether-clause · I am not entirely convinced that + clause · I could be wrong, but + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Express uncertainty or qualified doubt.

### `I am not sure whether/if + clause`

Leave a yes-or-no question unresolved; whether and if both work here.

- I am not sure whether this plan includes dental coverage.
- I am not sure if it includes dental coverage.

### `I have no idea + wh-/whether-clause`

Say you have no information; follow with a question-word clause in statement order.

- I have no idea why the build failed.

### `I am not entirely convinced that + clause`

Express qualified doubt; unconvinced by names the explanation that failed to persuade you. Also use be/remain unconvinced by an explanation or unconvinced that + clause. This does not prove the claim false.

- I am not entirely convinced that adding more meetings would help.
- I remain unconvinced by that explanation.

### `I could be wrong, but + clause`

Allow that your own judgment could be mistaken before giving it.

- I could be wrong, but I think we tested a different version.

## `may/might/could + base verb · There is a chance/possibility that + clause · be likely/unlikely to + verb · be bound to + verb`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Express possibility or likelihood without claiming certainty.

### `may/might/could + base verb`

Present something as possible; these modals do not encode fixed percentages.

- The release may slip.
- The release might slip.
- The release could slip.

### `There is a chance/possibility that + clause`

Introduce a possible outcome explicitly with a that-clause.

- There is a chance that the venue will be available.
- There is a possibility that the venue will be available.

### `be likely/unlikely to + verb`

Present a probable or improbable outcome; likely also allows it is likely that. Likely suggests probable, and unlikely suggests improbable, rather than fixed numerical probabilities.

- Traffic is likely to get worse after five; the roads are unlikely to clear before seven.
- It is likely that we will need another reviewer.

### `be bound to + verb`

Make a confident prediction, often based on experience.

- Something is bound to go wrong on the first day, so plan some extra time.

## `There is evidence that + clause · The results suggest/indicate/show/confirm that + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Report evidence and the conclusion drawn from it.

### `There is evidence that + clause`

Say that supporting information exists, without automatically claiming proof.

- There is evidence that the new schedule has reduced staff turnover.

### `The results suggest/indicate/show/confirm that + clause`

Choose the reporting verb to fit the evidence: suggest allows uncertainty; confirm verifies an earlier claim. Indicate points to a conclusion and can overlap with suggest/show; show presents a finding as demonstrated. There is no fixed verb-by-verb certainty scale. Singular evidence takes suggests/indicates/shows/confirms; plural results take suggest/indicate/show/confirm.

- The results suggest/indicate that network latency is affecting response times.
- We suspected the cache was serving old data. The logs showed/confirmed that it was returning last week’s results.

## `According to + source, + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Attribute information to a person, report, or other source. This does not necessarily mean you endorse the claim. To state your own view directly, use in my opinion rather than according to me.

### `According to + source, + clause`

Attribute information to a person, report, or other source. This does not necessarily mean you endorse the claim. To state your own view directly, use in my opinion rather than according to me.

- According to the latest report, bus ridership rose last year.
- According to Maya, the revised estimate includes testing.

## `always/usually/often/sometimes/occasionally/rarely/hardly ever/never + main verb · be + frequency adverb · every now and then / from time to time · How often ...?`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Describe how frequently something happens; these expressions do not represent fixed percentages.

### `always/usually/often/sometimes/occasionally/rarely/hardly ever/never + main verb`

Put a frequency adverb before an ordinary main verb, or after the first auxiliary. Always means every time; usually most of the time; often frequently; sometimes/occasionally on some occasions; rarely/hardly ever very infrequently; never at no time. These are not fixed percentages.

- I usually work from home.
- We often review difficult decisions together, and we always document the reasoning.
- I sometimes attend networking events, but I rarely stay until the end.
- I hardly ever check work email on vacation, and I never take work calls during family dinners.

### `be + frequency adverb`

Put the frequency adverb after be.

- I am occasionally in the office.

### `every now and then / from time to time`

Describe occasional events with these similar phrases; beginning or end position is natural. Occasionally can also appear before a main verb. These phrases can occur at the beginning or end of a clause.

- Every now and then, I attend an industry meetup.
- I attend industry meetups from time to time.

### `How often ...?`

Ask about frequency; answer with a frequency phrase or rate.

- How often do you travel for work? — About once a month.

## `tend to + verb · In general/Generally/In most cases, + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Describe a tendency while allowing exceptions.

### `tend to + verb`

Describe characteristic behavior while allowing exceptions. A tendency does not specify an exact frequency or exclude exceptions.

- I tend to focus better when I have a clear deadline.

### `In general/Generally/In most cases, + clause`

Introduce a generalization rather than a claim about every case.

- In general, shorter meetings are easier to schedule.
- Generally, shorter meetings are easier to schedule.
- In most cases, a written update is enough.

## `The main reason + clause + is that + clause · One reason for + noun/V-ing + is that + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Give an explanation.

### `The main reason + clause + is that + clause`

Name the most important reason; follow is that with its explanation.

- The main reason I chose this job is that I can work with an international team.

### `One reason for + noun/V-ing + is that + clause`

Introduce one contributing reason; for takes a noun or V-ing.

- One reason for the delay is that the requirements changed.
- One reason for recording meetings is that colleagues can catch up later.

## `because/since/as + clause · because of/due to + noun/V-ing`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Give a cause.

### `because/since/as + clause`

Give a reason with a complete clause; since/as can also have time meanings. Because makes the cause explicit; use it if since/as could be misunderstood as time.

- We postponed the launch because the tests failed.
- Since everyone is here, we can start.
- As everyone is here, we can start.

### `because of/due to + noun/V-ing`

Give a reason with a noun phrase or V-ing, rather than a bare subject–verb clause.

- We postponed the launch because of the failed tests.
- The delay was due to a supplier shortage.
- We made fewer errors because of double-checking the totals.

## `clause, so + result · As a result/Therefore/Consequently, + clause · That is why + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Present a result or conclusion.

### `clause, so + result`

Join the cause and result in one sentence; this is common in conversation.

- The trains were canceled, so we drove.

### `As a result/Therefore/Consequently, + clause`

Introduce a consequence or logical conclusion; use a sentence boundary or semicolon where needed. As a result emphasizes consequence; therefore often emphasizes reasoning; consequently is more formal. These uses overlap.

- The trains were canceled. As a result, we drove.
- Every request needs a token. This request has none; therefore, it will be rejected.
- The supplier closed its factory. Consequently, production stopped.

### `That is why + clause`

Explain why the previously named outcome happened.

- The buses stop running at ten. That is why I booked a taxi.

## `Given/Considering + noun/that-clause · Based on + evidence · In light of + facts · On that basis, + conclusion`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Present the grounds for a judgment or recommendation.

### `Given/Considering + noun/that-clause`

Bring relevant circumstances into your reasoning; given and considering often overlap.

- Given our budget, we should start small.
- Given that we have only two weeks, we should reduce the scope.
- Considering that we have only two weeks, we should reduce the scope.
- Considering our budget, we should start small.

### `Based on + evidence`

Name the evidence or criteria used to reach your judgment; a what-clause also works. According to instead attributes information to a source; it does not itself state your inference.

- Based on the benchmarks, I would choose Model A.
- On the basis of the benchmarks, I would choose Model A.
- Based on what you have told me, the smaller team sounds like a better fit.

### `In light of + facts`

Highlight relevant facts that inform a judgment or reassessment.

- In light of the recent failures, we should reconsider the design.

### `On that basis, + conclusion`

Refer back to grounds already stated and give the resulting decision.

- The model failed our security tests. On that basis, we rejected it.

## `One possible explanation is that + clause · It might have something to do with + noun · X has nothing to do with Y`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Suggest an explanation without presenting it as established.

### `One possible explanation is that + clause`

Propose an explanation without presenting it as established.

- One possible explanation is that users cannot find the search button.

### `It might have something to do with + noun`

Suggest a connection that still needs investigation.

- It might have something to do with the cached credentials.

### `X has nothing to do with Y`

Deny a connection; this is a stronger claim than proposing a possible explanation.

- The crash has nothing to do with your change; it started before you deployed.

## `The cause of X is Y · What is causing + noun?`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Identify or ask about the cause of a problem or event.

### `The cause of X is Y`

Identify a cause; distinguish it from a symptom or possible explanation.

- The cause of the timeout was a blocked connection; the slow interface was a symptom.

### `What is causing + noun?`

Ask which factor is producing the problem or event.

- What is causing the sudden increase in response time?

## `(in order) to + verb · so that + subject + can/could + verb · The goal is to + verb · be designed to + verb`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Explain purpose.

### `(in order) to + verb`

Express the actor's purpose; in order to makes it explicit, and in an effort to stresses the attempt. An effort does not guarantee success. In diving in an effort to avoid boats, in belongs to in an effort to, not to dive in.

- I muted notifications to concentrate.
- I muted notifications in order to concentrate.
- I muted notifications in an effort to concentrate.

### `so that + subject + can/could + verb`

State a purpose while explicitly naming who should benefit or act.

- I shared the recording so that colleagues in other time zones could watch it.

### `The goal is to + verb`

Name an intended objective, without claiming it was achieved.

- The goal of the trial is to identify confusing steps.

### `be designed to + verb`

Explain intended function; design does not guarantee success.

- The interface is designed to make common tasks easier.

## `clause, but + clause · Although/Though/Even though + clause, main clause · Despite/In spite of + noun/V-ing, main clause · statement. However, statement. · clause, though`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Something happened even though another fact might make you expect otherwise. These forms can express the same message after restructuring.

### `clause, but + clause`

Join contrasting clauses. But also has broader contrast uses beyond an unexpected outcome.

- I was tired, **but I finished the review**.

### `Although/Though/Even though + clause, main clause`

Use a complete subject–verb clause; even though emphasizes the unexpected result.

- **Although I was tired**, I finished the review.
- **Though I was tired**, I finished the review.
- **Even though I was tired**, I finished the review.

### `Despite/In spite of + noun/V-ing, main clause`

Use a noun or V-ing: despite my exhaustion / in spite of being tired. A complete clause requires the fact that. Never use despite of.

- **Despite being tired**, I finished the review.
- **In spite of being tired**, I finished the review.
- **Despite my exhaustion**, I finished the review.
- **Despite the fact that I was tired**, I finished the review.

### `statement. However, statement.`

Introduce a contrasting statement after a period or semicolon; often more formal than but.

- I was tired. **However, I finished the review**.
- I was tired; **however, I finished the review**.

### `clause, though`

Add a conversational contrast at the end. Although and even though cannot occupy this position.

- I was tired. **I finished the review, though**.

## `That said/Still/Even so/Nevertheless, + clause · While it is true that + clause, main clause · X may be true, but Y`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Acknowledge a point before limiting its implications.

### `That said/Still/Even so/Nevertheless, + clause`

Qualify a preceding point or stress what remains true despite it; nevertheless is more formal.

- The tool saves time. That said, we still need to check its output.
- The tool sometimes misses errors. Still, it saves time.
- The tool sometimes misses errors. Even so, it saves time.
- The tool sometimes misses errors. Nevertheless, it saves time.

### `While it is true that + clause, main clause`

Explicitly concede a point before stating a limitation.

- While it is true that the tool saves time, it still needs supervision.

### `X may be true, but Y`

Concede that X may be true while maintaining Y; may need not express uncertainty here.

- The tool may be useful, but it cannot replace a careful review.

## `clause, whereas/while + clause · On the one hand, + clause; on the other hand, + clause · One advantage/disadvantage of + noun/V-ing + is that + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Use these expressions to contrast facts or weigh the benefits and drawbacks of an option.

### `clause, whereas/while + clause`

Contrast two facts; while can also express time.

- I prefer written feedback, whereas my manager prefers a call.
- I prefer written feedback, while my manager prefers a call.

### `On the one hand, + clause; on the other hand, + clause`

Weigh two competing considerations without requiring either to be false.

- On the one hand, the job pays well; on the other hand, the hours are unpredictable.

### `One advantage/disadvantage of + noun/V-ing + is that + clause`

Name a benefit or drawback; the flip side introduces another aspect, while the downside names a drawback. The flip side can introduce a positive or negative aspect; comes at the cost of names a sacrifice.

- One advantage of remote work is that I avoid a long commute.
- One disadvantage is that I miss spontaneous conversations.
- Working from home gives me flexibility. The flip side is that I sometimes feel isolated.
- On the flip side, I have more time with my family.
- The downside is that the simpler design offers fewer options.
- Simplicity comes at the cost of customization.

## `I agree/disagree with + noun · I agree to some extent/up to a point, but + clause · I see your point, but + clause · That is a valid concern · To be fair, + clause · I have a different take on + noun`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Acknowledge another viewpoint before responding.

### `I agree/disagree with + noun`

State agreement or disagreement with a person, view, or conclusion.

- I agree with your assessment.
- I disagree with that conclusion.

### `I agree to some extent/up to a point, but + clause`

Agree partly, then introduce a reservation with but.

- I agree to some extent, but a higher salary is not the only priority.
- I agree up to a point, but a higher salary is not the only priority.

### `I see your point, but + clause`

Acknowledge someone's reasoning without necessarily accepting their conclusion.

- I see your point, but I would still prefer to test it first.

### `That is a valid concern`

Recognize a reasonable concern without necessarily agreeing with every proposed response.

- That is a valid concern. We should check who can access the recordings.

### `To be fair, + clause`

Introduce a consideration that deserves recognition.

- To be fair, the team had only two days to prepare.

### `I have a different take on + noun`

Offer a different interpretation of the same issue.

- I have a different take on the survey results: price may matter more than speed.

## `X does not necessarily mean Y · Just because + clause + does not mean + clause · X does not guarantee Y`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

One fact alone is not enough to establish a conclusion or ensure an outcome. All three work in this situation: the first two question an inference, and the third emphasizes the lack of certainty.

### `X does not necessarily mean Y`

Say the conclusion is not automatic, rather than saying it is always false. Shape: noun/V-ing + does not necessarily mean + noun/clause.

- Compiling successfully **does not necessarily mean** the program works correctly.

### `Just because + clause + does not mean + clause`

Push back against an assumption using two complete clauses. This expresses essentially the same idea as the first pattern.

- **Just because the program compiles does not mean it works correctly.**

### `X does not guarantee Y`

Deny certainty about an outcome, even when the first fact makes it more likely. Follow guarantee with a noun or that-clause.

- Compiling successfully **does not guarantee that** the program works correctly.

## `It is not that X; it is (just) that Y · It’s not like + clause · It’s not like + person + to + verb · I am not saying X; I am saying Y · The problem is not X; it is Y`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Correct a possible interpretation by stating the intended distinction.

### `It is not that X; it is (just) that Y`

Reject an assumed reason and give the real one; it's just that can soften a refusal.

- It is not that I dislike the idea; it is that we cannot afford it yet.
- I’d love to come. It’s just that I have a deadline tomorrow.

### `It’s not like + clause`

Dismiss a worry or assumption: it is not as if that situation were true or likely.

- Just ask him. It’s not like he’s going to say no.

### `It’s not like + person + to + verb`

Describe behavior as unusual for the named person.

- It’s not like her to miss a deadline. I hope everything is okay.

### `I am not saying X; I am saying Y`

Clarify your intended claim by rejecting a misunderstanding.

- I am not saying we should cancel the release; I am saying we should reduce its scope.

### `The problem is not X; it is Y`

Distinguish the real problem from an incorrectly identified one.

- The problem is not a lack of effort; it is a lack of clear priorities.

## `If anything, + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Use this to suggest that the opposite interpretation, or a stronger version of your point, is more accurate.

### `If anything, + clause`

Use this to suggest that the opposite interpretation, or a stronger version of your point, is more accurate.

- The change hasn’t simplified the process. If anything, it has made it more confusing.
- I don’t think remote work has reduced my productivity. If anything, I get more done.
- The trip didn’t make me less interested in living abroad. If anything, it strengthened that interest.

## `In terms of + noun/V-ing · in this/that regard/respect`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Limit an evaluation to one aspect, measure, or activity.

### `In terms of + noun/V-ing`

Name the aspect, measure, or activity you are evaluating.

- In terms of cost, the second proposal is more practical.
- In terms of reducing emissions, improving public transportation may have the greatest impact.
- In terms of long-term health, regular exercise matters more than short-term weight loss.

### `in this/that regard/respect`

Refer back to an aspect already clear from context; this/that and regard/respect often overlap. In that sense instead refers to an interpretation, rather than simply to an aspect already named.

- Model A uses less memory. In this regard, it has an advantage.
- In that regard, it has an advantage.
- The revised interface is easier to navigate. In this respect, it is an improvement.
- In that respect, it is an improvement.

## `What I need/value/like most is + noun/clause · What matters most is + noun/clause · What I struggle with most is + noun/V-ing`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Put the focus at the end of the sentence.

### `What I need/value/like most is + noun/clause`

Focus on what you need, value, or like; is introduces the important information.

- What I need most is uninterrupted time to finish the analysis.
- What I value most is that the team shares feedback openly.
- What I like most is the freedom to choose how I work.

### `What matters most is + noun/clause`

Identify the most important consideration.

- What matters most is whether we can maintain the system.

### `What I struggle with most is + noun/V-ing`

Identify the activity or issue you find most difficult.

- What I struggle with most is explaining complicated ideas briefly.

## `The thing is, + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Use this in conversation to introduce the key fact or complication that the listener needs to understand.

### `The thing is, + clause`

Use this in conversation to introduce the key fact or complication that the listener needs to understand.

Use an open condition for general rules or real possibilities.

### `If + present, + present/will/can/may`

State a general rule or a real future possibility, normally with a present form after if.

- If I skip lunch, I lose concentration.
- If I finish early, I will join you.

### `as long as/provided that + clause`

State a requirement; provided that is more formal than as long as.

- You can work remotely as long as you are available for the meeting.
- We can proceed provided that the client approves the budget.

### `only if + clause`

State a necessary condition; it does not guarantee the outcome.

- I will accept the offer only if the hours are flexible.

## `unless + clause · if not, + clause · otherwise, + clause · in that case/if that’s the case, + clause · That’s not the case`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Unless usually means if ...

### `unless + clause`

Usually express if ... not; check the meaning instead of substituting mechanically.

- Unless the weather improves, we will move the event indoors.

### `if not, + clause`

Negate a clear condition from the preceding question or statement.

- Can you send it today? If not, tomorrow morning is fine.

### `otherwise, + clause`

State what follows if a preceding requirement is not met.

- Please save a copy; otherwise, you could lose your changes.

### `in that case/if that’s the case, + clause`

Respond to the situation just described; both expressions work here.

- The venue is unavailable? In that case, we should meet online.
- If that’s the case, we should meet online.

### `That’s not the case`

Say that the described situation is not true.

- A: I heard the office is closing. B: That’s not the case; it’s just moving to the fourth floor.

## `Even though + fact, + clause · Even if + possible case, + clause · whether or not/no matter what/how + clause · It doesn’t matter + wh-/if-clause · regardless of + noun/V-ing/whether-clause`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Make an outcome independent of a circumstance while preserving the distinction between fact and possibility.

### `Even though + fact, + clause`

Treat the contrasting circumstance as a fact.

- Even though the task is difficult, I will try it.

### `Even if + possible case, + clause`

Treat it as a possible condition, without asserting it is true.

- Even if the task is difficult, I will try it.

### `whether or not/no matter what/how + clause`

Say the result is independent of both alternatives or of what/how something happens.

- Whether or not they approve the proposal, we need to document the risks.
- No matter what happens, I will keep you informed.
- No matter how busy I am, I make time for a short walk.

### `It doesn’t matter + wh-/if-clause`

State that a variation does not affect the result, using a complete sentence.

- It doesn't matter whether they approve it; we still need to document the risks.
- It doesn't matter which framework you use, as long as the tests pass.

### `regardless of + noun/V-ing/whether-clause`

Use a noun, V-ing, or whether-clause; a bare subject–verb statement does not follow regardless of.

- We need to document the risks regardless of their decision.
- All participants need a ticket, regardless of attending online or in person.
- We will publish the results regardless of whether the findings support our hypothesis.

## `in case + clause · just in case · in case of + noun`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Describe a precaution with in case: prepare before you know whether the event will happen.

### `in case + clause`

Describe a precaution before knowing whether an event will happen; compare if as a condition. Use a present form for a future event after in case.

- I saved a local copy in case the connection fails.
- If the connection fails, I will switch to the local copy.

### `just in case`

Leave the anticipated event unstated because context makes it clear.

- I do not expect rain, but I brought a jacket just in case.

### `in case of + noun`

Give an instruction for the event named by a noun phrase.

- In case of a power outage, use the stairs.

## `What if + clause? · Let’s say/Suppose + clause`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Invite someone to consider a hypothetical scenario.

### `What if + clause?`

Raise a hypothetical worry or propose an option.

- What if the client changes the requirements?
- What if we start with a smaller trial?

### `Let’s say/Suppose + clause`

Establish an imagined case for discussion.

- Let’s say we have only two weeks.
- Suppose we have only two weeks. What would you cut?

## `depend on + noun/whether/how-clause · Depending on + noun/wh-clause, + main clause · Whether + clause + depends on + noun/wh-clause`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Use depend on to name what determines an answer or outcome.

### `depend on + noun/whether/how-clause`

Name the factor or unresolved question that determines the outcome.

- The timeline depends on whether the client approves the design.
- It depends on how much testing we need.

### `Depending on + noun/wh-clause, + main clause`

Introduce a variable that changes which action is appropriate. This names a variable affecting the outcome, not personal dependence on someone.

- Depending on the weather, we may move the dinner inside.

### `Whether + clause + depends on + noun/wh-clause`

Make the yes-or-no outcome the subject, then name what determines it.

- Whether we travel this weekend depends on how much work remains.

## `If + past form, + would/could + base verb · If + subject + were to + verb, + would + base verb · Without + noun, + would/could + base verb`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Imagine a present or future situation as hypothetical or remote.

### `If + past form, + would/could + base verb`

Imagine a remote present or future case; the past form need not refer to past time. Were is standard for hypothetical be; was also occurs in conversation. Could describes an imagined possibility or ability.

- If I had more time, I would take a longer trip.
- What would you do if you were in my position?
- If I were you, I would ask for the details in writing.

### `If + subject + were to + verb, + would + base verb`

Present a future possibility as especially hypothetical, often in a sensitive discussion.

- If the company were to cut the budget, which project would you keep?

### `Without + noun, + would/could + base verb`

Express an absent condition and its imagined consequence.

- Without clear requirements, we could spend weeks solving the wrong problem.

## `If + had + past participle, + would/could have + past participle · If + had + past participle, + would + base verb + now`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Imagine a different past and its consequence.

### `If + had + past participle, + would/could have + past participle`

Imagine a different past and a past consequence; could have expresses a possibility. Without + noun can replace a clear condition; preserve the time of its imagined consequence.

- If we had left earlier, we would have caught the train.
- Without your help, I could not have finished the application.

### `If + had + past participle, + would + base verb + now`

Imagine a different past with a consequence that belongs to now.

- If I had accepted that offer, I would live in Chicago now.

## `I hope + clause · I wish + past form · I wish + had + past participle · I wish + would + verb`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Express desired outcomes, unreal wishes, or regret about the past.

### `I hope + clause`

Express a desired outcome that may be true or become true.

- I hope the interview goes well.
- I hope you enjoyed the trip.

### `I wish + past form`

Express an unreal or unsatisfactory present; could can name an unavailable ability or option.

- I wish I had more time to travel.
- I wish I could join you, but I have to work.

### `I wish + had + past participle`

Regret a past situation by imagining an alternative past.

- I wish I had asked about the schedule before accepting.

### `I wish + would + verb`

Express a desired change, often in another person's behavior. This is a desired change rather than merely a neutral prediction.

- I wish they would tell us about changes earlier.

## `could have + past participle · should/should not have + past participle`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Could have can describe an unrealized possibility or a possible explanation; context determines which.

### `could have + past participle`

Describe an unused possibility or a possible explanation; context determines which.

- I could have taken a taxi, but I decided to walk. — An unused option.
- The message could have gone to spam. — A possible explanation.

### `should/should not have + past participle`

Evaluate a past choice or express regret; should have can also express expected completion.

- I should have checked the address.
- I should not have rushed the review.

## `must/might/cannot + base verb · must/might/could not have + past participle · should + base verb`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Distinguish inference, possibility, and expectation.

### `must/might/cannot + base verb`

Draw a current inference, allow a possibility, or rule it out. Must here can express an inference rather than an obligation.

- She must be busy; she has not answered.
- She might be busy, but I am not sure.
- That cannot be the final version; it still has comments in it.

### `must/might/could not have + past participle`

Place the inference or possibility in the past with have + past participle.

- They must have missed the email.
- They might have missed the email.
- He could not have sent it yesterday; his account was still locked.

### `should + base verb`

Predict an expected event; should have predicts expected completion by a reference time.

- The package should arrive soon.
- The package should have arrived by now.

## `plan/decide/hope/agree/promise + to + verb · decide against + noun/V-ing · think about + V-ing`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

### `plan/decide/hope/agree/promise + to + verb`

Name an intended action after these verbs; each main verb keeps its own meaning. Do not assume every verb permits both to and V-ing complements.

- We plan to expand the trial, but we have not agreed to a date.
- I decided to apply.
- I hope to finish this week, and I promised to send an update tomorrow.

### `decide against + noun/V-ing`

Reject an option; against takes a noun or V-ing.

- I decided against applying.

### `think about + V-ing`

Consider an activity without committing to it.

- I have been thinking about taking a course before I decide.

## `have to/have got to/need to/must + verb · do not have to/do not need to + verb · did not have to + verb · be going to have to + verb · cannot/must not + verb · be supposed to + verb · be supposed to be + adjective/noun`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Describe necessity, lack of necessity, prohibition, and expectations.

### `have to/have got to/need to/must + verb`

Express necessity; have got to is conversational, while have to works across tenses.

- I have to submit the form today.
- I need to submit it today.
- I must submit it today.
- I have got to leave soon; the last train is at ten.

### `do not have to/do not need to + verb`

Remove necessity: the action is optional, rather than prohibited.

- You do not have to attend.
- You do not need to attend. — Attendance is optional.

### `did not have to + verb`

Say an action was unnecessary in the past; You didn't have to! can thank someone for a kindness.

- I didn’t have to pay for parking because the meter was broken.
- You didn’t have to bring anything, but thank you!

### `be going to have to + verb`

Announce a coming necessity, often an unwelcome one, relatively gently.

- I’m going to have to leave a little early today.

### `cannot/must not + verb`

Prohibit an action; this is different from not having to do it.

- You cannot share that password.
- You must not share it. — It is prohibited.

### `be supposed to + verb`

Describe an expectation, rule, or arrangement; was supposed to alone does not prove failure.

- We are supposed to check in by six.
- I was supposed to lead the meeting, and I did.

### `be supposed to be + adjective/noun`

Report what people say about something's quality or identity.

- That new taco place is supposed to be really good.

## `should + verb · might want to + verb · had better + verb · would be better off + V-ing · Have you tried + V-ing?`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Give advice.

### `should + verb`

Give a direct recommendation; use a base verb without to.

- You should ask which expenses are covered.

### `might want to + verb`

Soften a recommendation.

- You might want to save a copy before changing the settings.

### `had better + verb`

Give a warning about an important consequence; this can sound pushy for a small suggestion. Had better, like should, takes a base verb without to.

- We had better leave now, or we will miss the last train.

### `would be better off + V-ing`

Say an option would leave someone in a better position; use V-ing.

- You’d be better off asking HR directly.

### `Have you tried + V-ing?`

Suggest a possible solution by asking whether it has been tried.

- Have you tried restarting the router?

### `might as well/may as well + base verb`

Choose a sensible available option when there is no compelling reason to do something else. Might as well and may as well overlap; might as well is a natural conversational choice. This is not simply a prediction that you might act.

- We are already downtown, so we might as well visit the museum.

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Say an action should happen now.

### `It is time (for + person) to + verb`

Neutrally signal that an action should happen now.

- It is time for us to leave.

### `It is (about) time + subject + past form`

Suggest an action is overdue; the past form refers to now, not a past event.

- It is time we left.

## `prefer + noun/V-ing + to + noun/V-ing · would prefer to + verb · would rather + base verb + than + base verb · instead of + noun/V-ing · rather than + parallel alternative`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Express a preference or describe the alternative chosen.

### `prefer + noun/V-ing + to + noun/V-ing`

Express a preference; to in prefer A to B and in preference to is a preposition, so an action takes V-ing. In preference to is formal; prefer A to B, choose A over B, and rather than are often more conversational.

- I prefer working from home to commuting every day.
- We used reusable containers in preference to disposable ones.
- They chose to repair the device in preference to replacing it.

### `would prefer to + verb`

Express a desired current choice with to + base verb. Prefer often states a general preference; would prefer/would rather often state a current choice, with overlap.

- I would prefer to meet online.

### `would rather + base verb + than + base verb`

Express a preference with a base verb after would rather, without to.

- I would rather meet online than drive across town.

### `instead of + noun/V-ing`

Name the option not taken; of takes a noun or V-ing.

- We met online instead of driving across town.

### `rather than + parallel alternative`

Contrast parallel alternatives; repair and replace are parallel base verbs here.

- We chose to repair the system rather than replace it.

- I would love to join you for dinner.

- The legend is in the upper-left corner, and the compass is in the lower-right corner.

### `at/near/along the edge of`

At marks the boundary, near proximity to it, and along a path following it.

- A gate stands at the edge of the park, several homes are near the edge, and a path runs along the edge.

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- diagram: en-prepositions-master-v1.svg -->
<!-- diagram: en-prepositions-location-v1.svg -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Describe location, using spatial relationships and conventional combinations.

### `at + point/site`

Treat the location as a point or site.

- The information booth is at the entrance.

### `on + surface/line`

Describe contact with a surface or position on a line.

Locate something within an area or enclosure.

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Locate events in time with in, on, or at.

Use in with general periods such as months, seasons, and general parts of a day.

## `to the left/right of · next to/beside/right next to · by/near · adjacent to · between/among · around · across from/opposite · in front of/behind · above/below · over/under/beneath · on top of`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- diagram: en-prepositions-position-v1.svg -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Locate something relative to another object or place.

### `to the left/right of`

Compare horizontal positions relative to a reference point.

- The elevators are to the left of the entrance, and the restrooms are to the right of it.

### `next to/beside/right next to`

Describe immediately neighboring positions; right next to emphasizes closeness.

- The café is next to the bank, a bench is beside the fountain, and the ATM is right next to the door.

### `by/near`

Describe proximity; by can mean beside/near, while near need not mean immediately adjacent.

- A bench stands by the river, while several cafés are near the station.

### `adjacent to`

Describe adjacency in a more formal way than next to/beside.

- The parking garage is adjacent to the hospital.

### `between/among`

Between presents distinct reference points; among locates something within a group. Between can use more than two reference points when they are treated as distinct.

- The café is between the bank and the bookstore, while the statue stands among the trees.

### `around`

Describe surrounding positions; at the front instead identifies part of a larger space. About can also mean around an area, but around is more common for location in AmE.

- Guests sat around the table; their host stood at the front of the room.

### `across from/opposite`

Locate something on the other side facing a reference point; both expressions work.

- The pharmacy is across from the hotel, and the bus stop is opposite the station.

### `in front of/behind`

Locate something ahead of or behind another object; before an audience is a separate formal use.

- Taxis wait in front of the building, while staff parking is behind it.
- The speaker stood before a large audience; her colleague waited behind the curtain.

### `above/below`

Compare higher/lower positions without requiring direct vertical alignment or contact.

- A sign is above the entrance, a bench is below it, a canopy hangs over it, and several bicycles are stored under it.

### `over/under/beneath`

Describe above/below positions; over/under can emphasize directly vertical or covering relations, and beneath is often more formal.

- A sign is above the entrance, a bench is below it, a canopy hangs over it, and several bicycles are stored under it.
- A tunnel runs beneath the highway, and a communications antenna sits on top of the building.

### `on top of`

Describe a position on the upper surface of something, often with contact.

- A tunnel runs beneath the highway, and a communications antenna sits on top of the building.

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Describe a compass relationship, a region, a side, or an endpoint.

### `north/south/east/west of`

- The station is north of the mall, the stadium is south of it, the school is east of it, and the park is west of it.

### `northeast/northwest/southeast/southwest of`

- The four clinics are northeast, northwest, southeast, and southwest of downtown, respectively.

### `at the northern/southern/eastern/western end of X`

<!-- overview-card -->
<!-- grouped-layout -->

Describe proximity, measured distance, or travel time.

### `near + noun`

Put the nearby reference point directly after near.

- The hotel is near the station.

### `close to + noun`

Include to when close has a reference-point complement.

- The café is close to the hotel.

Use nearby without a complement, or before a noun.

- There is a pharmacy nearby, and a nearby convenience store stays open all night.

- My apartment is pretty close to campus.

### `not far from`

Describe a short distance using a negative far statement.

- The station is not far from the campus, my apartment, or the laboratory.

### `in (fairly) close proximity to`

Use a formal alternative to close to; fairly can qualify the closeness.

- The clinic is in close proximity to the hospital, and the frog lives in fairly close proximity to water.

Describe a large distance; quite/pretty modify its degree. Pretty far, like pretty close, is conversational AmE.

### `a long way/some distance from`

Describe walking duration with a singular unit in the compound adjective.

### `within walking distance of`

State travel time and the mode of transportation.

- The airport is 30 minutes away by car, while the city center is 20 minutes away by train.

## `... + (that/who/whom/which/when/where/why) + subject + verb`

<!-- grammar-card -->

This card focuses on deleting exactly one optional word. Usually every other word stays in place. If a preposition comes immediately before the deleted word, move the preposition to the end first.

**1**

In these patterns, the joining word can disappear when another subject and verb follow:

`think/say/believe/know/find + that + subject + verb`
`→ remove that`

I believe that public transportation should be more affordable.
→ I believe public transportation should be more affordable.

`be sure/glad/afraid + that + subject + verb`
`→ remove that`

I am glad that the city has expanded the bus network.
→ I am glad the city has expanded the bus network.

`noun + that/who/whom/which + subject + verb`
`→ remove that/who/whom/which`

The report that the auditors released identified three control failures.
→ The report the auditors released identified three control failures.

The neighborhood that many families moved into has become more diverse.
→ The neighborhood many families moved into has become more diverse.

`day + when / place + where / reason + why + subject + verb`
`→ remove when/where/why`

I remember the day when we first met.
→ I remember the day we first met.

The place where we first met is now a community center.
→ The place we first met is now a community center.

The main reason why many families moved was the high cost of living.
→ The main reason many families moved was the high cost of living.

**2**

Do not remove only one word in these forms:

`that/who/which + verb directly`
`→ do not remove only that/who/which`

The report that identified three control failures was released yesterday.
≠ The report identified three control failures was released yesterday.

`, who/which ... ,`
`→ do not remove only who/which`

The new policy, which took effect in January, has reduced waiting times.
≠ The new policy, took effect in January, has reduced waiting times.

`whose + noun`
`→ do not remove whose`

Students whose families have low incomes may qualify for financial assistance.
≠ Students families have low incomes may qualify for financial assistance.

**3**

Do not leave a preposition directly before an empty space:

`preposition + whom/which + subject + verb`
`→ subject + verb + preposition`

The city in which I grew up has changed considerably.
→ The city I grew up in has changed considerably.

≠ The city in I grew up has changed considerably.

## `noun/linker + (subject + be) + V-ing/past participle/adjective/PP`

<!-- grammar-card -->

This card deletes a subject together with **am/is/are/was/were**. The remaining words must clearly describe another noun or subject.

**1**

`noun + who/that/which + am/is/are/was/were + description`
`→ noun + description`

The students who are studying abroad often become more independent.
→ The students studying abroad often become more independent.

The data that were collected in rural areas reveal a significant gap in health care.
→ The data collected in rural areas reveal a significant gap in health care.

The bridge that is being repaired connects two residential areas.
→ The bridge being repaired connects two residential areas.

Communities that are in remote areas often lack reliable public transportation.
→ Communities in remote areas often lack reliable public transportation.

The next issue that is to be discussed concerns affordable housing.
→ The next issue to be discussed concerns affordable housing.

`, who/which + am/is/are/was/were + description,`
`→ , description,`

The new library, which was funded by local donations, opened last month.
→ The new library, funded by local donations, opened last month.

**2**

`while/when/if/although/even though + subject + am/is/are/was/were + description`
`→ while/when/if/although/even though + description`

While she was a student, she worked part-time at a local clinic.
→ While a student, she worked part-time at a local clinic.

If it is necessary, the policy can be revised next year.
→ If necessary, the policy can be revised next year.

Although it was designed for small towns, the program was adopted nationwide.
→ Although designed for small towns, the program was adopted nationwide.

Even though she was nervous, Maya gave a clear presentation.
→ Even though nervous, Maya gave a clear presentation.

When the removed subject names a person or thing, it must refer to the subject of the main sentence. Impersonal phrases such as **if necessary** and **if possible** are also normal.

While Maya was studying, her phone rang.
≠ While studying, her phone rang.

The shortened sentence incorrectly makes **her phone** the understood subject of **studying**.

**3**

After a noun, the remaining words must fit naturally directly after that noun.

An adjective with extra information can often remain:

`eligible for financial aid`
`responsible for the project`
`available to local residents`

Applicants who are eligible for financial aid should apply online.
→ Applicants eligible for financial aid should apply online.

A noun normally cannot remain directly after another noun:

The volunteers who are doctors can provide medical care.
≠ The volunteers doctors can provide medical care.

A short adjective normally moves before the noun:

The measures that are expensive may be difficult to maintain.
≠ The measures expensive may be difficult to maintain.

The measures that are expensive may be difficult to maintain.
→ The expensive measures may be difficult to maintain.

A complete main sentence cannot lose its subject and **be**:

That policy is expensive.
≠ Expensive.

## `noun + V-ing / while/when + V-ing`

<!-- grammar-card -->

Use this change when the noun performs a current or general action and the exact tense is not important.

**1**

`noun + who/that/which + active verb`
`→ noun + V-ing`

Students who live abroad often become more independent.
→ Students living abroad often become more independent.

Policies that affect low-income families should be evaluated carefully.
→ Policies affecting low-income families should be evaluated carefully.

`, who/which + active verb,`
`→ , V-ing,`

The survey, which covers more than 2,000 households, provides useful data on housing costs.
→ The survey, covering more than 2,000 households, provides useful data on housing costs.

`noun + who/that + do/does not + verb`
`→ noun + not + V-ing`

→ Applicants not meeting the income requirement will not qualify.

**2**

`while/when + subject + active verb`
`→ while/when + V-ing`

While they wait for the bus, they often check their phones.
→ While waiting for the bus, they often check their phones.

When you compare the two proposals, you should consider their long-term costs.
→ When comparing the two proposals, you should consider their long-term costs.

The person understood after **while** or **when** must also perform the main action.

**3**

Do not use ordinary **V-ing** when it would remove meaning carried by a modal:

`who + can/may/must/will/should + verb ≠ V-ing`

Employees who can work from home may choose a more affordable place to live.
≠ Employees working from home may choose a more affordable place to live.

**Can work** describes an ability or option. **Working** describes what the employees are actually doing.

Applicants who must provide additional evidence will receive an email.
≠ Applicants providing additional evidence will receive an email.

**Must provide** describes a requirement. **Providing** describes what the applicants are actually doing.

## `Having + past participle / Having been + ..., main clause`

<!-- grammar-card -->

**Having + ...** can shorten a whole clause about a situation earlier than the main situation. It does not mechanically replace every **has**, **have**, or **had** phrase.

**1**

For an earlier completed action:

`because/after/once + subject + has/have/had + past participle`
`→ having + past participle`

Because Maya has completed the required training, she is eligible to apply.
→ Having completed the required training, Maya is eligible to apply.

After the researchers had completed the safety inspection, they reopened the laboratory.
→ Having completed the safety inspection, the researchers reopened the laboratory.

**Has/have** uses the present as its reference point. **Had** uses a past reference point. **Having** has no tense of its own; the main sentence supplies the time.

When **after** or **once** already makes the order clear, the full clause may use the simple past:

`because/after/once + subject + completed simple-past action`
`→ having + past participle`

After the committee reviewed all the evidence, it rejected the proposal.
→ Having reviewed all the evidence, the committee rejected the proposal.

**2**

Keep negative, passive, and continuing meanings in the shortened form:

`has/have/had not + past participle`
`→ not having + past participle`

Because she had not received enough information, Maya postponed her decision.
→ Not having received enough information, Maya postponed her decision.

`has/have/had (not) been + past participle`
`→ (not) having been + past participle`

Because the proposal has been revised, it now addresses the main concerns.
→ Having been revised, the proposal now addresses the main concerns.

`has/have/had (not) been + V-ing`
`→ (not) having been + V-ing`

Because Maya had been working from home for several months, she found it difficult to return to the office.
→ Having been working from home for several months, Maya found it difficult to return to the office.

**Having completed** describes an earlier completed action. **Having been working** describes activity continuing over an earlier period and does not necessarily say that the activity was completed.

**3**

The subject of the main sentence must refer to the same person or thing as the subject removed from the earlier clause.

After the city had raised parking fees, many commuters began using public transportation.
≠ Having raised parking fees, many commuters began using public transportation.

The shortened sentence incorrectly says that **the commuters** raised the parking fees.

These forms are mainly useful in formal writing. In everyday conversation, a full **after**, **because**, or **once** clause is often more natural.

## `(for + person +) to + verb / wh-word + to + verb`

<!-- grammar-card -->

**To + verb** commonly describes an action that is needed, possible, planned, or performed for a purpose. It does not normally describe a completed fact.

**1**

Choose the form according to who will perform the action:

`action for an understood person → noun + to + verb`

I have three reports that I need to finish before Friday.
→ I have three reports to finish before Friday.

We need somewhere where we can stay for two nights.
→ We need somewhere to stay for two nights.

`action for a named person → noun + for + person + to + verb`

She prepared a short guide that new employees could use.
→ She prepared a short guide for new employees to use.

`noun receives the action → noun + to be + past participle`

The city published a list of buildings that inspectors will examine this year.
→ The city published a list of buildings to be examined this year.

The application lists several forms that must be completed.
→ The application lists several forms to be completed.

Use the passive form when the person performing the action is unimportant or already clear. **To be completed** presents the forms as a required task, but it does not state the force of **must** as explicitly. Keep **must** when the obligation itself is important.

**2**

For a purpose, keep the person who will perform the purpose action:

`same subject + so that + same subject + can/could + verb`
`→ same subject + to + verb`

Maya enrolled in an evening course so that she could improve her academic writing.
→ Maya enrolled in an evening course to improve her academic writing.

`so that + different subject + can/could + verb`
`→ for + subject + to + verb`

The city built a playground so that children could exercise safely.
→ The city built a playground for children to exercise safely.

The city built a playground so that children could exercise safely.
≠ The city built a playground to exercise safely.

The incorrect sentence suggests that **the city** will exercise.

**3**

`the first/next/last/only + noun + who/that + verb`
`→ the first/next/last/only + noun + to + verb`

Nadia was the first student who completed the survey.
→ Nadia was the first student to complete the survey.

Norway was the only country that met the target that year.
→ Norway was the only country to meet the target that year.

**4**

`who/what/where/when/how + subject + should/can + verb`
`→ who/what/where/when/how + to + verb`

`whether + subject + should + verb`
`→ whether + to + verb`

The person making the choice or performing the action must be clear from context.

I was not sure what I should include in the introduction.
→ I was not sure what to include in the introduction.

We have not decided where we should hold the meeting.
→ We have not decided where to hold the meeting.

The committee must determine how it can reduce unnecessary spending.
→ The committee must determine how to reduce unnecessary spending.

We have not decided whether we should apply for additional funding.
→ We have not decided whether to apply for additional funding.

Maya asked what Daniel should include in the report.
≠ Maya asked what to include in the report.

The shortened sentence would normally make **Maya**, not **Daniel**, the person choosing what to include.

Do not make the same change with **why**:

I do not understand why I should wait.
≠ I do not understand why to wait.

**5**

Do not use **to + verb** for a completed fact:

A policy that reduced emissions was expanded nationwide.
≠ A policy to reduce emissions was expanded nationwide.

The first sentence says that the policy actually reduced emissions. The second describes its purpose, not its actual result.

## `do/auxiliary/modal/to + (repeated action)`

<!-- grammar-card -->

When an action or description is already clear, keep only the small word that carries its tense, negative, ability, or other meaning.

**1**

Choose the small word that remains:

`ordinary verb → do/does/did`
`can/could/may/might/will/would/should/must + repeated action → keep the modal`
`has/have/had + repeated words → keep has/have/had`
`am/is/are/was/were + description/action → keep am/is/are/was/were`
`verb + to + repeated action → keep to`

Maya works remotely, and Daniel works remotely too.
→ Maya works remotely, and Daniel does too.
→ Maya works remotely, and so does Daniel.

Maya cannot attend the workshop, and Daniel cannot attend it either.
→ Maya cannot attend the workshop, and Daniel cannot either.
→ Maya cannot attend the workshop, and neither can Daniel.

Maya has submitted her application, and Daniel has submitted his application too.
→ Maya has submitted her application, and Daniel has too.

The downtown clinic is overcrowded, and the suburban clinic is overcrowded too.
→ The downtown clinic is overcrowded, and the suburban clinic is too.

I did not attend the workshop, although I had planned to attend it.
→ I did not attend the workshop, although I had planned to.

In American English, **have** meaning possession normally uses **do**:

`have/has + possession → do/does`

Maya has flexible working hours, and Daniel has flexible working hours too.
→ Maya has flexible working hours, and Daniel does too.

**2**

The same omission works in comparisons and concise coordination:

`than + subject + small word`

Housing costs have risen faster in the city than they have risen in nearby towns.
→ Housing costs have risen faster in the city than they have in nearby towns.

`subject + verb + information, and subject + different information`

The city invested two million dollars in buses, and the county invested one million dollars in bike lanes.
→ The city invested two million dollars in buses, and the county one million dollars in bike lanes.

The second structure is concise and slightly formal. Repeating the verb is usually more natural in everyday conversation.

**3**

Do not omit the action when the result could be misunderstood:

The manager asked Maya to revise the report, and Daniel did too.

This could be misread as Daniel also asking Maya or Daniel revising the report. Repeat the intended action when the shorter sentence is unclear.

## `number/quantity + (repeated noun) / adjective + one/ones`

When a noun is already clear, a number or quantity word can often stand without repeating that noun.

**1**

`number + repeated count noun → number`
`some/any/many/few/a few/several/more/most/fewer/enough/all/both/each/either/neither + repeated count noun → quantity word`

## `Me too · Me neither · So do I/So am I · Neither do I/Neither am I`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

### `Me too`

- A: I’m exhausted after that flight. B: Me too.

Give a casual reply saying the negative statement is also true for you; me either also occurs in casual AmE.

- A: I don’t really enjoy networking events. B: Me neither.
- A: I’ve never been to Chicago. B: Me neither.

### `So do I/So am I`

## `X applies to Y · The same goes for Y · X holds true for Y`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Use these frames to extend a rule, instruction, or statement to another person or situation.

### `X applies to Y`

Say which cases a rule/instruction covers; compare someone applies X to Y for using X.

- This confidentiality rule applies to contractors. The same applies to temporary staff.

### `The same goes for Y`

Conversationally extend the previous point to another case; the same means the point just made.

- Keep vocabulary examples short and practical. The same goes for pattern examples.

### `X holds true for Y`

Say a statement remains true for another case.

- Regular feedback helps new employees improve. The same holds true for experienced staff.

## `X is eating me up (inside) · X is weighing on me · X is getting to me`

<!-- grouped-layout -->

Describe how a feeling, thought, responsibility, or situation affects you emotionally.

### `X is eating me up (inside)`

Describe consuming inner distress; eat away at someone is a close alternative for persistent worry. X is the feeling/situation causing the reaction; me is the affected person. Substitute you/him/her/us/them as needed.

- The guilt is eating me up inside.
- What I said yesterday is eating away at me.

### `X is weighing on me`

Describe an emotional burden, such as worry or responsibility.

- The uncertainty about my job is weighing on me.

### `X is getting to me`

Describe something beginning to upset/annoy you; drive someone crazy expresses strong frustration.

- The constant interruptions are getting to me.
- These repeated delays are driving me crazy.
