## `I think/believe/feel (that) + clause · I do not think/believe + clause`

State a personal judgment. Think and believe often overlap; believe can sound more considered. Feel can present an intuitive or personal judgment. None establishes an objective certainty level. I do not think/believe commonly introduces a negative judgment politely; it can also mean you withhold belief.

Examples:

- I think the simpler design is easier to maintain. / I believe the simpler design is easier to maintain.
- I feel that the team needs more uninterrupted time.
- I do not think the deadline is realistic. / I do not believe the estimate includes testing.

## `In my opinion/view, + clause · From my perspective/point of view, + clause · Personally, + clause · As far as I am concerned, + clause · If you ask me, + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->

Mark a view as your own. In my opinion and in my view are close alternatives; from my perspective/point of view foregrounds your standpoint. Personally marks your own preference or judgment. As far as I am concerned can sound firmer, particularly when setting a boundary. If you ask me is conversational and often introduces a frank or mildly critical view. These are not different levels of certainty. Use in my opinion, but from my point of view.

Examples:

- In my opinion, the shorter commute is worth the higher rent. / In my view, the shorter commute is worth the higher rent.
- From my perspective, the main problem is the lack of clear ownership. / From my point of view, the main problem is the lack of clear ownership.
- Personally, I would rather have flexible hours than a private office.
- As far as I am concerned, the matter is settled.
- If you ask me, that meeting could have been an email.

## `I would say + clause · I guess/suppose/imagine + clause`

Offer a tentative judgment. I would say softens an assessment; guess and suppose can express uncertainty or reluctant acceptance. Imagine often introduces a reasonable inference about a situation you have not experienced. Their meanings overlap and depend on tone.

Examples:

- I would say the trial was successful, although we still have some bugs to fix.
- I guess we could postpone it. / I suppose we could postpone it. — Either can signal hesitant acceptance.
- I imagine working across three time zones gets exhausting.

## `I bet/I'll bet + clause`

Express an informal confident expectation or inference, often as a friendly reaction. A real wager is unnecessary. This is conversational; use a more neutral judgment when presenting evidence in formal writing.

Examples:

- I bet the earlier flight will be cheaper. / I'll bet the earlier flight will be cheaper.
- You had three interviews today? I bet you're exhausted.

## `In my experience, + clause · As far as I know, + clause · From what I have heard, + clause · Apparently, + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->

Identify the basis or limits of a claim. Experience means firsthand experience; as far as I know allows for missing information. The related expression as far as I can tell limits a judgment to available observations; it overlaps with as far as I know when those observations are the basis of your knowledge. From what I have heard identifies a report. Apparently presents something as seeming true from reports or evidence, without guaranteeing it.

Examples:

- In my experience, written decisions reduce misunderstandings across time zones.
- As far as I know, the office is still open on Fridays.
- As far as I can tell, the rollout succeeded; the error rate is back to normal.
- From what I have heard, the new manager is very approachable.
- Apparently, the venue has changed; I just received a new invitation.

## `It seems/appears that + clause · It seems/looks/sounds/feels like + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->

Present an impression rather than a confirmed fact. Seems and appears overlap; appears often sounds more formal. Looks like and sounds like can describe an inference, not only literal sight or sound. Feels like highlights your subjective impression.

Examples:

- It seems that the update helped. / It appears that the update helped.
- It seems like we have enough time. / It looks like we have enough time.
- It sounds like the client wants a smaller first release.
- It feels like we are solving the same problem twice.

## `look/sound/feel/taste/smell + adjective · look/sound/feel/taste/smell like + noun`

<!-- overview-card -->

Describe a perceived quality with an adjective; use like before a thing you compare it with. A person can look tired without looking like a particular tired person. These linking uses describe the subject, so use good, not well, for a pleasant taste or smell.

Examples:

- The interface looks clean, the proposal sounds reasonable, and the controls feel responsive.
- The sauce tastes smoky, and the kitchen smells wonderful.
- It looks like a charger, sounds like a fan, and feels like rubber.
- The soup tastes like coconut, and the room smells like fresh paint.

## `What does X look like? · How does X look?`

What does it look like usually asks for a description or resemblance. How does it look asks about appearance or an evaluation. The answers can overlap; neither question is automatically wrong when you want to know about appearance.

Examples:

- What does the new office look like? — It has large windows and an open kitchen.
- How does the new office look? — Bright and welcoming.

## `I assume/presume + clause · Assuming (that) + clause, main clause`

Accept something as true without confirmation, or state a working premise. Assume and presume often overlap; presume usually sounds more formal. Assuming introduces the condition your conclusion relies on. These expressions do not guarantee that the premise is correct.

Examples:

- I assume the meeting is still at three. / I presume the meeting is still at three.
- For this estimate, let's assume everyone is available for two hours.
- Assuming the requirements stay the same, we can finish by Friday.

## `I suspect + clause · I have a feeling (that) + clause`

Present an unconfirmed belief or intuition. Suspect often suggests a possible explanation; it does not necessarily imply wrongdoing. Have a feeling more explicitly presents an intuition. Either can work when evidence is limited.

Examples:

- I suspect the cache is involved, but we need more evidence.
- I have a feeling that she would prefer a quieter restaurant.

## `I am sure/certain/convinced/confident (that) + clause`

State a strong belief or assurance. Sure and certain are close alternatives; convinced stresses that you hold a belief firmly. Confident often stresses assurance about an outcome or ability. Context allows substantial overlap; the wording does not prove the claim. Pretty sure softens sure: you feel fairly confident while leaving room for doubt.

Examples:

- I am sure this is the right address. / I am certain this is the right address. / I am pretty sure this is the right address.
- I am convinced that the old process needs to change.
- I am confident that we can finish the migration this month.

## `I realize/understand (that) + clause · I can see that + clause`

Acknowledge or recognize a situation. Realize often suggests becoming aware of it; understand can acknowledge the facts or someone’s reasoning. I can see that signals recognition and need not describe literal sight. These often accept a point before you respond; they do not automatically mean agreement.

Examples:

- I realize that the deadline is tight, but we still need to check the results.
- I understand that you need an answer today.
- I can see that the current process is causing frustration.

## `I am not sure whether/if + clause · I have no idea + wh-/whether-clause · I am not entirely convinced that + clause · I could be wrong, but + clause`

Express uncertainty or qualified doubt. Whether/if introduces an unresolved yes-or-no question; I have no idea is stronger and more casual: you have no information at all. Not entirely convinced expresses doubt about a claim. I could be wrong acknowledges that your own judgment may be mistaken.

Examples:

- I am not sure whether this plan includes dental coverage. / I am not sure if it includes dental coverage. / I have no idea why the build failed.
- I am not entirely convinced that adding more meetings would help.
- I could be wrong, but I think we tested a different version.

## `may/might/could + base verb · There is a chance/possibility that + clause · be likely/unlikely to + verb · be bound to + verb`

<!-- overview-card -->

Express possibility or likelihood without claiming certainty. May, might, and could overlap; context and tone matter more than a fixed probability scale. Likely suggests a probable outcome; unlikely suggests an improbable one. There is a chance/possibility introduces an outcome explicitly. Be bound to makes a confident prediction, often based on experience.

Examples:

- The release may slip. / The release might slip. / The release could slip.
- There is a chance that the venue will be available. / There is a possibility that the venue will be available.
- Traffic is likely to get worse after five; the roads are unlikely to clear before seven.
- It is likely that we will need another reviewer.
- Something is bound to go wrong on the first day, so plan some extra time.

## `There is evidence that + clause · The results/evidence suggest that + clause`

Distinguish evidence from the conclusion drawn from it. There is evidence that says support exists. Suggest presents an interpretation without claiming proof. Name the actual evidence when it matters.

Examples:

- There is evidence that the new schedule has reduced staff turnover.
- The results suggest that network latency is affecting response times.

## `According to + source, + clause`

Attribute information to a person, report, or other source. This does not necessarily mean you endorse the claim. To state your own view directly, use in my opinion rather than according to me.

Examples:

- According to the latest report, bus ridership rose last year.
- According to Maya, the revised estimate includes testing.

## `usually/often/rarely/hardly ever + main verb · be + frequency adverb · every now and then · How often ...?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Describe how frequently something happens. These adverbs normally go before a main verb, after be, or after the first auxiliary. Hardly ever means almost never; every now and then means occasionally.

Examples:

- I usually work from home, but I am rarely unavailable during office hours.
- I hardly ever check work email on vacation, but every now and then I check for an urgent update.
- How often do you travel for work? — About once a month.
- We often review difficult decisions together before responding to the client.

## `tend to + verb · In general/Generally/In most cases, + clause`

Describe a tendency while allowing exceptions. Tend to presents characteristic behavior; the sentence openings introduce a generalization. They do not specify an exact frequency or claim that every case is the same.

Examples:

- I tend to focus better when I have a clear deadline.
- In general, shorter meetings are easier to schedule. / Generally, shorter meetings are easier to schedule.
- In most cases, a written update is enough.

## `The main reason + clause + is that + clause · One reason for + noun/V-ing + is that + clause`

Give an explanation. The main reason identifies the most important reason; one reason introduces a contribution without claiming it is the only one. Use a clause after the reason I/we/etc.; use for before a noun or an -ing action.

Examples:

- The main reason I chose this job is that I can work with an international team.
- One reason for the delay is that the requirements changed.
- One reason for recording meetings is that colleagues can catch up later.

## `because/since/as + clause · because of/due to + noun/V-ing`

Give a cause. Because makes the reason explicit; since and as often present background reasons, but they can also refer to time. Use because for clarity if that ambiguity matters. Because of and due to take a noun phrase or an -ing phrase, not an ordinary subject–verb clause.

Examples:

- We postponed the launch because the tests failed. / We postponed the launch because of the failed tests.
- Since everyone is here, we can start. / As everyone is here, we can start.
- The delay was due to a supplier shortage. / We made fewer errors because of double-checking the totals.

## `clause, so + result · As a result/Therefore/Consequently, + clause · That is why + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->

Present a result or conclusion. So joins clauses and is common in conversation. As a result highlights a consequence; therefore often highlights a logical conclusion; consequently is more formal. That is why explains an outcome. These can overlap. Start a new sentence or use a semicolon before therefore/consequently when linking independent statements.

Examples:

- The trains were canceled, so we drove. / The trains were canceled. As a result, we drove.
- Every request needs a token. This request has none; therefore, it will be rejected.
- The supplier closed its factory. Consequently, production stopped.
- The buses stop running at ten. That is why I booked a taxi.

## `Given + noun/that-clause, + clause`

Treat an existing fact as the basis for a judgment. Given takes a noun phrase; given that takes a full clause. This introduces your reasoning, not necessarily a direct physical cause.

Examples:

- Given our budget, we should start small.
- Given that we have only two weeks, we should reduce the scope.

## `One possible explanation is that + clause · It might have something to do with + noun · X has nothing to do with Y`

Suggest an explanation without presenting it as established. A possible explanation proposes how or why something happened. Might have something to do with suggests a connection that still needs investigation. Has nothing to do with firmly denies a connection.

Examples:

- One possible explanation is that users cannot find the search button.
- It might have something to do with the cached credentials.
- The crash has nothing to do with your change; it started before you deployed.

## `The cause of X is Y · What is causing + noun?`

Identify or ask about the cause of a problem or event. Naming the cause is stronger than proposing a possible explanation. A symptom is an observable effect, not automatically the cause.

Examples:

- The cause of the timeout was a blocked connection; the slow interface was a symptom.
- What is causing the sudden increase in response time?

## `(in order) to + verb · so that + subject + can/could + verb · The goal is to + verb · be designed to + verb`

<!-- overview-card -->

Explain purpose. A purpose infinitive normally shares its understood actor with the main action. So that lets you name a different actor. In order to makes purpose explicit; plain to is usually enough. A goal or design expresses an intention, not guaranteed success.

Examples:

- I muted notifications to concentrate. / I muted notifications in order to concentrate.
- I shared the recording so that colleagues in other time zones could watch it.
- The goal of the trial is to identify confusing steps.
- The interface is designed to make common tasks easier.

## `clause, but + clause · Although/Though/Even though + clause, main clause · Despite/In spite of + noun/V-ing, main clause · statement. However, statement. · clause, though`

<!-- overview-card -->

Express a contrast despite an expectation. These forms can convey the same message after restructuring. Although and though overlap; even though emphasizes the unexpected result. Despite and in spite of are near-equivalents. However often sounds more formal and needs a sentence boundary or semicolon between independent statements. But also has broader contrast uses. Sentence-final though is conversational; although/even though cannot take that position.

Examples:

- I was tired, but I finished the review.
- Although I was tired, I finished the review. / Though I was tired, I finished the review. / Even though I was tired, I finished the review.
- Despite being tired, I finished the review. / In spite of being tired, I finished the review.
- I was tired. However, I finished the review.
- I was tired. I finished the review, though.

## `That said/Still/Even so, + clause · While it is true that + clause, main clause · X may be true, but Y`

<!-- overview-card -->

Acknowledge a point before limiting its implications. That said signals a qualification; still and even so stress what remains true despite the previous point. While it is true and may ... but explicitly concede a point. These choices often overlap, rather than expressing mutually exclusive meanings.

Examples:

- The tool saves time. That said, we still need to check its output.
- The tool sometimes misses errors. Still, it saves time. / The tool sometimes misses errors. Even so, it saves time.
- While it is true that the tool saves time, it still needs supervision.
- The tool may be useful, but it cannot replace a careful review.

## `clause, whereas/while + clause · On the one hand, + clause; on the other hand, + clause`

Whereas and contrastive while compare different facts. On the one hand/on the other hand balances considerations, often about the same choice. You can omit on the one hand when the first consideration is already clear. While can also express time, so choose whereas if the context is ambiguous.

Examples:

- I prefer written feedback, whereas my manager prefers a call. / I prefer written feedback, while my manager prefers a call.
- On the one hand, the job pays well; on the other hand, the hours are unpredictable.
- The apartment is close to work. On the other hand, it has very little natural light.

## `I agree/disagree with + noun · I agree to some extent, but + clause · I see your point, but + clause · That is a valid concern · To be fair, + clause · I have a different take on + noun`

<!-- overview-card -->

Acknowledge another viewpoint before responding. Partial agreement accepts some of the claim; seeing someone’s point or recognizing a concern does not require agreement. To be fair introduces a consideration you think deserves recognition.

Examples:

- I agree to some extent, but a higher salary is not the only priority.
- I see your point, but I would still prefer to test it first.
- That is a valid concern. We should check who can access the recordings.
- To be fair, the team had only two days to prepare.
- I have a different take on the survey results: price may matter more than speed.
- I agree with your assessment. / I disagree with that conclusion.

## `X does not necessarily mean Y · Just because + clause + does not mean + clause · X does not guarantee Y`

Reject an overstrong inference. Not necessarily means the conclusion is not automatic, rather than saying it is always false. Does not guarantee denies certainty, even when the first fact makes the outcome more likely.

Examples:

- A higher salary does not necessarily mean a better quality of life.
- Just because the code compiles does not mean it works correctly.
- Passing the benchmark does not guarantee good performance in production.

## `It is not that X; it is (just) that Y · It’s not like + clause · It’s not like + person + to + verb · I am not saying X; I am saying Y · The problem is not X; it is Y`

<!-- overview-card -->

Correct a possible interpretation by stating the intended distinction. It is not that rejects an assumed reason; adding just, or using It’s just that alone, gives the real reason more softly, especially when you decline something. It’s not like + clause looks similar but dismisses a worry or assumption: it means it is not as if. It’s not like + person + to + verb says that a behavior is unusual for that person. I am not saying clarifies your claim. Keep the two alternatives parallel enough to compare.

Examples:

- It is not that I dislike the idea; it is that we cannot afford it yet.
- I’d love to come. It’s just that I have a deadline tomorrow.
- Just ask him. It’s not like he’s going to say no.
- It’s not like her to miss a deadline. I hope everything is okay.
- I am not saying we should cancel the release; I am saying we should reduce its scope.
- The problem is not a lack of effort; it is a lack of clear priorities.

## `If anything, + clause`

Use this to suggest that the opposite interpretation, or a stronger version of your point, is more accurate.

- The change hasn’t simplified the process. If anything, it has made it more confusing.
- I don’t think remote work has reduced my productivity. If anything, I get more done.
- The trip didn’t make me less interested in living abroad. If anything, it strengthened that interest.

## `In terms of + noun / V-ing, + clause`

Use this to limit an evaluation to one particular aspect, measure, or activity.

- In terms of cost, the second proposal is more practical.
- In terms of reducing emissions, improving public transportation may have the greatest impact.
- In terms of long-term health, regular exercise matters more than short-term weight loss.

## `What I need/value/like most is + noun/clause · What matters most is + noun/clause · What I struggle with most is + noun/V-ing`

<!-- overview-card -->

Put the focus at the end of the sentence. The opening what-clause acts as the subject; is introduces the important information. Choose the opening verb for the relationship you mean, rather than treating every frame as an interchangeable introduction.

Examples:

- What I need most is uninterrupted time to finish the analysis.
- What I value most is that the team shares feedback openly. / What I like most is the freedom to choose how I work.
- What matters most is whether we can maintain the system.
- What I struggle with most is explaining complicated ideas briefly.

## `The thing is, + clause`

Use this in conversation to introduce the key fact or complication that the listener needs to understand.

- I would like to live downtown. The thing is, I cannot afford the rent there.
- The job sounds interesting. The thing is, the commute would take almost two hours.
- We could postpone the event. The thing is, several speakers have already booked their flights.

## `What I mean is + clause · What I am trying to say is + clause · That is not what I meant · In other words/To put it simply, + clause · Sorry, I meant to say + correction`

<!-- overview-card -->

Clarify or reformulate a message. What I mean introduces your intended meaning; what I am trying to say can help repair a difficult explanation. That is not what I meant rejects a misunderstanding. In other words restates; to put it simply promises a simpler version.

Examples:

- What I mean is that we need a smaller first release.
- What I am trying to say is that the deadline depends on the requirements.
- That is not what I meant. I was asking whether the estimate includes testing.
- The service has no spare capacity. In other words, it cannot handle more traffic.
- To put it simply, the faster option uses more memory.
- Sorry, I meant to say Thursday, not Tuesday.

## `For example/For instance, + clause · such as/like + noun/V-ing`

Illustrate a general point with a specific case. For example and for instance can introduce a sentence; such as introduces examples within a phrase. Like also introduces examples in ordinary conversation. They do not themselves express a cause or correction.

Examples:

- Small changes can help. For example, we could shorten the form. / Small changes can help. For instance, we could remove optional fields.
- I enjoy activities such as hiking and taking photographs. / I enjoy activities like hiking and taking photographs.

## `In fact/Actually, + clause`

Add a fact that strengthens or corrects what was just said or assumed. In fact often reinforces a claim; actually often corrects an expectation. Both can correct a claim, and neither simply means for example. Actually can sound confrontational when correcting someone, depending on tone.

Examples:

- The trip was not expensive. In fact, it cost less than staying home that week.
- I thought the office was closed. Actually, it is open until six.
- I expected the task to be easy. In fact, it took all afternoon. / I expected the task to be easy. Actually, it took all afternoon.

## `Also, + clause · In addition/Furthermore, + clause · Another thing is that + clause · not only X but also Y`

<!-- overview-card -->

Add a related point. Also and another thing are comfortable in conversation; in addition is neutral-to-formal, and furthermore is more formal. Not only ... but also emphasizes the combination. Keep X and Y grammatically parallel.

Examples:

- The apartment is quiet. Also, it is close to the station.
- The policy reduces paperwork. In addition, it makes decisions easier to track. / The policy reduces paperwork. Furthermore, it improves transparency.
- Another thing is that we need a backup plan.
- The change not only reduced latency but also cut our hosting costs.

## `First/First of all, + clause · Then/Next/After that, + clause · Finally, + clause`

Put steps or points in a clear order. First of all can introduce your first point; finally introduces the last one. These expressions organize a sequence and do not automatically summarize an argument.

Examples:

- First, reproduce the issue. Then, check the logs. Finally, test the proposed fix.
- First of all, confirm the address. Next, choose a delivery time. After that, check the total.

## `The main point is that + clause · The bottom line is that + clause · At the end of the day, + clause`

Highlight the central message or practical conclusion. Main point identifies what you most want someone to understand. Bottom line often stresses what ultimately matters for a decision; it is common in conversation and workplace discussion. At the end of the day introduces what matters most after everything else is considered; it is very common in speech but sounds like a cliché if overused.

Examples:

- The main point is that users need a clear way to recover their accounts.
- The bottom line is that we cannot support two versions indefinitely.
- At the end of the day, the client cares more about reliability than new features.

## `To sum up/In summary/In conclusion/Overall, + clause · Long story short, + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->

Summarize or give an overall assessment. To sum up and in summary introduce a summary; in conclusion more explicitly closes an argument and suits formal writing or presentations. Overall evaluates the whole picture and can also introduce a data overview. Long story short is casual: it skips the details of a story and gives the outcome.

Examples:

- To sum up, the proposal is feasible but needs more testing. / In summary, the proposal is feasible but needs more testing.
- In conclusion, the evidence supports a limited trial.
- Overall, response times improved even though costs increased slightly.
- Long story short, we missed the connection and spent an extra night in Denver.

## `base verb + object/complement · Do not/Don’t + base verb · Make sure (that) + clause`

Give instructions with an understood you as the subject. Please can soften a request; make sure asks someone to ensure a condition holds. Use an ordinary tense inside the that-clause.

Examples:

- Save a local copy before you close the page.
- Do not share the link outside the team. / Don’t share the link outside the team.
- Make sure the attachment opens before you send the message.

## `Speaking of + noun, + clause · Going back to + topic, + clause · Anyway, as I was saying, + clause · Now that you mention it, + clause · One thing I forgot to mention is + noun/clause`

<!-- overview-card -->

Move between topics clearly. Speaking of introduces a related topic; going back returns to an earlier one. As I was saying resumes an interrupted point. Now that you mention it introduces something prompted by the other person’s remark.

Examples:

- Speaking of travel, have you booked your flight?
- Going back to your question about cost, we have two options.
- Anyway, as I was saying, we need to confirm the venue.
- Now that you mention it, I noticed the same error yesterday.
- One thing I forgot to mention is that the hotel offers a free shuttle.

## `Can I jump in for a second? · Let me finish + noun`

Ask to take a turn or keep your turn long enough to finish. These are direct conversational tools; a calm tone helps. Let me finish can sound firm when someone repeatedly interrupts.

Examples:

- Can I jump in for a second? I have a question about the timeline.
- Let me finish this point, and then I would like to hear your view.

## `Let me think for a second · I have never really thought about + noun/wh-clause · I am not sure how to answer that · It’s hard to say + wh-/whether-clause`

Acknowledge that you need thinking time or do not yet have an answer. These phrases let you respond honestly instead of inventing an opinion. It’s hard to say signals that the answer depends on factors nobody knows yet. Follow with a relevant thought when you have one.

Examples:

- Let me think for a second. There may be another way to approach this.
- I have never really thought about how cities should fund public art.
- I am not sure how to answer that without seeing the results. / It’s hard to say whether prices will drop next year.

## `To be honest, + clause · I have to say/admit, + clause`

Use these only when you are genuinely being candid or admitting something; do not use them as empty fillers. I have to say stresses a frank judgment, often a positive or surprising one. I have to admit concedes something you did not expect or would rather not say.

Examples:

- To be honest, I would not choose to live in such a crowded area. / I have to say, the neighborhood is livelier than I expected.
- To be honest, I had expected the course to be easier. / I have to admit, the course was harder than I expected.
- To be honest, convenience matters more to me than having a large home.

## `simple present · am/is/are + V-ing · Do/Does + subject + base verb? · Am/Is/Are + subject + V-ing?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use the simple present for habits, general facts, and stable situations; use the present continuous for an activity in progress or a temporary developing situation. The simple present takes -s with he/she/it, but after does use the base verb. State verbs such as know, own, and need normally use the simple form.

Examples:

- I work with overseas clients. / I am working with a new client this week.
- The server handles requests automatically. / The server is handling a large request right now.
- Does she work remotely? / Is she working remotely today?
- I need more time; I do not know the answer yet.
- Is your English improving? — Ask about progress now.

## `I think + clause · I am thinking about/of + noun/V-ing`

Distinguish holding an opinion from actively considering an idea. Opinion think normally uses a simple form; thinking about/of describes the mental activity. The same verb can take different forms because its meaning changes.

Examples:

- I think the plan is realistic. / I am thinking about changing the plan.
- What do you think of the offer? / Are you thinking of accepting it?

## `be + adjective · be being + behavior adjective`

Use be for a state or characteristic; be being highlights current behavior. It works with behavior descriptions such as helpful, rude, careful, or unreasonable. Do not use being merely to make a nonbehavioral state temporary.

Examples:

- She is usually patient, but she is being unusually impatient today.
- I am being careful because the floor is slippery. / I am tired after the trip.

## `be always/constantly + V-ing`

Comment on repeated behavior, often with irritation, surprise, or appreciation. This is not simply the neutral statement that something happens every time. A simple form is better for an unemotional routine.

Examples:

- The printer is always breaking down right before a deadline.
- She is constantly finding ways to make new colleagues feel welcome.

## `simple past · have/has + past participle · Have you ever ...? · have/has not ... yet`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use the simple past for a finished past time, stated or understood. Use the present perfect to look back from now: experience up to now, a current result, or an unfinished period. With a stated finished time, use the simple past. In casual AmE, the simple past often overlaps with the present perfect for recent events with just, already, and yet.

Examples:

- I submitted the report yesterday. / I have submitted the report, so we can discuss it now.
- I worked there from 2021 to 2023. / I have worked here since 2023.
- Have you ever spoken at a conference? / I have never spoken at one before.
- I have not seen her in ages, but we still exchange messages.
- Have you finished yet? / Did you finish yet? — Both are common in AmE.
- I have already checked the address. / I already checked the address.
- I have just started learning to drive. / I just started learning to drive.
- Has your English improved? — Ask about the result by now.

## `have/has + noun · have/has got + noun`

Describe possession, relationships, or current conditions. Have got is conversational; plain have and do you have are versatile everyday AmE choices. For activities, use ordinary have, which can be continuous. For past possession, use had, not have got.

Examples:

- I have a spare charger. / I have got a spare charger.
- Do you have any allergies? — No, I do not. / I had a food allergy as a child.
- We are having lunch now. — An activity, not possession.

## `have/has + past participle · have/has been + V-ing · for + duration · since + starting point`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use the simple perfect for a result, count, or established state; use the continuous perfect to emphasize activity or duration up to now. The activity may still be happening or may have just stopped. Live and work often allow both. Use for for a length of time and since for its starting point. Know and own normally take the simple form.

Examples:

- I have reviewed three proposals. / I have been reviewing proposals all morning.
- I have lived here for two years. / I have been living here for two years.
- How long have you been working on this? / I have been working on it since Monday.
- I have known her for a decade.

## `The first/last time + past clause, + clause · This is the first time + present perfect · When was the last time + past clause?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Distinguish narrating an occasion from counting experience up to now. The first time I did something introduces a past occasion. This is the first time I have done something relates the current experience to earlier ones. After was, had done is available when looking back from that past point.

Examples:

- The first time I led a meeting in English, I prepared far too many notes.
- This is the first time I have handled the entire release myself.
- When was the last time you took a full week off?
- The last time I visited, the museum was closed for repairs.

## `It has been + duration + since + past clause · How long has it been since + past clause?`

Measure the time from an earlier event to now. The since-clause identifies the event, while the main clause gives the elapsed duration. Conversational it’s been is natural.

Examples:

- It has been six months since I last took a vacation.
- How long has it been since you visited your family?

## `used to + base verb · would + base verb for repeated past actions · be/get used to + noun/V-ing`

Used to describes a past habit or state that no longer holds. Would can describe repeated past actions after the past setting is clear; it does not usually replace used to for a past state. Be used to means familiar with; get used to describes becoming familiar. The to in these last two forms takes a noun or -ing action.

Examples:

- I used to live downtown, and I used to walk to work.
- When I lived downtown, I would stop at the same café every morning.
- I am used to presenting online, but I am still getting used to speaking to a large audience.

## `was/were + V-ing + when + past event · While + past continuous, + past continuous`

Set an activity in progress as background to an event, or describe two simultaneous activities. The past continuous does not itself mean an action was interrupted; the surrounding event tells you that.

Examples:

- I was reviewing the proposal when the client called.
- While I was cooking dinner, my roommate was setting the table.
- We were discussing the budget when the meeting ended.

## `simple past · had + past participle · had been + V-ing`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use the simple past for the main events in a past account. Use the past perfect when you need to look back from one past moment to an earlier event. Do not use the past perfect merely because an event happened a long time ago.

Use had been + V-ing to look back at an activity continuing up to a past moment. Had just/barely + past participle + when places an action shortly before another event; barely emphasizes how little time passed. In I’d just sat down, I’d means I had; in I’d ask, it means I would.

Examples:

- I had just sent the proposal when the client called. / I sent the proposal and then called the client.
- I’d barely fallen asleep when the alarm went off.
- She ate before the meeting. / She was not hungry because she had eaten before the meeting.
- After I finished the report, I sent it. / By the time I sent the report, I had checked every figure.
- I had been waiting for an hour when the clinic finally called my name.

## `be about to + verb + when + event · was/were going to + verb, but + clause · meant to + verb, but + clause`

About to means an action is imminent; it does not guarantee that another event prevents it. Was going to states an earlier plan or expectation. Meant to states an intention. Adding but makes the change explicit, rather than relying on the tense alone.

Examples:

- I was about to leave when the courier arrived, so I waited.
- We were going to meet in person, but her flight was canceled.
- I meant to reply yesterday, but I could not find the attachment.

## `will not/would not + base verb`

Describe refusal or failure to cooperate. Won’t can concern a person or a machine now; wouldn’t can report refusal or a failure in the past. Context distinguishes this from a prediction or imagined situation.

Examples:

- I have asked twice, but she will not discuss the deadline.
- The car would not start, so we took the bus.

## `As soon as/Once/The moment + clause, + main clause · right after + noun/clause`

<!-- overview-card -->

Link events in sequence. As soon as and the moment emphasize immediacy; once emphasizes reaching a stage or condition and does not promise immediate action. Right after emphasizes a short gap. In future time clauses, use a present form rather than will for the expected event.

Examples:

- As soon as I get the results, I will call you.
- Once we have approval, we can choose a launch date.
- The moment I saw the warning, I stopped the test.
- I called her right after the meeting. / I called her right after the meeting ended.

## `by + deadline · until + endpoint · not ... until + time/clause · It was not until X that Y`

By sets a latest completion time. Until describes continuation up to an endpoint; not ... until delays the start or occurrence. It was not until ... that ... emphasizes the delayed timing. With future time clauses, use a present form.

Examples:

- I will finish the review by Friday. / I will keep reviewing applications until Friday.
- I will not send the report until you approve it.
- It was not until I checked the logs that I understood the failure.

## `By the time + clause, + perfect form`

Use a past perfect form for a completed state before a past event and a future perfect form for completion by a future event.

- By the time the meeting started, the team had resolved the main issue.
- By the time I reached the station, the last train had already left.
- By the time the new line opens, the city will have completed the surrounding roadwork.

## `will + base verb · be going to + base verb · present continuous + future time · will be + V-ing · simple present for a timetable`

<!-- overview-card -->

Will can express predictions, promises, or decisions made now. Going to often expresses an existing intention or a prediction based on present evidence. The present continuous presents an arrangement. Will be doing describes activity in progress at a future time. These forms overlap; they are not rigid one-to-one categories.

Examples:

- I will send you the notes tonight; I promise.
- I am going to take a week off after the release.
- I am meeting the client at ten tomorrow.
- At ten tomorrow, I will be meeting with the client, so I may not answer.
- The train leaves at seven tomorrow morning. — A scheduled service.

## `Whenever/Every time + clause, + clause · when/if + clause, + clause`

Whenever and every time describe repeated occasions. When presents an event as expected or established; if leaves it conditional. In general rules, when and if can overlap. Use a present form in a future time or condition clause.

Examples:

- Whenever I travel, I save a copy of my reservation offline. / Every time I travel, I do the same thing.
- When I get home tonight, I will send the photos.
- If I get home early enough, I will join the call.

## `At first, + clause; eventually/in the end, + clause · Over time, + clause · Over the past + period, + present perfect`

<!-- overview-card -->

Contrast an initial situation with a later outcome. Eventually emphasizes reaching a point after time or effort; in the end emphasizes the final outcome. Over time describes gradual development. Over the past ... connects a period to now and commonly takes the present perfect.

Examples:

- At first, I found the schedule exhausting; eventually, I adjusted.
- We considered several options. In the end, we stayed with the original design.
- Over time, I became more comfortable asking questions.
- Over the past year, our response times have improved considerably.

## `still + verb · not ... anymore/any longer · no longer + verb · already/yet`

<!-- overview-card -->

Describe whether a situation continues or an expected change has happened. Still marks continuation; not ... anymore/any longer and no longer mark an ended situation. Already marks completion by now or earlier than expected; yet usually appears in questions and negatives about an expected change. AmE commonly writes adverbial anymore as one word.

Examples:

- I still work remotely. / I do not work remotely anymore. / I no longer work remotely.
- We do not use that system any longer.
- Have you sent the draft yet? — I already sent it.
- The printer is still broken. / It has not been fixed yet.

## `at the beginning/end of + noun · in the beginning/end`

At the beginning/end of identifies a point in a period, event, or object. In the beginning describes an initial situation; in the end gives the eventual outcome. Do not add of after in the end for a date or event endpoint.

Examples:

- We will review the results at the end of the month.
- In the beginning, we met every day. In the end, we settled on weekly updates.

## `What happened was (that) + clause`

Use this to introduce the event that explains a situation, especially in a spoken story.

- What happened was that my flight was canceled at the last minute.
- What happened was, we took the wrong exit and ended up in another town.
- What happened was that the landlord sold the building, so everyone had to move.

## `You will not believe what happened · So there’s this + noun + clause · All of a sudden/Out of nowhere, + clause · The next thing I knew, + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->

Introduce a surprising story or an abrupt event. So there’s this + noun introduces a new person or thing in a casual story, even though the listener does not know it yet. All of a sudden emphasizes suddenness; out of nowhere emphasizes an unexpected appearance or event. The next thing I knew skips to a rapid or surprising development from the speaker’s perspective. These are conversational storytelling choices.

Examples:

- You will not believe what happened on the way to the airport.
- So there’s this guy on my team who answers every email in thirty seconds.
- All of a sudden, the lights went out.
- Out of nowhere, a dog ran across the road.
- The next thing I knew, everyone was helping us push the car.

## `Luckily/Fortunately/Unfortunately, + clause · To make matters worse, + clause`

Evaluate whether events were fortunate or unfortunate. Luckily and fortunately overlap; unfortunately marks bad news or a disappointing outcome. To make matters worse adds a further problem to an already bad situation.

Examples:

- Luckily, someone had turned my wallet in. / Fortunately, someone had turned my wallet in.
- Unfortunately, our train was canceled.
- The flight was canceled. To make matters worse, the hotel was full.

## `Surprisingly, + clause · I could not believe + clause`

Express surprise about an event. Surprisingly marks something as unexpected; could not believe often expresses a stronger personal reaction, without literally denying what happened.

Examples:

- Surprisingly, the repair took only ten minutes.
- I could not believe how quickly everyone offered to help.

## `almost/nearly + verb · almost/nearly all/every + noun`

Say an action came close to happening or a quantity was close to complete. Almost missing a train means you caught it; almost finishing means some work remained. Almost and nearly overlap in these patterns.

Examples:

- I almost missed the last train, but I got on just before the doors closed. / I nearly missed the last train, but I made it.
- Almost all the reviewers agreed. / Nearly every reviewer agreed.

## `If + present, + present/will/can/may · as long as/provided that + clause · only if + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use an open condition for general rules or real possibilities. As long as and provided that state a requirement; provided that is more formal. Only if marks a necessary condition, not a guarantee of the result. Present forms normally express future conditions.

Examples:

- If I skip lunch, I lose concentration. / If I finish early, I will join you.
- You can work remotely as long as you are available for the meeting.
- We can proceed provided that the client approves the budget.
- I will accept the offer only if the hours are flexible.

## `unless + clause · if not, + clause · otherwise, + clause · in that case/if that’s the case, + clause · That’s not the case`

<!-- overview-card -->

Unless usually means if ... not, but do not mechanically substitute it into every negative condition. Otherwise gives the consequence if the preceding requirement is not met. If not negates a clear prior condition. In that case and if that’s the case respond to the situation just described. That’s not the case politely says that a described situation is not true.

Examples:

- Unless the weather improves, we will move the event indoors.
- Can you send it today? If not, tomorrow morning is fine.
- Please save a copy; otherwise, you could lose your changes.
- The venue is unavailable? In that case, we should meet online. / If that’s the case, we should meet online.
- A: I heard the office is closing. B: That’s not the case; it’s just moving to the fourth floor.

## `Even though + fact, + clause · Even if + possible case, + clause · whether or not/no matter what/how + clause · It doesn’t matter + wh-/if-clause`

Even though presents a fact. Even if says the outcome holds under a possible condition. Whether or not covers both alternatives; no matter what/how makes the outcome independent of the named variation. It doesn’t matter + wh-/if-clause expresses the same independence as a complete sentence. Do not erase the fact-versus-possibility difference simply because all these forms can express determination.

Examples:

- Even though the task is difficult, I will try it. / Even if the task is difficult, I will try it.
- Whether or not they approve the proposal, we need to document the risks. / It doesn’t matter whether they approve it; we still need to document the risks.
- No matter what happens, I will keep you informed. / No matter how busy I am, I make time for a short walk. / It doesn’t matter which framework you use, as long as the tests pass.

## `in case + clause · just in case · in case of + noun`

Describe a precaution with in case: prepare before you know whether the event will happen. If instead makes the action conditional. In case of in instructions means if the named event occurs. Use a present form for a future event after in case.

Examples:

- I saved a local copy in case the connection fails. / If the connection fails, I will switch to the local copy.
- I do not expect rain, but I brought a jacket just in case.
- In case of a power outage, use the stairs.

## `What if + clause? · Let’s say/Suppose + clause`

Invite someone to consider a hypothetical scenario. What if can raise a worry or propose an option. Let’s say and suppose establish an imagined case for discussion; they do not claim it is true.

Examples:

- What if the client changes the requirements? / What if we start with a smaller trial?
- Let’s say we have only two weeks. / Suppose we have only two weeks. What would you cut?

## `depend on + noun/whether/how-clause · Depending on + noun/wh-clause, + main clause · Whether + clause + depends on + noun/wh-clause`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use depend on to name what determines an answer or outcome. Whether introduces an unresolved yes-or-no condition; how introduces a manner or degree. Depending on introduces a variable that changes the situation, rather than saying someone is personally dependent.

- The timeline depends on whether the client approves the design.
- It depends on how much testing we need.
- Depending on the weather, we may move the dinner inside.
- Whether we travel this weekend depends on how much work remains.

## `If + past form, + would/could + base verb · If + subject + were to + verb, + would + base verb · Without + noun, + would/could + base verb`

<!-- overview-card -->

Imagine a present or future situation as hypothetical or remote. The past form marks distance from reality, not necessarily past time. Were is standard for hypothetical be; was also occurs in conversation. Were to makes a future possibility sound even more hypothetical, often for a sensitive or unlikely case. Without can express the absent condition. Use could for an imagined possibility or ability.

Examples:

- If I had more time, I would take a longer trip.
- What would you do if you were in my position?
- If the company were to cut the budget, which project would you keep?
- Without clear requirements, we could spend weeks solving the wrong problem.
- If I were you, I would ask for the details in writing.

## `If + had + past participle, + would/could have + past participle · If + had + past participle, + would + base verb + now`

Imagine a different past and its consequence. Would have gives an imagined past result; could have gives an imagined possibility. Use would + base verb when the consequence belongs to now. Without + noun can replace a clear condition, but keep the consequence’s time intact.

Examples:

- If we had left earlier, we would have caught the train.
- Without your help, I could not have finished the application.
- If I had accepted that offer, I would live in Chicago now.

## `I hope + clause · I wish + past form · I wish + had + past participle · I wish + would + verb`

<!-- overview-card -->

Hope leaves room for a desired outcome to be true. Wish + past describes an unreal or unsatisfactory present; wish + past perfect regrets a past situation. Wish + would often expresses a desired change, sometimes with frustration, rather than a neutral prediction.

Examples:

- I hope the interview goes well. / I hope you enjoyed the trip.
- I wish I had more time to travel.
- I wish I had asked about the schedule before accepting.
- I wish they would tell us about changes earlier.
- I wish I could join you, but I have to work.

## `could have + past participle · should/should not have + past participle`

Could have can describe an unrealized possibility or a possible explanation; context determines which. Should have often evaluates a past choice or expresses regret. It can also express an expected completed event, so do not assume every should have sentence criticizes someone.

Examples:

- I could have taken a taxi, but I decided to walk. — An unused option.
- The message could have gone to spam. — A possible explanation.
- I should have checked the address. / I should not have rushed the review.

## `must/might/cannot + base verb · must/might/could not have + past participle · should + base verb`

<!-- overview-card -->

Distinguish inference, possibility, and expectation. Must can express a strong inference rather than an obligation. Cannot/could not can reject a possibility. Have + past participle places the inference in the past. Should can predict what is expected, without making a promise.

Examples:

- She must be busy; she has not answered. / She might be busy, but I am not sure.
- That cannot be the final version; it still has comments in it.
- They must have missed the email. / They might have missed the email.
- He could not have sent it yesterday; his account was still locked.
- The package should arrive soon. / The package should have arrived by now.

## `plan/decide/hope/agree/promise + to + verb · decide against + noun/V-ing · think about + V-ing`

<!-- overview-card -->

These verbs can take to + base verb for an intended action; each verb still keeps its own meaning. Decide against rejects an option and takes a noun or -ing form. Think about doing something describes consideration, not a commitment. Do not assume every verb accepts both complement shapes.

Examples:

- We plan to expand the trial, but we have not agreed to a date.
- I decided to apply. / I decided against applying.
- I hope to finish this week, and I promised to send an update tomorrow.
- I have been thinking about taking a course before I decide.

## `have to/have got to/need to/must + verb · do not have to/do not need to + verb · did not have to + verb · be going to have to + verb · cannot/must not + verb · be supposed to + verb · be supposed to be + adjective/noun`

<!-- overview-card -->

Have to, have got to, need to, and must can express necessity. Have got to is conversational; have to is useful across tenses. Do not have to/need to removes necessity; did not have to says something was unnecessary in the past, and You didn’t have to! thanks someone for an unnecessary kindness. Be going to have to announces an unwelcome necessity politely. Cannot/must not can prohibit an action. Supposed to expresses an expectation, arrangement, or rule. Was supposed to does not itself prove that the action failed to happen. Be supposed to be + adjective/noun reports what people say about something.

Examples:

- I have to submit the form today. / I need to submit it today. / I must submit it today.
- You do not have to attend. / You do not need to attend. — Attendance is optional.
- I didn’t have to pay for parking because the meter was broken. / You didn’t have to bring anything, but thank you!
- I’m going to have to leave a little early today.
- You cannot share that password. / You must not share it. — It is prohibited.
- We are supposed to check in by six.
- I was supposed to lead the meeting, and I did.
- That new taco place is supposed to be really good.
- I have got to leave soon; the last train is at ten.

## `should + verb · might want to + verb · had better + verb · would be better off + V-ing · Have you tried + V-ing?`

Give advice. Should is a direct recommendation; might want to softens it. Had better suggests an important warning or consequence, so it can sound pushy in a low-stakes suggestion. Would be better off + V-ing says an option would leave someone in a better position. Have you tried + V-ing? suggests a solution as a question. Should and had better take a base verb without to.

Examples:

- You should ask which expenses are covered. / You’d be better off asking HR directly.
- You might want to save a copy before changing the settings. / Have you tried restarting the router?
- We had better leave now, or we will miss the last train.

## `might as well/may as well + base verb`

Choose a sensible available option when there is no compelling reason to do something else. Might as well and may as well overlap; might as well is a natural conversational choice. This is not simply a prediction that you might act.

Examples:

- We are already downtown, so we might as well visit the museum.
- The download will take an hour. We may as well get lunch while we wait.

## `It is time (for + person) to + verb · It is (about) time + subject + past form`

Say an action should happen now. The to-form is a neutral signal; the past-form construction can imply that the action is overdue. About time often strengthens that implication. The past form here does not locate the action in the past.

Examples:

- It is time for us to leave. / It is time we left.
- It is about time they fixed the broken elevator.

## `prefer + noun/V-ing + to + noun/V-ing · would prefer to + verb · would rather + base verb + than + base verb · instead of + noun/V-ing · rather than + parallel alternative`

<!-- overview-card -->

Prefer can express a general preference; would prefer/would rather often expresses a current choice. After would rather, use a base verb. Instead of takes a noun or -ing action. Rather than contrasts alternatives and works best when their shapes are parallel. These forms can express the same preference after restructuring.

Examples:

- I prefer working from home to commuting every day.
- I would prefer to meet online. / I would rather meet online than drive across town.
- We met online instead of driving across town.
- We chose to repair the system rather than replace it.

## `like/love/hate + V-ing / to + verb · would like/love + to + verb`

Both -ing and to can express general likes and dislikes, especially in AmE. An -ing form often focuses on the activity as an experience; like to can foreground a chosen routine. Would like/love usually introduces a particular wish and takes to. Do not invent a rigid enjoyment-versus-duty rule.

Examples:

- I love working with a small team. / I love to work with a small team.
- I like to check the agenda before a meeting. — A preferred routine.
- I would like to discuss the offer before I decide. / I would love to join you for dinner.

## `Either one works for me · What are our options for + noun/V-ing? · Which would you recommend?`

Discuss a choice among options. Either one works accepts either of two choices. Ask about options before choosing; ask which someone recommends when you want their judgment.

Examples:

- We could meet Tuesday or Thursday. Either one works for me.
- What are our options for reducing the cost?
- Which would you recommend for someone who travels frequently?

## `be willing/unwilling to + verb`

Describe whether someone is prepared to do something. Willing does not necessarily mean enthusiastic; unwilling describes a lack of willingness, not inability. Use can/be able to when the issue is ability.

Examples:

- I am willing to adjust the schedule if we can keep the deadline.
- They were unwilling to share the data without permission.

## `be worth + noun/V-ing · There is no point in + V-ing · It makes sense to + verb`

Evaluate whether an action offers enough value or serves a purpose. Worth takes a noun or -ing form. No point in says an action lacks a useful purpose. Makes sense to says it is reasonable in context; it does not necessarily mean it is morally right or the only option.

Examples:

- Is the upgrade worth the extra cost? / Is it worth upgrading now?
- There is no point in collecting feedback if nobody reads it.
- It makes sense to test a smaller version first. / Does it make sense to renew for another year?

## `can/be able to + verb · could + verb · managed to + verb · could not + verb + in time · cannot seem to + verb`

<!-- overview-card -->

Describe ability and actual success. Could often describes general past ability; managed to highlights success on a particular difficult occasion. Was able to can also report that success. Could not can describe a particular failure. Cannot seem to expresses difficulty despite trying. Finally adds the sense that a delay or obstacle has ended.

Examples:

- I can work remotely now. / I am able to work remotely now.
- When I was younger, I could study for hours without a break.
- I managed to recover the file. / I was able to recover the file.
- I could not finish the application in time.
- I can finally access the portal, but I cannot seem to upload the attachment.

## `try to + verb · try + V-ing`

Try to emphasizes an attempt; try doing presents an action as a method or experiment. The meanings can overlap when an attempted action also tests a solution. Neither construction guarantees success.

Examples:

- I tried to restart the laptop, but it would not respond.
- Try restarting the laptop; that may fix it.

## `mean to + verb · mean + V-ing`

Mean to expresses intention; mean doing describes what something entails. Did not mean to is useful when acknowledging an unintended action. The different complement changes the relationship, not just the style.

Examples:

- I did not mean to interrupt you.
- Accepting this role would mean moving to another city.

## `need + V-ing · need to be + past participle`

Describe an action that needs to be performed on the subject. The -ing form has a passive interpretation here. Need to be + past participle is a broadly useful AmE alternative. Contrast with a person who needs to perform an action.

Examples:

- The report needs updating. / The report needs to be updated.
- We need to update the report before the meeting.

## `remember/forget + to + verb · remember/forget + V-ing`

Remember/forget to points to an action you need to perform. Remember doing points to a memory of an earlier event; never forget doing says that memory will stay with you.

Examples:

- I remembered to attach the file. / I remember attaching the file.
- I forgot to lock the door. / I will never forget meeting her.

## `stop + V-ing · stop + to + verb`

Stop doing ends an activity. Stop to do pauses another activity so you can do something else. The infinitive gives the purpose of stopping.

Examples:

- I stopped checking email during dinner. — I no longer do that.
- I stopped to check an urgent email. — I paused what I was doing.

## `regret + V-ing · regret to say/inform + clause`

Regret doing looks back at an action with sorrow. Regret to say/inform introduces unwelcome news now and is formal, especially in notices or letters. In ordinary conversation, I am sorry is usually less distant.

Examples:

- I regret sending that message before checking the facts.
- We regret to inform you that your application was unsuccessful.

## `go on + V-ing · go on + to + verb`

Distinguish continuing the same activity from moving to a new one. Go on doing continues; go on to do introduces a subsequent action or stage.

Examples:

- She paused for questions, then went on explaining the same process.
- After explaining the problem, she went on to propose a solution.

## `have trouble/difficulty + V-ing · have trouble with + noun`

Describe something you find hard to do. Trouble/difficulty take an -ing action without to; trouble with names the problematic thing. Having trouble describes a current difficulty.

Examples:

- I have trouble explaining this briefly. / I have difficulty explaining this briefly.
- I am having trouble with the payment page.

## `be done + V-ing / with + noun · still need to + verb`

Report what is finished and what remains. Done doing and done with can describe completion. Done with can also mean wanting no further involvement; context determines which meaning applies.

Examples:

- I am done checking the figures. / I am done with the spreadsheet.
- I still need to write the summary before I can send it.

## `It takes + (person +) time/effort + to + verb · spend + time/money + on + noun / V-ing · How much longer will + clause?`

Takes describes the time or effort required; spend makes the person or organization the subject. Use on before a noun after spend; with time + -ing, on is usually omitted. How much longer asks about the remaining duration.

Examples:

- It takes me an hour to prepare the report. / I spend an hour preparing the report.
- The company spent a large part of its budget on training.
- How long does it take to get there? / How much longer will the repairs take?

## `be busy + V-ing · be busy with + noun`

Say an activity or task is occupying your time. Use -ing directly for the activity and with before a noun. This can explain why you are unavailable without rejecting someone personally.

Examples:

- I am busy preparing for tomorrow’s presentation.
- She is busy with a customer right now.

## `have/get a chance to + verb · give + person + a chance/the opportunity to + verb · Have you had a chance to + verb?`

Describe an opportunity or politely ask about progress. Have you had a chance to can sound less demanding than Have you done it, but context and tone still matter. Get a chance can mean an opportunity becomes available.

Examples:

- I finally got a chance to discuss the proposal with her.
- The placement gave me the opportunity to work with customers directly. / It gave me a chance to handle real customer requests.
- Have you had a chance to look at the estimate?

## `Could you + verb? · I was wondering if you could + verb · Would it be possible to + verb? · Could I ask you a favor? · I would be grateful if you could + verb`

<!-- overview-card -->

Make a request. Could you is a normal polite choice; I was wondering softens the approach and does not necessarily refer to a past request. Would it be possible asks about feasibility and can leave the actor implicit. Add a clear object or deadline when needed.

Examples:

- Could you send me the revised estimate by Thursday?
- I was wondering if you could review the opening paragraph.
- Would it be possible to move the appointment to next week?
- Could I ask you a favor? Could you check the address before I book?
- I would be grateful if you could confirm the booking by Friday. — A more formal request.

## `Can/Could I + verb? · Do you mind if + present clause? · Would you mind + V-ing? · Is it okay if + clause? · Would you mind if + past clause?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Ask permission or request an action. Do you mind if I asks about your action; would you mind doing asks the other person to act. A no response to mind normally means no objection. Use a clear phrase such as Go ahead or Sure to avoid confusion.

Examples:

- Do you mind if I record the call? — No, go ahead.
- Would you mind closing the window? — Sure, no problem.
- Is it okay if I send the draft tomorrow morning?
- Can I use this seat? / Could I use this seat?
- Would you mind if I opened the window? — A polite request about now, despite the past form.

## `Do you want me to + verb? · Do you need a hand with + noun? · What would you like me to + verb?`

Offer help or ask which action the other person wants. In want/like + me + to, me performs the following action. Need a hand is conversational.

Examples:

- Do you want me to book the room?
- Do you need a hand with your luggage?
- What would you like me to check first?

## `Are you free + time/to + verb? · Does + time + work for you? · Could we move + event + to + time? · I can’t make it (to + event) · Can we make it + time? · When will you be ready to + verb? · Do you have any plans for + time?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Check availability, propose a time, reschedule, or ask about readiness. Free refers to availability, not price, in this context. Work for you asks whether a proposal is suitable. Make it means manage to attend or arrive; Can we make it + time? proposes a different time.

Examples:

- Are you free tomorrow afternoon? / Are you free to talk for ten minutes?
- Does three o’clock work for you?
- Could we move the meeting to Friday?
- Sorry, I can’t make it to the team lunch. Can we make it Thursday instead?
- When will you be ready to share the first draft?
- Do you have any plans for the weekend?

## `Do you want to/Would you like to + verb? · How about + noun/V-ing? · Why don’t we + verb? · Let’s + verb`

<!-- overview-card -->

Invite someone or suggest a joint action. Do you want to asks about their preference; how about and why don’t we propose an option. Let’s is the normal conversational contraction of let us for a suggestion. We should ... sometime is a friendly suggestion, but it does not establish a specific plan.

Examples:

- Do you want to get dinner after the conference? / Would you like to get dinner after the conference?
- How about meeting somewhere near your office?
- Why don’t we split the work and compare notes tomorrow?
- Let’s take a short break. / We should go hiking sometime.

## `go + recreational activity in -ing`

Describe participating in activities such as hiking, shopping, swimming, or skiing. This is a conventional activity pattern, not permission to put any verb after go in -ing form.

Examples:

- We went hiking after the conference.
- Would you like to go swimming this weekend?

## `That would be great, thanks · I’m good, thanks · Thanks for asking, but + clause · I’d love to, but + clause · I would rather not + verb`

<!-- overview-card -->

Accept or decline an offer or invitation. I’m good, thanks politely declines a small offer such as food, a drink, or help. Thanks for asking acknowledges the offer before declining; I’d love to, but declines an invitation warmly. Would rather not states a preference without requiring an invented excuse.

Examples:

- That would be great, thanks. I could use some help with the setup.
- A: Want some more coffee? B: I’m good, thanks.
- Thanks for asking, but I already have plans that evening. / I’d love to, but I already have plans that evening.
- I would rather not join the group photo.

## `Please do not + verb · I need you to + verb · I need + noun + from + person`

Set a boundary or state what you need from someone. These can be firm instructions; use them when the situation justifies directness. Name the action or information clearly.

Examples:

- Please do not share this outside the team.
- I need you to let me finish before responding.
- I need a clear answer from you before I can make plans.

## `Sorry for/about + noun · Sorry for + V-ing · That was my mistake`

Apologize and acknowledge responsibility. Sorry about commonly names a situation; sorry for can name your action. Explain a concrete repair when one is needed. Sorry to hear that instead expresses sympathy, not necessarily responsibility.

Examples:

- I am sorry about the confusion. / I am sorry for sending the wrong link.
- That was my mistake. I will send the corrected version now.

## `I appreciate + noun/V-ing/how-clause · Thank you for + noun/V-ing`

Express thanks. Appreciate takes a direct object; do not add for before it. Thank you takes for before the thing or action. Object-form subjects such as you taking are natural in conversation.

Examples:

- I appreciate your patience. / I appreciate you taking the time to explain.
- Thank you for checking the figures.
- I appreciate how carefully you explained the options.

## `How have you been? · How is + event/day + going? · How is + task + coming along? · What have you been up to? · How was + event?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Open a conversation. Have you been and been up to look back over a recent period; how is ... going asks about something in progress; how is ... coming along asks about progress on a task or project; how was asks about a finished event. What have you been up to asks about recent activities, not just health.

Examples:

- How have you been? It has been a while since we last talked.
- How is your first week going?
- How is the new website coming along?
- What have you been up to since you moved?
- How was your trip to Los Angeles?

## `I think/suppose/guess/hope so · I do not think so · I suppose/guess/hope not · I am afraid so/not`

<!-- overview-card -->

Respond to a yes-or-no proposition without restating it. So stands for the positive proposition. The negative forms depend on the expression: I do not think so, but I hope not. I am afraid so/not politely introduces an unwelcome answer; it does not necessarily express fear.

Examples:

- Will the office be open? — I think so. / I suppose so. / I guess so.
- Will the meeting run late? — I do not think so. / I suppose not. / I guess not. / I hope not.
- Will the replacement arrive today? — I hope so.
- Do we need to start again? — I am afraid so. / Can we recover the original file? — I am afraid not.

## `What do you think of/about + noun? · How do you feel about + noun/V-ing?`

Invite a view or reaction. Think of and think about often overlap when asking for an opinion. Feel about can foreground an attitude or personal reaction; it does not demand an emotional answer.

Examples:

- What do you think of the new office? / What do you think about the new office?
- How do you feel about working abroad?

## `I am glad/relieved that + clause · I am glad/relieved to + verb`

Express a positive reaction. Glad expresses happiness; relieved suggests that a worry has eased. Use that to name a complete situation and to before an action with the same understood subject.

Examples:

- I am glad that you enjoyed the visit. / I am glad to hear that you enjoyed the visit.
- I am relieved that everyone got home safely. / I am relieved to be home after that flight.

## `I am worried/concerned that + clause · I am worried/concerned about + noun/V-ing · It concerns me that + clause · My concern is that + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->

Express worry and identify its cause. Worried and concerned often overlap in everyday and professional English. That introduces a clause; about introduces a noun or -ing action. It concerns me makes the situation the cause of worry. My concern uses the noun to introduce the issue. As far as I am concerned instead introduces your viewpoint.

Examples:

- I'm worried that we're promising more than we can deliver. / I'm concerned that we're promising more than we can deliver.
- I'm worried about missing the deadline. / I'm concerned about missing the deadline.
- It concerns me that nobody explained the change.
- My concern is that users could lose access to their accounts.

## `I feel + adjective + when + clause · It bothers me that/when + clause · I love it/hate it/can’t stand it when + clause`

Describe how a situation affects you. I feel names your reaction. It bothers me expresses annoyance, distress, or worry. In I love it/hate it when, it points forward to the situation in the when-clause; can’t stand is stronger than hate in everyday speech. State the behavior or situation without assuming you know the other person’s intentions.

Examples:

- I feel left out when decisions are made without me. / I love it when the whole team is involved in a decision.
- It bothers me that nobody explained the change.
- It bothers me when people interrupt before I have finished. / I can’t stand it when people interrupt before I’ve finished.

## `I can see why + clause · Do you want to talk about it? · I am sorry to hear that + clause`

Acknowledge another person’s experience and offer support. I can see why recognizes a reason without endorsing every reaction. Sorry to hear expresses sympathy; it does not mean you caused the problem.

Examples:

- I can see why you are frustrated; the instructions changed twice.
- Do you want to talk about it, or would you prefer some time alone?
- I am sorry to hear that your trip was canceled.

## `be comfortable/uncomfortable + V-ing · be comfortable/uncomfortable with + noun`

Describe whether an activity or situation feels acceptable or manageable. Uncomfortable can express hesitation or a boundary without claiming inability. Getting comfortable describes growing confidence.

Examples:

- I am not comfortable sharing that information.
- I am getting more comfortable speaking up in meetings.
- Are you comfortable with the proposed schedule?

## `It was great talking to you · I should get going · Let’s keep in touch`

Close a conversation politely. It was great talking to you acknowledges the interaction; I should get going signals departure; let’s keep in touch expresses interest in future contact without scheduling it.

Examples:

- It was great talking to you. I enjoyed hearing about your work.
- I should get going; I have an early meeting tomorrow.
- Let’s keep in touch. I will send you the article we discussed.

## `I work as/for/on + noun · I have + duration + of experience in/with + noun · My role involves/involved + V-ing`

Introduce your work precisely. As names a role; for names an employer or client; on names a project or task. Experience in often names a field or activity; with often names a tool, group, or practical area. They can overlap. Involves describes what a role includes.

Examples:

- I work as a software engineer for a small company, mainly on internal tools.
- I have five years of experience in backend development and three years of experience with distributed systems.
- My previous role involved coordinating releases across three teams.

## `be responsible for + noun/V-ing · My contribution was to + verb · I + action, which + result`

Explain responsibility and contribution. Responsible for can identify a duty or accountability for an outcome; it does not always mean personally causing it. Follow a role description with a concrete action and result. Which can refer to the preceding action or situation, not just a single noun.

Examples:

- I was responsible for reviewing deployment plans.
- My contribution was to automate the repetitive checks.
- I shortened the approval process, which reduced the average waiting time by two days.

## `Where are we with + task? · Could you give me an update on + task? · I will keep you posted on + noun`

Request or promise progress information. Where are we with asks about status, not physical location. Keep you posted promises updates as information becomes available.

Examples:

- Where are we with the data migration?
- Could you give me an update on the customer interviews?
- I will keep you posted on the launch date.

## `Here is what we agreed on · The next step is to + verb`

Summarize a decision and its immediate follow-through. Here is what introduces the actual agreement; the next step identifies the next action. Neither expression substitutes for naming an owner or deadline when those matter.

Examples:

- Here is what we agreed on: the first release will support email login only.
- The next step is to confirm who owns the approval.

## `We are blocked by + noun · We need more time to + verb`

Explain an obstacle or request more time. Blocked by names a dependency preventing progress. Need more time describes the time requirement; explain the reason when requesting a change.

Examples:

- We are blocked by the missing test credentials.
- We need more time to verify the migration before we commit to a date.

## `Just a heads-up: + clause · I (just) wanted to let you know/check/ask + clause · Just a quick question: + question`

Give advance notice or open a short work message without sounding demanding. Heads-up is conversational and common at work. I wanted to let you know softens the introduction; the information itself can be current. Just makes a check, question, or follow-up sound small and low-pressure.

Examples:

- Just a heads-up: the maintenance window has moved to Friday.
- I wanted to let you know that I will be offline tomorrow morning. / I just wanted to check whether Thursday still works for you.
- Just a quick question: are the slides due today or tomorrow?

## `I would appreciate your feedback on + noun · Could you tell me what I could improve?`

Ask for an assessment that helps you revise your work. Name the part you want reviewed. The embedded what-clause uses statement order.

Examples:

- I would appreciate your feedback on the revised timeline.
- Could you tell me what I could improve in the opening paragraph?

## `I am writing to + verb · I am writing about/regarding/in response to + noun`

State the purpose of an email or letter. To introduces your intended action; about/regarding names the topic; in response to names the message or announcement prompting you to write. Regarding is more formal than about.

Examples:

- I am writing to ask whether the workshop is open to remote participants.
- I am writing about the duplicate charge. / I am writing regarding the duplicate charge.
- I am writing in response to your request for feedback.

## `I am following up on + noun · Please let me know + if/whether/wh-clause`

Refer back to an earlier request and specify the information you need. Follow up on identifies the earlier matter; let me know asks the recipient to inform you. Keep statement word order after whether or a question word.

Examples:

- I am following up on the estimate you sent last week.
- Please let me know whether the price includes installation.
- Please let me know when you expect to finish.

## `Something went wrong with + noun · X is not working properly · It only happens when + clause`

Describe an observable problem and the conditions that trigger it. Name the failed behavior before proposing a cause. Only happens when limits the circumstances in which you observed it.

Examples:

- Something went wrong with the upload; half the files are missing.
- The search filter is not working properly.
- It only happens when I switch accounts.

## `Can you walk me through + noun? · Let me walk you through + noun`

Ask for or offer a step-by-step explanation. Walk someone through can refer to a process, experience, or reasoning; it is not limited to troubleshooting.

Examples:

- Can you walk me through the steps that led to the error?
- Let me walk you through how we estimated the cost.

## `That did not solve/fix + noun · X solved/fixed + noun`

Report whether an attempted remedy worked. Name the remaining problem or the improvement. Solve suits problems; fix commonly suits faults and broken things. The words overlap for many practical problems.

Examples:

- That did not solve the problem; the same error appears after I sign in.
- Restarting the service fixed the connection issue.

## `The problem with + noun/V-ing + is that + clause · A possible solution to + noun + is to + verb · One way to + verb + is to + verb`

Move from a problem to a response or method. Problem with identifies a weakness; solution to names a response to a problem; one way to describes a method without claiming it is the only one.

Examples:

- The problem with the current process is that nobody owns the final decision.
- A possible solution to the delay is to assign one reviewer.
- One way to reduce misunderstandings is to summarize decisions in writing.

## `One advantage/disadvantage of + noun/V-ing + is that + clause · The downside is that + clause · X comes at the cost of Y`

Compare benefits and costs. Advantage/disadvantage introduces one consideration. The downside highlights a drawback. Comes at the cost of identifies what is sacrificed for a benefit; name the loss clearly rather than naming an ambiguous metric.

Examples:

- One advantage of remote work is that I can avoid a long commute.
- One disadvantage of remote work is that informal conversations take more effort. / The downside is that it can feel isolating.
- The simpler design comes at the cost of fewer customization options.

## `There is a risk of + V-ing`

Use this to describe a possible harmful or unwanted result.

- There is a risk of losing public trust if the process is not transparent.
- There is a serious risk of widening the gap between urban and rural communities.
- Without regular maintenance, there is a risk of the equipment failing unexpectedly.

## `Compared with/to + noun, + clause · in terms of + aspect · be about the same · differ in + aspect · What makes X better than Y?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Set a comparison and its dimension. Compared with and compared to often overlap in ordinary comparisons. About the same means approximately equal; differ in identifies the dimension of difference. Make sure the subject after a comparison phrase is the thing being compared.

Examples:

- Compared with the old design, the new one is easier to maintain. / Compared to the old design, the new one is easier to maintain.
- They are about the same in terms of cost, but they differ in reliability.
- I do not see much difference between the two plans.
- What makes this option better than the other one?

## `much/far/slightly/a little + comparative + than · any/no + comparative`

<!-- overview-card -->

Place these words before a comparative to show whether the difference is large or small.

- Public transportation is far more affordable than owning a car. / It is much cheaper than driving every day.
- The revised proposal is slightly less expensive than the original one.
- The second explanation was a little clearer than the first.
- Is the connection any faster now? — No, it is no faster than before.

## `twice/three times as + adjective/adverb + as · twice/three times the + size/number/amount + of`

Compare quantities by a multiplier. Keep the measured quantity clear: twice as long refers to duration or length, while twice as many refers to a count. Avoid ambiguous wording such as twice smaller.

Examples:

- The new battery lasts twice as long as the old one.
- This year we received three times as many applications as last year.
- The new office is twice the size of the old one.

## `as + adjective/adverb + as · not as + adjective/adverb + as · comparative + than expected · as + adjective/adverb + as possible/you can`

Compare equality, a lower degree, or a result against expectations. As ... as works with adjectives and adverbs. As ... as possible or as ... as you can asks for the greatest feasible degree; it does not always mean quickly.

Examples:

- The smaller room is as quiet as the larger one, but it is not as bright.
- The task was easier than I expected. / It was not as difficult as it seemed.
- Please explain the problem as clearly as possible. / Please explain it as clearly as you can.

## `the + superlative + noun · one of the + superlative + plural noun · the + superlative + noun + I have ever + past participle`

Identify an extreme within a relevant group. One of requires a plural group after the superlative. Add ever + present perfect to compare the current example with your experience up to now.

Examples:

- This is the most practical option within our budget.
- She is one of the most reliable people I have worked with.
- That was the best meal I have ever had on a flight.

## `too + adjective/adverb + to + verb · adjective/adverb + enough + to + verb · enough + noun + to/for`

Too describes exceeding a useful limit; enough describes reaching the required amount or degree. Put enough after an adjective/adverb but before a noun. Use for to identify a person or purpose. Too does not merely mean very.

Examples:

- The room is too small to hold everyone. / The room is large enough to hold everyone.
- She explained it slowly enough for everyone to follow.
- We have enough time to test it, and two days should be enough for the review.

## `so + adjective/adverb (that + clause) · such + (a/an +) noun phrase (that + clause)`

Use so before an adjective/adverb and such before a noun phrase. Both intensify a description and can introduce a result with that. A singular count noun needs a/an after such; use so much/many before a quantity.

- The application process was so complicated that many people gave up.
- It was such a convincing presentation that the committee approved the proposal immediately.
- She explained it so clearly that we finished in minutes. / It was such a useful explanation.

## `even + focused phrase · even + comparative`

Highlight an unexpected inclusion or a further change in degree. Position even near what is surprising; moving it can change the focus. This use is separate from even though and even if, which introduce clauses.

Examples:

- Even the manager had trouble finding the setting. — The manager was an unexpected person to have trouble.
- The new interface is even easier to use than the previous version.
- She even checked the links in the footnotes. — An unexpected extra action.

## `only + focused phrase · not all/every + noun`

Limit a claim precisely. Place only where the intended restriction is clear, and use stress or rephrasing if needed. Not all/every means at least one exception; it does not mean none.

Examples:

- Only Maya reviewed the draft. / Maya reviewed only the draft. — Who reviewed versus what she reviewed.
- Not all the changes were useful. / None of the changes were useful. — Some exceptions versus zero useful changes.

## `The more/less + clause, the more/less + clause · As + quantity + changes, + clause`

Describe linked changes. The paired comparative makes the relationship prominent; as can also link changes over time or degree. Neither construction by itself proves that one change causes the other.

Examples:

- The more clearly we define the task, the less time we spend revising it.
- The longer I work without a break, the harder it is to concentrate.
- As the number of users increases, the average response time rises.

## `get/become + adjective/comparative · comparative and comparative · more and more + adjective/noun · a growing number of + plural noun`

<!-- overview-card -->

Describe a developing state or trend. Get is often conversational; become is neutral and works well in formal explanations too. Repeat a short comparative, but use more and more with adjectives that take more. A growing number of takes a plural verb.

Examples:

- The evenings are getting colder. / The evenings are becoming colder.
- The process is getting easier and easier.
- The requirements are becoming more and more complicated.
- A growing number of employees are choosing flexible schedules.
- More and more people are choosing to work remotely.

## `increase/decrease by + amount · increase/decrease from X to Y · an increase/decrease in + variable`

Distinguish the amount of change from its starting and ending levels. By names the change; from and to name levels; in names the variable. A change from 20% to 30% is ten percentage points, or a 50% relative increase.

Examples:

- The price increased by ten dollars, from fifty to sixty. / The price increased to sixty dollars.
- There has been a decrease in the number of complaints.
- The fee decreased by ten dollars, from sixty to fifty. / The fee decreased to fifty dollars.

## `account for/make up + share + of + whole · fraction/percentage + of + group`

Describe a share of a total. Account for and make up overlap in this numerical sense. Use of before the whole or group; this is different from reporting the amount by which a number changed.

Examples:

- Support requests account for about a third of our incoming messages. / Support requests make up about a third of our incoming messages.
- About 40% of the respondents work remotely.
- Three out of four participants preferred the shorter form.

## `The chart/table shows + noun/wh-clause · The figures for + group + verb`

Introduce what a visual or data set represents. Shows can introduce a noun phrase or a clause such as how much changed. Figures for identifies the group or measure. Describe what the data support; the construction does not establish a cause.

Examples:

- The chart shows how average commuting time changed over five years.
- The figures for remote workers remained stable throughout the period.

## `the number of + plural count noun · the amount of + uncountable noun · a number of + plural count noun`

The number of measures a count and takes a singular verb; the amount of measures an uncountable quantity and also takes a singular verb. A number of means several and takes a plural verb.

Examples:

- The number of applications has increased, but the amount of available funding has not.
- A number of applicants have asked for feedback.

## `about/roughly + number · more or less + quantity/description · at least/no less than + number · at most/no more than + number`

<!-- overview-card -->

Give an estimate or bound. About, roughly, and more or less can express approximation. At least sets a minimum; at most sets a maximum. No less than can emphasize a surprisingly large amount; no more than can emphasize a small one. The bound is not necessarily the exact amount.

Examples:

- About forty people attended. / Roughly forty people attended.
- The task took more or less an hour. / The two versions are more or less identical.
- We need at least three reviewers; no fewer would be enough.
- The visit will take at most two hours. / The visit will take no more than two hours.
- No less than fifty people volunteered.

## `few/a few + plural count noun · a couple of + plural count noun · little/a little + uncountable noun · fewer/less + noun`

Few/little emphasizes scarcity; a few/a little emphasizes that some is available. A couple of means two, or loosely a small number, in conversation. Use fewer with countable items and less with amounts. Less is also standard with measurements treated as amounts, such as time or money. Use many with plural count nouns and much with uncountable nouns; a lot of works with either.

Examples:

- Few applicants had relevant experience. / A few applicants had relevant experience. / A couple of applicants had worked in healthcare.
- We have little time left. / We have a little time left.
- We received fewer complaints and spent less time answering them. / The repair took less than two hours.

## `much + uncountable noun · many + plural count noun · a lot of/lots of/plenty of + noun`

Choose a quantity expression that matches countability. A lot of and lots of work with both types and are common in conversation. Much is especially natural in questions, negatives, and after too/so; plain affirmative much often sounds formal. Plenty of means enough or more than enough.

Examples:

- Do we have much time? / Do we have many options?
- We have a lot of work and lots of unanswered questions.
- There is plenty of room, but there are too many chairs near the door.

## `all/most/some + noun · all/most/some of + determiner/pronoun`

Without of, refer to a group directly. Use of before the/these/my or an object pronoun to select from an identified group. All can omit of before a determiner, but keep it before an object pronoun.

Examples:

- Most people prefer clear instructions. / Most of the people we interviewed preferred a shorter form.
- All of us contributed, and some of our suggestions were accepted.
- All my notes are online. / All of my notes are online.

## `both + plural noun · either/neither + singular noun · both/either/neither of + identified pair`

Describe two choices: both includes the two; either selects one or the other; neither excludes both. Use of before a determiner or object pronoun. Either/neither before a singular noun takes a singular verb; agreement after of varies, with singular the cautious formal choice.

Examples:

- Both options work. Either option is acceptable. Neither option requires a subscription.
- Both of us can drive, so either of us can take you.
- Neither of these plans includes support.

## `some/any + noun · someone/anyone · something/anything`

Some often introduces an unspecified amount in positive statements; any is common in open questions, negatives, and conditions. Some is natural in offers or requests that expect an available item. Any can also mean whichever you choose.

Examples:

- I bought some fruit, but I did not buy any bread.
- Would you like something to drink? / Do you have anything without caffeine?
- If anyone needs help, ask someone at the front desk. / Choose any seat.

## `no + noun · not ... any + noun · none of + identified group`

Express zero quantity. No takes a noun; none of selects zero members from a specified group. Not ... any often expresses the same meaning as no. Do not add another negative to express the same negation in standard English.

Examples:

- There are no seats left. / There are not any seats left.
- None of the proposals addressed the main concern.

## `all (of) the + noun · the whole + singular count noun · every + singular count noun`

Distinguish a complete amount, a complete individual thing, and every member or occasion. All and whole overlap with singular countable periods or objects, but use all with uncountable nouns. Every day means each day; all day means throughout one day.

Examples:

- I read the whole report. / I read all of the report.
- We need all the information before deciding.
- I worked all day yesterday. / I work from home every day.

## `each + singular noun · every + singular noun · each of + group · every one of + group`

Each draws attention to individual members; every includes all members of a group. They often overlap. Each can refer to two; every normally involves a larger group and also expresses frequency. Use every one of, not every of.

Examples:

- Each reviewer has a copy. / Every reviewer has a copy.
- Each of the two proposals has a clear advantage.
- I checked every one of the attachments; each of them opened correctly.

## `Do/Does/Did + subject + base verb? · be/auxiliary/modal + subject ...? · Who/What + verb ...?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use do/does/did for ordinary simple-tense questions; keep the main verb in its base form. Invert be or an existing helper/modal instead of adding do. A question word that is itself the subject normally does not require do. Distinguish Who called you? from Who did you call?

Examples:

- How does the system work? / Why did you decide to leave?
- Where is the entrance? / Have they confirmed? / Can we start?
- Who changed the settings? / What happened to the original plan?
- Who did you ask for help?

## `How come + subject + verb? · Why + auxiliary + subject + verb?`

Ask for a reason. How come is conversational and uses statement word order. Why normally uses ordinary question order. They can ask about the same event; the difference is wording and structure, not a separate kind of cause.

Examples:

- How come the deadline changed? / Why did the deadline change?
- How come she is leaving early? / Why is she leaving early?

## `Do you know/Could you tell me + wh-word + subject + verb? · I wonder + wh-clause/if/whether-clause · Do you happen to know + noun?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use statement word order inside an indirect question. Use if/whether for a yes-or-no question, and keep the relevant question word for other questions. I wonder and I have been wondering present an unresolved question. Happen to softens a question about knowledge. Ordinary present simple forms still agree with their subjects.

Examples:

- Could you tell me where the meeting is? / Do you know when it starts?
- I have been wondering how this feature works.
- I wonder whether the office is open tomorrow.
- Do you happen to know the new project manager?

## `positive statement, negative auxiliary + pronoun? · negative statement, positive auxiliary + pronoun?`

Use a question tag to invite confirmation. Match the tense and helper; use do/does/did when there is no helper. A falling voice often expects agreement; a rising voice asks more openly. The common tag after I am is aren’t I. Answer according to the fact, even after a negative statement.

Examples:

- The meeting starts at ten, doesn’t it?
- You have not sent it yet, have you? — Yes, I have; I sent it this morning.
- I am next, aren’t I?

## `do/does/did not + base verb · be/auxiliary/modal + not · never/nobody/nothing + affirmative verb`

Form a negative with the first helper; ordinary simple-tense main verbs need do/does/did. Never, nobody, and nothing already express negation in standard English, so do not add not to the same clause for the same negative meaning. Negative contractions are normal in everyday AmE.

Examples:

- I do not understand the error. / She did not receive the message.
- The document is not ready, and I have not checked the figures.
- Nobody replied. / I did not hear anything. / I never received a reply.

## `There is/are + noun · There is/are + noun + V-ing · There seems to be + noun · There used to be + noun`

<!-- overview-card -->

Introduce the existence or presence of something. Use is with a singular or uncountable noun and are with a plural noun in careful speech and writing; plural there’s is common casually but is not the careful model here. An -ing modifier describes the noun; it does not need a second are. There seems to be expresses an impression; there used to be describes a past state.

Examples:

- There is a problem with the estimate. / There are two problems with the estimate.
- There are more and more people using AI at work.
- There seems to be a misunderstanding.
- There used to be a bookstore on this corner.

## `There is/are + new thing · It is + known thing’s description · It is + weather/time/distance`

There introduces existence; it describes an identified thing or serves as the subject for weather, time, and distance. Do not use it to introduce a new object merely because your first language omits a subject.

Examples:

- There is a café downstairs. It is open until eight.
- It is raining, and there is water coming through the roof.
- It is almost noon, and it is about two miles to the station.

## `a/an + singular count noun · the + identified noun · plural/uncountable noun for a general class`

Use a/an for one nonspecific countable member and the when the listener can identify which one. General plurals and uncountable nouns often take no article. Choose a/an by the next sound, not just the next letter. A singular count noun normally needs an article or another determiner such as my or this.

Examples:

- I spoke to a recruiter. The recruiter asked about my current role.
- Software can save time, but the software we bought is difficult to use.
- She is an engineer at a university, and she has a useful perspective.

## `a piece/item/unit/container of + uncountable noun · a pair of + plural item`

Count an amount with a suitable unit rather than giving an uncountable noun an arbitrary plural. Some nouns also become countable for a serving or an individual experience. Choose the unit that fits the noun.

Examples:

- She gave me two pieces of advice and a useful piece of information.
- I need a bottle of water and a pair of scissors.
- We ordered two coffees. / I do not drink much coffee.

## `at school/work · in college/bed/prison · go to school/work/bed · at/in the + specific place`

Some institutional activities use no article, while a specific building or object often takes the. These are conventional combinations, not a rule for every place. In AmE, a patient is usually in the hospital; in hospital is a standard British form. AmE commonly uses in college for enrollment.

Examples:

- My daughter is at school. I am at the school to meet her teacher.
- She is in college, and her brother is at work.
- I went to bed early, but my phone was under the bed. / He is in the hospital recovering from surgery.

## `noun + noun · noun + of + contents · number-unit + noun`

Use a noun before another noun to identify its type or purpose; the first noun is usually singular, with conventional exceptions. Of often identifies contents. A measurement before a noun normally uses a singular unit; after a verb, use the ordinary plural.

Examples:

- We need a coffee cup. / We need a cup of coffee. — A kind of container versus its contents.
- It is a two-week course. / The course lasts two weeks.
- The customer support team handles account questions.

## `singular subject + singular verb · plural subject + plural verb · each/every + singular noun · one of + plural noun`

Choose the verb form from the actual subject, not a nearby noun. Each/every + singular noun and one of a group normally take a singular verb. A pair of nouns joined by and usually takes a plural verb; an -ing activity used as one subject normally takes a singular verb.

Examples:

- The list of requirements is on the shared drive. / The requirements are on the shared drive.
- Each reviewer has a copy. / One of the reviewers has raised a concern.
- Reading and writing are different skills. / Reading long reports takes practice.

## `possessive determiner + noun · possessive pronoun · a friend/colleague of mine/yours/hers/ours/theirs`

Use my/your/their before a noun and mine/yours/theirs when the noun is not repeated. A colleague of mine means one of my colleagues; use a possessive form after of in this construction.

Examples:

- Is this your charger? — No, mine is on the desk.
- A colleague of mine recommended the course.

## `subject + verb + reflexive pronoun · each other/one another`

A reflexive points back to the subject; each other and one another describe reciprocal actions. Reflexives can also emphasize who did something. Do not use myself simply as a formal replacement for me.

Examples:

- I taught myself the basics, then asked a colleague to check my work.
- We introduced ourselves and helped each other find our seats.
- I checked the original figures myself.

## `person/organization + ’s + noun · plural ending in s + ’ + noun · the + part + of + whole`

Express possession, association, or a part–whole relationship. Possessive ’s is common with people, organizations, and time expressions; of is often useful with parts and long noun phrases. These choices overlap, so do not teach a rigid living-versus-nonliving rule.

Examples:

- The company’s policy changed after the employees’ feedback. / Today’s meeting starts at ten.
- The cover of the report lists the authors.
- The children’s coats are beside the entrance.

## `my/your/their own + noun · a/an + noun + of my/your/their own`

Emphasize that something belongs or is assigned to that person, often rather than being shared or borrowed. Own can also emphasize doing something yourself when it appears with the object you act on.

Examples:

- I would like my own desk. / I would like a desk of my own.
- She writes her own presentations instead of using a template.

## `on my/your/their own · by myself/yourself/themselves`

Say someone is alone or does something without help. These expressions often overlap. Context determines whether you mean absence of other people or independent effort.

Examples:

- I live on my own. / I live by myself.
- She completed the application on her own. / She completed the application by herself.

## `adjective + noun · be/look/seem + adjective · verb + adverb · adverb + adjective`

Use adjectives to describe a noun or a state, and adverbs to describe how an action is performed or to modify an adjective. Not all adverbs end in -ly: fast and hard can be adverbs; hardly means almost not. Good is an adjective; well often describes how an action is performed.

Examples:

- She gave a clear explanation. / She explained it clearly.
- The design looks good, and the system works well.
- The unusually long meeting ended surprisingly quickly.

## `feeling adjective in -ed · feeling-causing adjective in -ing`

With paired adjectives such as bored/boring and confused/confusing, -ed describes the experienced feeling; -ing describes what causes it. This is a meaning contrast, not a tense rule. A person can be boring and a situation can be interesting.

Examples:

- The instructions were confusing, so I was confused.
- I am interested in the project. / I am interesting to talk to. — The second describes how other people may experience talking to me.
- It was an exhausting week, and everyone felt exhausted.

## `opinion + size/age/color/material + noun`

Put descriptive adjectives in a conventional order rather than simply translating their order from another language. Opinion usually comes before factual properties; common sequences include size, age, color, origin, and material. Use only the adjectives the listener needs.

Examples:

- They rented a comfortable new apartment.
- I keep my notes in a small blue leather notebook.

## `pretty/fairly/really/quite + adjective · not quite + adjective`

Adjust the strength of a description. Pretty is conversational; fairly often sounds moderate; really is a common intensifier. In AmE, quite can be strong, but its force varies with the adjective, tone, and context. Do not memorize a fixed ranking. Not quite means not completely.

Examples:

- The instructions are pretty clear. / The instructions are fairly clear. — The second can sound more restrained.
- The final result was really impressive. / The final result was quite impressive.
- The draft is not quite ready.

## `verb + direct object · verb + object + preposition phrase · give/send + person + thing / thing + to + person`

A verb determines the shape of its complements; do not add a preposition by translating from Mandarin. Discuss takes the topic directly. Explain takes the explanation directly and names the recipient with to. Some verbs, such as give and send, allow two object orders; do not generalize that option to every verb.

Examples:

- We discussed the plan with the client. / I explained the plan to the client.
- I sent Maya the report. / I sent the report to Maya.
- I bought her a ticket. / I bought a ticket for her.

## `subject + verb + object + manner/place/time`

Use this as a practical default order, not an absolute rule. Normally keep an ordinary object close to its verb, then add how, where, or when. A time phrase can move to the front to establish the setting. Frequency adverbs follow their own positions.

Examples:

- I explained the problem clearly during the meeting.
- We reviewed the proposal at the office yesterday. / Yesterday, we reviewed the proposal at the office.

## `subject + also/already/probably + main verb · be/first auxiliary + adverb`

Place common middle-position adverbs where they modify the verb phrase. They normally precede an ordinary main verb, follow be, or follow the first helper. Other positions are possible for focus; probably often comes before a negative contraction.

Examples:

- We also need a backup plan. / The backup is already available.
- She has already sent the draft, and we can probably review it today.
- I probably will not finish today. / I probably won’t finish today.

## `verb + object + to + verb`

Use this when the object is understood to perform or experience the following action or state. The meaning comes from the main verb: ask requests, tell directs, allow permits, cause produces a result, and expect anticipates. Common verbs include ask, tell, advise, remind, allow, enable, encourage, convince, persuade, force, want, need, and expect. Use not before to to negate the following action.

- I asked her to check the figures, and she told me to revise the estimate.
- The new tool allows us to test changes faster. / The outage caused us to postpone the release.
- We expected the client to agree, but I could not convince her to accept the revised terms.

## `make/let/have + person + base verb · have/get + object + past participle · want/need + object + past participle · get + person + to + verb · be made to + verb`

<!-- overview-card -->

Make causes or forces an action; let permits it; have arranges or instructs it. In have someone repair something, the person performs the action. In have/get something repaired, the object receives it. Get someone to do also arranges or brings about an action, but takes to. Passive make also takes to. Want/need something done describes a desired or required completed action. Have/get something done can also describe an experience, including an unwanted one; the context determines the reading.

Examples:

- The delay made us reconsider the schedule.
- My supervisor let me work from home.
- I had the technician check my laptop. / I had my laptop checked. / I got my laptop checked.
- The manager wants all applications checked by Friday. / We need the figures verified.
- I had my wallet stolen while I was traveling.
- I got the technician to check the wiring. / We were made to wait outside.

## `help + person + (to) + verb`

Help allows a base verb with or without to. The person named after help performs the action. Cannot help doing is a different expression meaning you cannot stop yourself.

Examples:

- The examples helped me understand the rule. / The examples helped me to understand the rule.
- Could you help us move the meeting to a quieter room?

## `prevent/stop/keep + person + from + V-ing`

Describe blocking or stopping an action. Use an -ing form after from. Keep someone from doing blocks the action; keep someone doing makes it continue.

Examples:

- A clear agenda prevents us from wasting time on unrelated issues.
- Noise kept me from sleeping; an urgent problem kept me working.

## `consider/find + object + adjective · regard/see/treat + object + as + adjective/noun`

Express an evaluation or classification. The verb controls the complement: consider something risky, but regard it as risky. See as describes a perspective; treat as describes how you handle it. These can overlap, but their grammar is not interchangeable.

Examples:

- I consider the proposal risky. / I regard the proposal as risky.
- I found the instructions confusing, but I see the examples as a useful starting point.
- The clinic treated the case as urgent.

## `make/keep + object + adjective`

Describe causing or maintaining a state. Make introduces the resulting condition; keep maintains it. The adjective describes the object, not how the subject performs the action.

Examples:

- The examples made the task clearer.
- Please keep the exit clear.

## `call/name/elect + object + name/role noun`

Assign a description, name, or role to an object or person. The noun after the object identifies that object. Articles depend on the complement: a descriptive category often takes a/an, while a unique appointed role may have no article.

Examples:

- Critics called the plan a mistake.
- The team named the platform Atlas, and the committee elected Maya chair.

## `verb + object + place/state phrase · find/leave + object + past participle`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use the final phrase to show where the object is placed, where it ends up, or what situation it is left in.

A past participle after find/leave can describe the object’s discovered or resulting state.

Common verbs include put, place, set, keep, leave, and send.

Examples:

- Please put the signed forms on my desk.
- The manager left me in charge of the office.
- The company sent everyone home early.
- We found the side door locked and left it closed.

## `perception verb + object + base verb / V-ing`

Use the base verb for a complete event and V-ing for an action observed in progress.

Common perception verbs include see, hear, watch, notice, and feel.

Examples:

- I watched the train leave the station.
- When I entered, I saw two students discussing the results.
- We heard someone knock on the door, then heard people talking outside.

## `verb + object + V-ing`

Use this when the object is involved in an ongoing action or situation. The object belongs to the main verb, and the V-ing part describes what that object is doing or experiencing.

Common verbs include catch, find, keep, leave, and have.

Examples:

- Security caught him entering a restricted area.
- The delay kept us waiting for nearly two hours.
- The announcement had everyone talking.

## `It is + adjective + for + person + to + verb · find/make it + adjective + to + verb · It is + adjective + of + person + to + verb`

For names the performer of an evaluated action. Find it describes your evaluation; make it describes what causes an activity to become easier, harder, or possible. Of evaluates someone’s behavior or character with adjectives such as kind or careless. It stands in for the following action, so it is not omitted.

Examples:

- It is difficult for new employees to ask for help.
- I find it useful to summarize the decision. / A template makes it easier to write the summary.
- It was kind of you to stay and help.

## `thing/person + be + easy/hard/difficult/safe + to + verb`

Evaluate an action involving the subject. The subject fills an object role in the infinitive: this tool is easy to use means using this tool is easy. Do not repeat that object. Keep a required final preposition, as in easy to work with.

Examples:

- The report is difficult to understand. / It is difficult to understand the report.
- The new manager is easy to work with.
- The water is safe to drink. — Do not add it after drink.

## `V-ing clause + singular verb · To + verb + is + complement · What + clause + is + complement`

Use an action or a what-clause as the subject. An -ing subject is a common way to discuss an activity. A to-infinitive subject is possible but often more formal; It is ... to ... is frequently easier in conversation. What ... introduces the thing or idea that fills the role.

Examples:

- Putting related expressions together helps me compare them.
- To change the deadline now would create confusion. / It would create confusion to change the deadline now.
- What she proposed is a shorter review cycle.

## `enjoy/avoid/consider/finish/keep + V-ing · feel like/cannot help + V-ing`

<!-- overview-card -->

These verbs and expressions select an -ing action. Enjoy expresses pleasure, avoid keeps away from an action, consider weighs an option, finish completes it, and keep continues it. Feel like expresses inclination; cannot help describes something you cannot easily stop yourself from doing. Many other verbs also select -ing; do not replace it with to merely because the intended action is future.

Examples:

- I enjoy discussing ideas, but I avoid interrupting people.
- We are considering changing the schedule; we have not decided yet.
- I finished reviewing the draft, but she kept adding comments.
- I feel like taking a walk. / I cannot help wondering whether we missed something.

## `suggest/recommend + V-ing / suggest/recommend (that) + subject + base verb · It is important/essential that + subject + base verb`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use V-ing for a general recommended activity and a base-verb clause when naming who should act. In this AmE construction, the verb stays in the base form even with he/she/it. The same construction can express a requirement after important or essential.

- Health experts recommend limiting the amount of added sugar in children's diets.
- I suggest that the city expand the late-night bus service.
- The doctor recommended that Maya reduce her working hours for a few weeks.
- It is essential that each participant be informed before the schedule changes.

## `preposition + (subject +) V-ing`

Use V-ing when an action follows a preposition such as **after, before, by, without, despite, about, for, in, of,** or **on**. This also applies to prepositional **to** in **look forward to, object to, be used to, be committed to, contribute to, admit to,** and **be opposed to**. By + V-ing describes a method, whereas to + verb can describe a purpose. These prepositions can also take noun phrases: I look forward to the trip. Insert a subject before V-ing when you need to name who performs the action.

- You can improve your pronunciation by recording yourself.
- The workshop ended without anyone asking a question.
- We look forward to hearing the committee's final decision.

## `... + subject + V-ing`

Use this when the V-ing situation has its own named performer. Object-form pronouns are common in conversation; possessive forms such as **his waiting** are more formal.

- I do not mind him waiting outside.
- She left without me noticing.
- Residents objected to the company collecting their personal information.

## `being + past participle · (not) having + past participle · having been + past participle/V-ing/adjective/noun`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use being + past participle when an -ing complement is passive. Having + past participle emphasizes that an event is earlier than the main situation; not having negates that earlier event. Having been preserves an earlier passive event, continuing activity, state, or identity. These perfect complements often sound more formal or explicit than a simpler -ing complement. This card describes complements, not rules for deleting a clause.

Examples:

- I dislike being interrupted during a presentation.
- She regretted not having applied earlier. / She admitted having ignored the warning.
- They objected to the data having been shared without consent.
- I remember him having been working there for several years by that point.
- She regretted having been so impatient. / He mentioned having been a volunteer before joining the staff.

## `be/get + past participle · be being + past participle · have/has been + past participle · modal + be + past participle`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use the passive when the subject receives an action. Be is the neutral passive helper; get often highlights an event, sometimes an unexpected one, and is more conversational. Being marks an action in progress; been after have/has marks a perfect form. Name the actor with by when it matters. A participle can also describe a state, so context matters.

Examples:

- The team approved the proposal. / The proposal was approved by the team.
- My account got locked after several failed attempts.
- The application is being reviewed. / The application has been reviewed.
- The engineer has been checking the system. / The system has been checked.
- The file can be shared internally, but it must not be published.

## `appear/seem + to be ... / to have + past participle`

Use `to be` when the apparent situation exists at the same time as the judgment. Use `to have + past participle` when present evidence suggests an earlier event. Use `to have been + V-ing` for an earlier continuing activity and `to have been + past participle` for an earlier passive event.

Examples:

- The server appears to be stable now. / It appears to have recovered overnight.
- The process seems to have been running all night.
- The database appears to have been corrupted during the update.

## `say (that) + clause · tell + person + clause · ask + person + if/whether/wh-clause`

Report speech or questions. Tell normally names the listener; say does not take that listener directly. Reported questions use statement order. Adjust pronouns and time references to the reporting situation. Past reporting verbs often take backshift, but still-current information can retain present tense.

Examples:

- She said that the deadline had changed. / She told me that the deadline had changed.
- He asked whether I was available and when I could start.
- She said she works remotely; that is still her current arrangement.

## `It is said/reported/believed/expected that + clause · subject + be said/reported/believed/expected + to-infinitive`

Report a claim or expectation with less focus on the source. These passives suit reports and formal explanations. To have + past participle places the reported event earlier. A reporting passive attributes a claim; it does not guarantee truth.

Examples:

- It is believed that the policy reduces costs. / The policy is believed to reduce costs.
- It is expected that demand will rise. / Demand is expected to rise.
- The supplier is reported to have missed several deadlines.

## `noun + who/that/which + clause · noun + whose + noun + clause · place/time + where/when + clause`

<!-- overview-card -->

Add information about a person, thing, possession, place, or time. An identifying relative clause normally has no commas; an extra-information clause uses commas and does not use that. Whose expresses possession and can refer to things too. Do not repeat the noun’s subject/object role with an extra pronoun inside the clause.

Examples:

- The colleague who reviewed my draft noticed a missing assumption.
- The proposal that we discussed yesterday needs a clearer budget.
- My manager, who works in Boston, will join remotely.
- I contacted the engineer whose team maintains the service.
- This is the room where we met. / I remember the day when we first spoke.

## `plural noun, some/most/none of whom/which + clause`

Add information about a subset of an already identified group. Use whom for people and which for things after of. This is useful in writing and formal explanations; in conversation, a separate sentence with them is often simpler.

Examples:

- We interviewed twelve applicants, three of whom had led remote teams.
- They proposed five changes, most of which were inexpensive.

## `It is a kind/type of + noun that + clause · It is something/someone that/who + clause · It is used for + V-ing / to + verb · What do you call + description? · What’s the word for + description?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Describe a category, person, or function when introducing an unfamiliar term or when the exact word is unavailable. That/who adds identifying information. Used for takes an -ing activity; used to takes a base verb for a function here, not the separate past-habit expression. To ask for the missing word itself, use What do you call ...? or What’s the word for ...? and then describe it.

Examples:

- It is a type of sensor that detects changes in air quality.
- It is someone who helps new employees settle in.
- It is used for measuring response times. / It is used to measure response times.
- It is something that warns you when the pressure gets too high.
- What do you call the thing you plug several chargers into? / What’s the word for a person who checks facts before an article is published?

## `consist of + parts · include + item · involve + noun/V-ing`

Describe how parts relate to a whole. Consist of presents the composition of the whole; include identifies members without requiring a complete list. Involve identifies something that participates in a process or situation. They can describe the same situation from different perspectives; they are not freely interchangeable.

Examples:

- The package consists of a camera, a cable, and a carrying case.
- The package includes a carrying case; other items are included too.
- The installation involves connecting the camera and configuring the software.

## `both X and Y · either X or Y · neither X nor Y · X rather than Y`

<!-- overview-card -->

Coordinate matching grammatical units. Both includes two; either ... or offers alternatives; neither ... nor excludes both. Rather than chooses or contrasts alternatives. With either/neither subjects, agreement usually follows the nearer subject; rephrase an awkward mix instead of forcing it.

Examples:

- We need both accurate estimates and clear priorities.
- We can either meet online or postpone the discussion.
- Neither the manager nor the reviewers are available today.
- I prefer checking the assumptions rather than guessing at a solution.

## `subject + do/does/did + base verb`

Use a stressed do, does, or did to emphasize an affirmative statement, often when correcting an assumption or acknowledging a point. The main verb stays in its base form.

- I do think AI will change parts of our work.
- She does understand the risks; she just disagrees about their likelihood.
- We did test the migration, but we missed that edge case.

## `the fact/idea/claim that + clause · noun + that + relative clause`

A content clause states what the fact, idea, or claim is. A relative clause identifies which noun you mean by giving information about it. In a content clause, that does not fill a missing subject or object role; in a relative clause, the noun has a role inside the clause. This contrast helps you distinguish reporting a claim from identifying a claim someone made.

Examples:

- The claim that prices had fallen surprised us. — The content of the claim is that prices had fallen.
- The claim that she made surprised us. — She made the claim; the clause identifies it.
- The fact that everyone agreed does not prove the idea was sound.

## `This/That + summary noun + clause · These/Those + plural noun + clause`

Refer back to a previous idea with a noun that accurately summarizes it. This approach, that decision, and these changes keep the connection explicit. The noun must match the idea; it is not an arbitrary synonym replacement.

Examples:

- We replaced weekly meetings with written updates. This approach gave everyone more uninterrupted time.
- The team shortened the form and removed duplicate questions. These changes reduced completion time.

## `Could you say/repeat + noun + again? · Could you slow down? · What do you mean by + noun? · Could you clarify + wh-clause?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Request repetition, a slower pace, or an explanation. Repeat/say again asks for the wording; clarify/mean by asks about its meaning. Use statement order inside a question embedded after clarify.

Examples:

- Could you say the last part again? / Could you repeat the address?
- Could you slow down a little? I missed the number.
- What do you mean by flexible hours?
- Could you clarify how the approval process works?

## `If I understand correctly, + clause · Just to confirm, + clause · So you are saying that + clause? · Does that make sense? · Are we on the same page about + noun?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Check your interpretation or shared understanding before proceeding. Does that make sense checks whether your explanation was clear; on the same page can check understanding or agreement, so name the issue. State the specific interpretation rather than just asking whether you understand. So you are saying can sound challenging if delivered sharply; use a neutral tone.

Examples:

- If I understand correctly, the deadline applies only to the first draft.
- Just to confirm, we are meeting at the downtown office, right?
- So you are saying that the existing accounts will keep their settings?
- Does that make sense, or should I go through an example?
- Are we on the same page about the deadline and who will review the draft?

## `Can you give me an example of + noun? · Could you explain + noun + in plain English? · I do not understand why/how + clause`

Ask for a concrete illustration or a clearer explanation. Plain English means language without unnecessary jargon. Name what remains unclear so the other person can respond usefully. The clause after why/how uses statement order.

Examples:

- Can you give me an example of a situation where this would help?
- Could you explain the limitation in plain English?
- I do not understand why the same request produces different results.

## `How would you say X in English? · Does X sound natural? · Can I use X to mean Y? · What is the difference between X and Y?`

<!-- overview-card -->

Ask about expression, naturalness, meaning, or a contrast. Give a context when possible because a phrase can be natural in one setting and unsuitable in another. How does X compare with/to Y asks for a broader comparison than a single definition.

Examples:

- How would you say “I cannot attend” more politely in English?
- Does “Could we move the meeting?” sound natural?
- Can I use “available” to mean that I have time to talk?
- What is the difference between “borrow” and “lend”? / How does this expression compare with the other one?

## `Can/Could I get + noun? · I would like + noun, please · without + ingredient · What does X come with?`

Order something or ask what is included. Can I get is ordinary conversational AmE; could I get and I would like are polite alternatives. Without requests an omission. Come with asks about included items, not necessarily everything available at extra cost.

Examples:

- Can I get a coffee? / Could I get a coffee? / I would like a coffee, please.
- Can I get the sandwich without mayonnaise?
- What does the meal come with?

## `I ordered/asked for X, but I got Y · I have not gotten my + item + yet · Could you + remedy?`

Explain a service problem and request a concrete remedy. Gotten is standard AmE for received here. Focus on what happened, then say what would resolve it.

Examples:

- I ordered a vegetarian meal, but I got the chicken. Could you replace it?
- I have not gotten my refund yet. Could you check its status?
- I asked for a room away from the elevator. Could you move me to a quieter one?

## `Do you have + item + in + size/color? · Is there another + option/time?`

Ask what is available or request an alternative. In introduces a size, color, or version. Another requests a different or additional option, depending on context.

Examples:

- Do you have this jacket in a medium? / Do you have it in blue?
- Is there another appointment time on Thursday?

## `I would like to book/reschedule/cancel + noun`

Manage a reservation or appointment. Name the booking and action clearly. Book creates the reservation; reschedule changes its time; cancel ends it.

Examples:

- I would like to book a table for two.
- I would like to reschedule my appointment for next week.
- I would like to cancel my reservation for Friday.

## `How much is it per + unit? · Is this charged by weight?`

Clarify the unit price and what determines the charge. Per asks for a rate per unit, such as a pound or a day. By weight asks whether the price depends on the item’s weight instead of a fixed price per item or container.

Examples:

- How much is it per pound?
- How much is the rental per day?
- Is the salad charged by weight or by the container?

## `check bags through to + destination`

Use this when checked luggage is sent onward to a later or final destination. Ask the airline to confirm the arrangement for your particular itinerary.

- Will our bags be checked through to Taipei?
- Can you check this bag through to Los Angeles?
- Our bags were checked through to the final destination.

## `be coated in/with + substance · be packed in + liquid/container · be served + adjective / with + accompaniment`

Describe a food or item’s presentation. Coated in/with describes a surface layer; both prepositions often work. Packed in identifies a surrounding liquid or container. Served + adjective describes the condition at serving; served with names an accompaniment.

Examples:

- The cookies are coated in chocolate. / The cookies are coated with chocolate.
- The tuna is packed in olive oil, and the jars are packed in protective boxes.
- The soup is served hot. / The soup is served with bread.

## `I have had + symptom + for + duration · It hurts when + clause`

Describe a symptom, its duration, or the action that causes discomfort. Have had relates the problem to a period up to now. These are communication patterns, not diagnostic advice.

Examples:

- I have had a headache for two days.
- It hurts when I put weight on my ankle.

## `I need help with + noun · Could you call + person/service?`

Ask for practical or urgent assistance. State the problem or needed service clearly; the same request shape works with ordinary help and emergencies.

Examples:

- I need help with my luggage.
- Could you call an ambulance? Someone has collapsed.

## `on the left/right · at the top/bottom · in the middle/center · at the top left/bottom right · in the upper-left/lower-right corner · at/near/along the edge of`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use these expressions to describe general positions and boundary areas on a
map, diagram, or plan. **At the center** is also natural when the center is
treated as an exact point.

Examples:

- The entrance is on the left, and the parking lot is on the right.
- The title is at the top, while the scale is at the bottom.
- A courtyard is in the middle of the resort, with a fountain in the center.
- The office is at the top left, and the playground is at the bottom right.
- The legend is in the upper-left corner, and the compass is in the lower-right corner.
- A gate stands at the edge of the park, several homes are near the edge, and a path runs along the edge.

## `at + point/site · on + surface/line · in + area/enclosure · located/situated at/on/in · inside/outside/within · at home/work · on a bus/train/plane · in a car/taxi`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use **at** for a point or site, **on** for a surface or line, and **in** for an
area or enclosure. **Located** is neutral; **situated** is slightly more
formal. The point-surface-area model is useful, but English also has fixed
location conventions.

Examples:

- The information booth is at the entrance, the map is on the wall, and the café is in the lobby.
- The office is located at 120 Main Street, on Fifth Avenue, in Boston.
- The cabin is situated at the edge of the lake, on a wooded hillside, in a national park.
- Equipment must remain inside the marked zone, visitors must wait outside it, and staff must stay within the designated area.
- I worked at home while my supervisor was at work.
- I reviewed the report on the bus, revised it on the train, and emailed it on the plane.
- I left my umbrella in the car and remembered it while I was in a taxi.

## `to the left/right of · next to/beside/right next to · adjacent to · between/among · across from/opposite · in front of/behind · above/below · over/under/beneath · on top of`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use these expressions to locate something relative to another object or
place. **Between** presents distinct reference points, even when there are
more than two; **among** presents something within a group. **Adjacent to** is
more formal than **next to** or **beside**.

Examples:

- The elevators are to the left of the entrance, and the restrooms are to the right of it.
- The café is next to the bank, a bench is beside the fountain, and the ATM is right next to the door.
- The parking garage is adjacent to the hospital.
- The café is between the bank and the bookstore, while the statue stands among the trees.
- The pharmacy is across from the hotel, and the bus stop is opposite the station.
- Taxis wait in front of the building, while staff parking is behind it.
- A sign is above the entrance, a bench is below it, a canopy hangs over it, and several bicycles are stored under it.
- A tunnel runs beneath the highway, and a communications antenna sits on top of the building.

## `north/south/east/west of · northeast/northwest/southeast/southwest of · in northern/southern/eastern/western X · in the north/south/east/west of X · on the northern/southern/eastern/western side of X · at the northern/southern/eastern/western end of X`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use **north of**, for example, to compare a location with a reference point.
Use **in northern X** or **in the north of X** for a location within a larger
area. Use **on the eastern side of X** for a side and **at the eastern end of
X** for an endpoint. A relative direction does not by itself say whether two
places share a wider region.

Examples:

- The station is north of the mall, the stadium is south of it, the school is east of it, and the park is west of it.
- The four clinics are northeast, northwest, southeast, and southwest of downtown, respectively.
- The company has offices in northern, southern, eastern, and western Taiwan.
- Most farms are in the north of the county, the vineyards are in the south of the county, the port is in the east of the county, and the industrial district is in the west of the county.
- The main entrances are on the northern and southern sides of the campus, while the parking areas are on its eastern and western sides.
- The trailheads are at the northern and southern ends of the route.
- The bridge is at the eastern end of the highway, and a service area is at its western end.

## `near + noun · close to + noun · nearby · fairly/pretty/relatively close to · not far from · in (fairly) close proximity to · far/quite far/pretty far from · a long way/some distance from · [distance] from or north/south/east/west of X · a [time]-minute walk from · within walking distance of · [time] away by car/train`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use **near + noun**, but **close to + noun**. **Nearby** normally has no
complement or appears before a noun. **In close proximity to** is correct but
formal. **Pretty close** and **pretty far** are conversational AmE.

Examples:

- The hotel is near the station, and the café is close to the hotel.
- There is a pharmacy nearby, and a nearby convenience store stays open all night.
- The campus is fairly close to downtown, my apartment is pretty close to campus, the laboratory is relatively close to the hospital, and the station is not far from any of them.
- The clinic is in close proximity to the hospital, and the frog lives in fairly close proximity to water.
- The airport is far from downtown, the harbor is quite far from the station, the village is pretty far from the highway, the cabin is a long way from town, and the campsite is some distance from the river.
- The hotel is two miles from the airport.
- The clinic is one mile north of downtown, the depot is two miles south of it, the station is three miles east of it, and the airport is ten miles west of it.
- The office is a ten-minute walk from the subway, and several restaurants are within walking distance of it.
- The airport is 30 minutes away by car, while the city center is 20 minutes away by train.

## `on + street · at + exact address/intersection · on/at the corner of two streets · in the corner of a room · on + floor · at + entrance/exit/end · at the traffic light(s)/next light`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use **on** for a named street and **at** for an exact address or point. A
building can be **on** or **at the corner of** two streets. Use **in the
corner** for a corner inside an enclosed space.

Common landmarks include an entrance, exit, intersection, traffic light,
crosswalk, roundabout, bridge, fountain, playground, and parking lot.

Examples:

- The café is on Main Street, and the clinic is at 120 Main Street.
- Meet me at the intersection of Main Street and First Avenue.
- The bank is on the corner of Main and First; at the corner of Main and First is also natural.
- An ATM stands in the corner of the lobby, and the office is on the third floor.
- Meet me at the entrance, not at the rear exit; the bus stop is at the end of the street.
- Turn right at the traffic lights or, in common conversational AmE, at the next light.

## `go/head/walk/drive + compass direction · go/continue straight ahead · go up/down + street · walk/continue along + route · go as far as + landmark · continue until + clause · turn at/onto · take the first/second left/right · on your left/right · just before/after/past`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use **at** with a turning landmark and **onto** with the street you enter.
**As far as** and **until you reach** identify where the current instruction
ends. In directions, **up/down a street** does not always mean geographic
north/south.

Examples:

- Go north for two blocks, then head east at the park.
- Walk south through the plaza.
- Drive west toward the highway.
- Go straight ahead, then continue straight ahead through the intersection.
- Go up Bridge Street, then go down Queens Road.
- Walk along the river, then continue along the footpath.
- Go as far as the traffic lights and continue until you reach the bank.
- Turn left at the bank, then turn right at the next traffic light.
- Turn left onto Main Street, then turn right onto First Avenue.
- Take the first left, followed by the second right.
- The pharmacy will be on your left, and the station will be on your right.
- The entrance is just before the bridge, the parking lot is just after it, and the hotel is just past the next intersection.

## `to/toward/from · into/out of · onto/off · across/through · along/past · around · over/under`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use **to** for an intended destination and **toward** for direction without
presenting the place as the endpoint. The other pairs distinguish starting
points, boundaries, surfaces, and different paths. In AmE, **toward** is more
common; **towards** is also correct and is more common in British English.

Examples:

- The route runs from the hotel to the station, but you should first walk toward the clock tower.
- Go into the lobby and out of the rear exit.
- Step onto the platform, then get off the train at Central Station.
- Walk across the street and through the park.
- Continue along the river and past the bank.
- Go around the construction barrier, and then walk around the campus to explore its different areas.
- Cross over the footbridge, then pass under the railroad tracks.

## `upstairs/downstairs · go up/down the stairs · take the elevator to + floor · on the same floor · one floor above/below · down/along/across the hall · at the far end of the hall · through + door · around the corner · the first/second door on your left/right`

<!-- overview-card -->

Use **upstairs/downstairs** without **to**. Use **on** with a floor and
**above/below** to compare floors.

Examples:

- The conference room is upstairs, while the cafeteria is downstairs.
- Go up the stairs to reach reception, or go down the stairs to reach the parking garage.
- Take the elevator to the third floor; the accounting department is on the same floor.

- The restrooms are at the far end of the hall.
- Go through the glass door and around the corner.
- The office is the first door on your left; the meeting room is the second door on your right.

Use these expressions in spoken navigation instructions and GPS directions.

## `... + (that/who/whom/which/when/where/why) + subject + verb`

<!-- grammar-card -->

This card focuses on deleting exactly one optional word. Usually every other word stays in place. If a preposition comes immediately before the deleted word, move the preposition to the end first.

**1**

In these patterns, the joining word can disappear when another subject and verb follow:

`think/say/believe/know/find + that + subject + verb`
`→ remove that`

I believe that public transportation should be more affordable.
→ I believe public transportation should be more affordable.

`be sure/glad/afraid + that + subject + verb`
`→ remove that`

I am glad that the city has expanded the bus network.
→ I am glad the city has expanded the bus network.

`noun + that/who/whom/which + subject + verb`
`→ remove that/who/whom/which`

The report that the auditors released identified three control failures.
→ The report the auditors released identified three control failures.

The neighborhood that many families moved into has become more diverse.
→ The neighborhood many families moved into has become more diverse.

`day + when / place + where / reason + why + subject + verb`
`→ remove when/where/why`

I remember the day when we first met.
→ I remember the day we first met.

The place where we first met is now a community center.
→ The place we first met is now a community center.

The main reason why many families moved was the high cost of living.
→ The main reason many families moved was the high cost of living.

**2**

Do not remove only one word in these forms:

`that/who/which + verb directly`
`→ do not remove only that/who/which`

The report that identified three control failures was released yesterday.
≠ The report identified three control failures was released yesterday.

`, who/which ... ,`
`→ do not remove only who/which`

The new policy, which took effect in January, has reduced waiting times.
≠ The new policy, took effect in January, has reduced waiting times.

`whose + noun`
`→ do not remove whose`

Students whose families have low incomes may qualify for financial assistance.
≠ Students families have low incomes may qualify for financial assistance.

**3**

Do not leave a preposition directly before an empty space:

`preposition + whom/which + subject + verb`
`→ subject + verb + preposition`

The city in which I grew up has changed considerably.
→ The city I grew up in has changed considerably.

≠ The city in I grew up has changed considerably.

## `noun/linker + (subject + be) + V-ing/past participle/adjective/PP`

<!-- grammar-card -->

This card deletes a subject together with **am/is/are/was/were**. The remaining words must clearly describe another noun or subject.

**1**

`noun + who/that/which + am/is/are/was/were + description`
`→ noun + description`

The students who are studying abroad often become more independent.
→ The students studying abroad often become more independent.

The data that were collected in rural areas reveal a significant gap in health care.
→ The data collected in rural areas reveal a significant gap in health care.

The bridge that is being repaired connects two residential areas.
→ The bridge being repaired connects two residential areas.

Communities that are in remote areas often lack reliable public transportation.
→ Communities in remote areas often lack reliable public transportation.

The next issue that is to be discussed concerns affordable housing.
→ The next issue to be discussed concerns affordable housing.

`, who/which + am/is/are/was/were + description,`
`→ , description,`

The new library, which was funded by local donations, opened last month.
→ The new library, funded by local donations, opened last month.

**2**

`while/when/if/although/even though + subject + am/is/are/was/were + description`
`→ while/when/if/although/even though + description`

While she was a student, she worked part-time at a local clinic.
→ While a student, she worked part-time at a local clinic.

If it is necessary, the policy can be revised next year.
→ If necessary, the policy can be revised next year.

Although it was designed for small towns, the program was adopted nationwide.
→ Although designed for small towns, the program was adopted nationwide.

Even though she was nervous, Maya gave a clear presentation.
→ Even though nervous, Maya gave a clear presentation.

When the removed subject names a person or thing, it must refer to the subject of the main sentence. Impersonal phrases such as **if necessary** and **if possible** are also normal.

While Maya was studying, her phone rang.
≠ While studying, her phone rang.

The shortened sentence incorrectly makes **her phone** the understood subject of **studying**.

**3**

After a noun, the remaining words must fit naturally directly after that noun.

An adjective with extra information can often remain:

`eligible for financial aid`
`responsible for the project`
`available to local residents`

Applicants who are eligible for financial aid should apply online.
→ Applicants eligible for financial aid should apply online.

A noun normally cannot remain directly after another noun:

The volunteers who are doctors can provide medical care.
≠ The volunteers doctors can provide medical care.

A short adjective normally moves before the noun:

The measures that are expensive may be difficult to maintain.
≠ The measures expensive may be difficult to maintain.

The measures that are expensive may be difficult to maintain.
→ The expensive measures may be difficult to maintain.

A complete main sentence cannot lose its subject and **be**:

That policy is expensive.
≠ Expensive.

## `noun + V-ing / while/when + V-ing`

<!-- grammar-card -->

Use this change when the noun performs a current or general action and the exact tense is not important.

**1**

`noun + who/that/which + active verb`
`→ noun + V-ing`

Students who live abroad often become more independent.
→ Students living abroad often become more independent.

Policies that affect low-income families should be evaluated carefully.
→ Policies affecting low-income families should be evaluated carefully.

`, who/which + active verb,`
`→ , V-ing,`

The survey, which covers more than 2,000 households, provides useful data on housing costs.
→ The survey, covering more than 2,000 households, provides useful data on housing costs.

`noun + who/that + do/does not + verb`
`→ noun + not + V-ing`

Applicants who do not meet the income requirement will not qualify.
→ Applicants not meeting the income requirement will not qualify.

**2**

`while/when + subject + active verb`
`→ while/when + V-ing`

While they wait for the bus, they often check their phones.
→ While waiting for the bus, they often check their phones.

When you compare the two proposals, you should consider their long-term costs.
→ When comparing the two proposals, you should consider their long-term costs.

The person understood after **while** or **when** must also perform the main action.

**3**

Do not use ordinary **V-ing** when it would remove meaning carried by a modal:

`who + can/may/must/will/should + verb ≠ V-ing`

Employees who can work from home may choose a more affordable place to live.
≠ Employees working from home may choose a more affordable place to live.

**Can work** describes an ability or option. **Working** describes what the employees are actually doing.

Applicants who must provide additional evidence will receive an email.
≠ Applicants providing additional evidence will receive an email.

**Must provide** describes a requirement. **Providing** describes what the applicants are actually doing.

## `Having + past participle / Having been + ..., main clause`

<!-- grammar-card -->

**Having + ...** can shorten a whole clause about a situation earlier than the main situation. It does not mechanically replace every **has**, **have**, or **had** phrase.

**1**

For an earlier completed action:

`because/after/once + subject + has/have/had + past participle`
`→ having + past participle`

Because Maya has completed the required training, she is eligible to apply.
→ Having completed the required training, Maya is eligible to apply.

After the researchers had completed the safety inspection, they reopened the laboratory.
→ Having completed the safety inspection, the researchers reopened the laboratory.

**Has/have** uses the present as its reference point. **Had** uses a past reference point. **Having** has no tense of its own; the main sentence supplies the time.

When **after** or **once** already makes the order clear, the full clause may use the simple past:

`because/after/once + subject + completed simple-past action`
`→ having + past participle`

After the committee reviewed all the evidence, it rejected the proposal.
→ Having reviewed all the evidence, the committee rejected the proposal.

**2**

Keep negative, passive, and continuing meanings in the shortened form:

`has/have/had not + past participle`
`→ not having + past participle`

Because she had not received enough information, Maya postponed her decision.
→ Not having received enough information, Maya postponed her decision.

`has/have/had (not) been + past participle`
`→ (not) having been + past participle`

Because the proposal has been revised, it now addresses the main concerns.
→ Having been revised, the proposal now addresses the main concerns.

`has/have/had (not) been + V-ing`
`→ (not) having been + V-ing`

Because Maya had been working from home for several months, she found it difficult to return to the office.
→ Having been working from home for several months, Maya found it difficult to return to the office.

**Having completed** describes an earlier completed action. **Having been working** describes activity continuing over an earlier period and does not necessarily say that the activity was completed.

**3**

The subject of the main sentence must refer to the same person or thing as the subject removed from the earlier clause.

After the city had raised parking fees, many commuters began using public transportation.
≠ Having raised parking fees, many commuters began using public transportation.

The shortened sentence incorrectly says that **the commuters** raised the parking fees.

These forms are mainly useful in formal writing. In everyday conversation, a full **after**, **because**, or **once** clause is often more natural.

## `(for + person +) to + verb / wh-word + to + verb`

<!-- grammar-card -->

**To + verb** commonly describes an action that is needed, possible, planned, or performed for a purpose. It does not normally describe a completed fact.

**1**

Choose the form according to who will perform the action:

`action for an understood person → noun + to + verb`

I have three reports that I need to finish before Friday.
→ I have three reports to finish before Friday.

We need somewhere where we can stay for two nights.
→ We need somewhere to stay for two nights.

`action for a named person → noun + for + person + to + verb`

She prepared a short guide that new employees could use.
→ She prepared a short guide for new employees to use.

`noun receives the action → noun + to be + past participle`

The city published a list of buildings that inspectors will examine this year.
→ The city published a list of buildings to be examined this year.

The application lists several forms that must be completed.
→ The application lists several forms to be completed.

Use the passive form when the person performing the action is unimportant or already clear. **To be completed** presents the forms as a required task, but it does not state the force of **must** as explicitly. Keep **must** when the obligation itself is important.

**2**

For a purpose, keep the person who will perform the purpose action:

`same subject + so that + same subject + can/could + verb`
`→ same subject + to + verb`

Maya enrolled in an evening course so that she could improve her academic writing.
→ Maya enrolled in an evening course to improve her academic writing.

`so that + different subject + can/could + verb`
`→ for + subject + to + verb`

The city built a playground so that children could exercise safely.
→ The city built a playground for children to exercise safely.

The city built a playground so that children could exercise safely.
≠ The city built a playground to exercise safely.

The incorrect sentence suggests that **the city** will exercise.

**3**

`the first/next/last/only + noun + who/that + verb`
`→ the first/next/last/only + noun + to + verb`

Nadia was the first student who completed the survey.
→ Nadia was the first student to complete the survey.

Norway was the only country that met the target that year.
→ Norway was the only country to meet the target that year.

**4**

`who/what/where/when/how + subject + should/can + verb`
`→ who/what/where/when/how + to + verb`

`whether + subject + should + verb`
`→ whether + to + verb`

The person making the choice or performing the action must be clear from context.

I was not sure what I should include in the introduction.
→ I was not sure what to include in the introduction.

We have not decided where we should hold the meeting.
→ We have not decided where to hold the meeting.

The committee must determine how it can reduce unnecessary spending.
→ The committee must determine how to reduce unnecessary spending.

We have not decided whether we should apply for additional funding.
→ We have not decided whether to apply for additional funding.

Maya asked what Daniel should include in the report.
≠ Maya asked what to include in the report.

The shortened sentence would normally make **Maya**, not **Daniel**, the person choosing what to include.

Do not make the same change with **why**:

I do not understand why I should wait.
≠ I do not understand why to wait.

**5**

Do not use **to + verb** for a completed fact:

A policy that reduced emissions was expanded nationwide.
≠ A policy to reduce emissions was expanded nationwide.

The first sentence says that the policy actually reduced emissions. The second describes its purpose, not its actual result.

## `do/auxiliary/modal/to + (repeated action)`

<!-- grammar-card -->

When an action or description is already clear, keep only the small word that carries its tense, negative, ability, or other meaning.

**1**

Choose the small word that remains:

`ordinary verb → do/does/did`
`can/could/may/might/will/would/should/must + repeated action → keep the modal`
`has/have/had + repeated words → keep has/have/had`
`am/is/are/was/were + description/action → keep am/is/are/was/were`
`verb + to + repeated action → keep to`

Maya works remotely, and Daniel works remotely too.
→ Maya works remotely, and Daniel does too.
→ Maya works remotely, and so does Daniel.

Maya cannot attend the workshop, and Daniel cannot attend it either.
→ Maya cannot attend the workshop, and Daniel cannot either.
→ Maya cannot attend the workshop, and neither can Daniel.

Maya has submitted her application, and Daniel has submitted his application too.
→ Maya has submitted her application, and Daniel has too.

The downtown clinic is overcrowded, and the suburban clinic is overcrowded too.
→ The downtown clinic is overcrowded, and the suburban clinic is too.

I did not attend the workshop, although I had planned to attend it.
→ I did not attend the workshop, although I had planned to.

In American English, **have** meaning possession normally uses **do**:

`have/has + possession → do/does`

Maya has flexible working hours, and Daniel has flexible working hours too.
→ Maya has flexible working hours, and Daniel does too.

**2**

The same omission works in comparisons and concise coordination:

`than + subject + small word`

Housing costs have risen faster in the city than they have risen in nearby towns.
→ Housing costs have risen faster in the city than they have in nearby towns.

`subject + verb + information, and subject + different information`

The city invested two million dollars in buses, and the county invested one million dollars in bike lanes.
→ The city invested two million dollars in buses, and the county one million dollars in bike lanes.

The second structure is concise and slightly formal. Repeating the verb is usually more natural in everyday conversation.

**3**

Do not omit the action when the result could be misunderstood:

The manager asked Maya to revise the report, and Daniel did too.

This could be misread as Daniel also asking Maya or Daniel revising the report. Repeat the intended action when the shorter sentence is unclear.

## `known event + who/what/where/when/why/how`

<!-- grammar-card -->

When a full event is already known, English can leave only the information that is still missing or newly supplied.

**1**

`known event + who/what/where/when/why/how + repeated clause`
`→ keep only who/what/where/when/why/how`

Someone approved the proposal, but I do not know who approved it.
→ Someone approved the proposal, but I do not know who.

Maya bought something for the office, but she did not say what she bought.
→ Maya bought something for the office, but she did not say what.

They held the meeting somewhere off campus, but I do not know where they held it.
→ They held the meeting somewhere off campus, but I do not know where.

The policy will take effect sometime this year, but we have not been told when it will take effect.
→ The policy will take effect sometime this year, but we have not been told when.

The application was rejected for some reason, but no one explained why it was rejected.
→ The application was rejected for some reason, but no one explained why.

The repairs will cost a certain amount, but we do not know how much they will cost.
→ The repairs will cost a certain amount, but we do not know how much.

**2**

A direct answer can contain only the new information:

Who submitted the final report?
Maya submitted the final report.
→ Maya.

Where will the meeting take place?
The meeting will take place in the main conference room.
→ In the main conference room.

Did the council approve the proposal?
Yes, the council approved the proposal.
→ Yes.

**3**

Keep enough words when the remaining question word could refer to more than one event:

Maya told Daniel that the meeting had been canceled, but I do not know when.

This could mean when Maya told Daniel or when the meeting was canceled.

→ I do not know when Maya told Daniel.
→ I do not know when the meeting was canceled.

## `number/quantity + (repeated noun) / adjective + one/ones`

<!-- grammar-card -->

When a noun is already clear, a number or quantity word can often stand without repeating that noun.

**1**

`number + repeated count noun → number`
`some/any/many/few/a few/several/more/most/fewer/enough/all/both/each/either/neither + repeated count noun → quantity word`
`some/any/much/little/a little/more/most/less/enough/all + repeated mass noun → quantity word`
`none of + the + repeated noun → none`

We needed five volunteers, but only three volunteers arrived.
→ We needed five volunteers, but only three arrived.

Several proposals were submitted, but only a few proposals met the requirements.
→ Several proposals were submitted, but only a few met the requirements.

The city needs more affordable housing, but it does not have enough affordable housing.
→ The city needs more affordable housing, but it does not have enough.

Both solutions were expensive, but neither solution was practical.
→ Both solutions were expensive, but neither was practical.

The committee reviewed five proposals, but none of the proposals met the safety standard.
→ The committee reviewed five proposals, but none met the safety standard.

**2**

When an adjective remains, use **one** or **ones** instead of leaving the adjective alone:

`adjective + repeated singular count noun → adjective + one`
`adjective + repeated plural count noun → adjective + ones`

The larger apartment is expensive, so we chose the smaller apartment.
→ The larger apartment is expensive, so we chose the smaller one.

The city replaced the older buses but kept the newer buses.
→ The city replaced the older buses but kept the newer ones.

**3**

Repeat the noun when the remaining number or quantity would be unclear:

I ordered two reports and three application forms, but they sent only one.

**One** could mean one report or one application form.

→ They sent only one report.
→ They sent only one application form.

## `(predictable opening words) + casual statement/question`

<!-- grammar-card -->

In casual American conversation, texts, and notes, predictable words at the beginning can disappear when the meaning remains obvious.

**1**

Common informal statements:

`I hope ... → Hope ...`
`it/that sounds/looks/seems ... → Sounds/Looks/Seems ...`
`I am sorry ... → Sorry ...`
`I am + V-ing → V-ing`
`there is no need ... → No need ...`
`it is + adjective + to ... → Adjective + to ...`

I hope you have a safe trip.
→ Hope you have a safe trip.

That sounds good to me.
→ Sounds good to me.

I am sorry I missed your call.
→ Sorry I missed your call.

I am heading home now.
→ Heading home now.

There is no need to bring anything.
→ No need to bring anything.

It is good to see you again.
→ Good to see you again.

**2**

Common informal questions:

`do you want/need ...? → Want/Need ...?`
`did you + verb ...? → verb ...?`
`are you + V-ing ...? → You + V-ing ...? / V-ing ...?`
`have you + past participle ...? → Past participle ...?`
`is everything ...? → Everything ...?`

Do you want some coffee?
→ Want some coffee?

Did you get home okay?
→ Get home okay?

Are you coming with us?
→ You coming with us?
→ Coming with us?

Have you finished the report?
→ Finished the report?

Is everything okay?
→ Everything okay?

**3**

Use these shortened openings only when the missing subject and meaning are obvious.

Heading home now. See you soon.

This is natural in a text message. In formal or academic writing, write the complete sentence:

I am heading home now.

Avoid a shortened opening when it could hide important information:

Need more funding.

This does not clearly identify who needs the funding. In formal writing, name the subject:

The public transportation system needs more funding.

## `It is + focused person/thing + that/who + clause · What + subject + did + was + (to) + verb`

Highlight the person, thing, or action that answers an implicit contrast. A cleft changes focus rather than necessarily changing the underlying event. Do not use it to make every sentence sound emphatic.

Examples:

- It was the missing permission that caused the failure, not the file size.
- It was Maya who noticed the discrepancy.
- What we did was compare the two logs line by line. / What we did was to compare the two logs line by line.

## `whether X or Y · whether + clause + or not`

Use whether to present alternatives or an unresolved yes-or-no question. A whether-clause after a verb does not invert its subject and verb. Whether or not can also introduce a condition-independent outcome, but a question such as I do not know whether ... leaves the answer unresolved.

- We need to decide whether to repair the laptop or replace it.
- I do not know whether she will attend or not.
- The answer depends on whether we can finish the tests.

## `as if/as though + clause`

Describe an appearance or manner through a comparison. As if and as though usually overlap. An ordinary present form can describe a plausible impression; a past form can signal an imagined or unreal comparison. The construction does not guarantee that the comparison is true.

Examples:

- He talks as if he knows everyone. / He talks as though he knows everyone.
- She treated me as if I were a complete stranger.
- It looks as if the rain has stopped.

## `like + noun · as + role/function noun · as + subject + verb`

Use like for similarity and as + noun for an actual role or function. As + clause can mean in the way that; conversational like + clause also occurs. As I said and like I said can both refer back to your earlier words, with like more conversational.

Examples:

- I work as a mentor. / I work like a mentor. — An actual role versus similar behavior.
- We used a box as a table; it looked like a shipping crate.
- Leave the settings as they are. / As I said earlier, the default works for most users.

## `start/begin + to + verb / V-ing`

Start and begin often allow either to + verb or an -ing form with little practical difference. With verbs of understanding and knowing, a to-infinitive is usually the natural choice. After a progressive starting/beginning form, a to-infinitive often avoids an awkward sequence of -ing forms.

- I started checking the figures. / I started to check the figures.
- I am starting to notice recurring problems.
- I am beginning to understand why the review takes so long.

## `X matters more to + person + than Y · choose X over Y`

Compare priorities or state a choice. Matters more describes relative importance; choose over identifies the preferred alternative. Neither requires claiming the other option is bad.

Examples:

- Flexibility matters more to me than a prestigious job title.
- I would choose a shorter commute over a slightly higher salary.

## `would rather + person + past form · would rather + person + had + past participle`

State a preference about another person’s action. Use a past form for a present or future preference; use past perfect for a preferred past that did not happen. When you perform the action yourself, use would rather + base verb.

Examples:

- I would rather you sent the draft tomorrow, after you have checked it.
- I would rather you did not share my number.
- I would rather you had told me before inviting everyone.

## `When/Why/How + do you think + subject + verb?`

Ask for someone’s judgment about a question. Invert do you think, but keep statement word order in the following clause. This differs from a direct question such as When can we start?

Examples:

- When do you think we can finish the review?
- Why do you think the client rejected the proposal?
- How do you think we should explain the delay?

## `for + duration · during + noun · while + clause · before/after + noun/clause/V-ing`

For measures duration; during names the period in which something happens; while introduces a simultaneous situation. Before/after can take a noun, a clause, or an -ing action. With an understood -ing subject, keep the actor clear. For and since in continuing situations are covered by the perfect-duration frame.

Examples:

- I waited for twenty minutes during the lunch break.
- I took notes while she explained the process.
- I checked the address before the trip. / I checked the address before I left. / I checked the address before leaving.

## `as/while + clause · just as + clause`

Describe overlapping events. As can show simultaneous actions or change; just as emphasizes the exact moment. While often highlights the duration. Because as can also mean because, use while or when if the intended time relationship would otherwise be unclear.

Examples:

- As we walked to the station, we discussed the proposal. / While we walked to the station, we discussed the proposal.
- Just as I opened the document, my laptop shut down.

## `on time · in time for + noun / to + verb`

On time means at the scheduled or expected time. In time means early enough for a purpose or event; just in time means almost too late. One arrival can satisfy both descriptions.

Examples:

- The train arrived on time, exactly as scheduled.
- We arrived in time for the opening talk. / We arrived in time to hear the opening talk.

## `verb + particle + noun / verb + noun + particle · verb + pronoun + particle · verb + preposition + object`

Separable phrasal verbs allow the noun object in either position, but their ordinary pronoun object goes between the verb and particle. Prepositional verbs keep their object after the preposition. Which type a verb belongs to is lexical knowledge; not every short word after a verb is a movable particle.

Examples:

- Please turn off the alarm. / Please turn the alarm off. / Please turn it off.
- We need to look into the issue. / We need to look into it.
- I picked up the parcel and brought it back.

## `with + object + V-ing/past participle/adjective/phrase`

Add background circumstances with a named subject. Use -ing when that subject performs an ongoing action, a past participle when it receives an action or is in a resulting state, and an adjective or phrase for a condition. The background subject can differ from the main subject.

Examples:

- With everyone working remotely, we needed a clearer handoff process.
- With the budget approved, we could book the venue.
- With the office empty and the lights off, the building felt unfamiliar.

## `result in + outcome / result from + cause`

Use **result in** before the effect produced and **result from** before the cause that produced it.

- Poor maintenance can result in costly repairs.
- Many workplace conflicts result from unclear expectations.
- The new scheduling system resulted in shorter waiting times.

## `come to a conclusion/decision/agreement`

Describe reaching a judgment or shared outcome after thought or discussion. The nouns keep their own meanings: conclusion concerns what you believe, decision what you choose, and agreement a shared position or arrangement.

Examples:

- After checking the logs, we came to the conclusion that the timeout was unrelated.
- The team came to a decision about which design to adopt.
- We came to an agreement on how to divide the work.

## `come to a halt/stop · come to an end`

Describe movement or an activity ending. Halt and stop often overlap for movement; halt can also describe a process stopping. Come to an end suits an event or period reaching its endpoint.

Examples:

- Traffic came to a halt when the tunnel closed. / Traffic came to a stop when the tunnel closed.
- The partnership came to an end after its funding was withdrawn.

## `come to realize/understand/believe (that) + clause`

Describe developing an understanding or belief over time or through experience. Here to introduces a verb, not a noun. The new understanding may arrive gradually or after a decisive experience.

Examples:

- I gradually came to realize that clear feedback prevents larger problems.
- After working with the team, I came to understand why the review takes so long.
- I came to believe that a smaller first release would be more useful.

## `have an impact/effect on + target · contribute to + outcome · play a role in + process · be associated with + noun`

<!-- overview-card -->

Distinguish influence, contribution, participation, and association. Impact/effect identifies something affected. Contribute to presents a partial cause or contribution. Play a role identifies involvement or influence. Associated with identifies a connection without by itself claiming causation. These frames can overlap; the evidence determines what you can claim.

Examples:

- The schedule change had an effect on attendance.
- Unclear instructions contributed to the delay.
- Training plays a role in improving consistency.
- Higher satisfaction was associated with greater flexibility; that alone does not prove a causal relationship.

## `have access to + noun`

Say someone can reach, obtain, or use something. Access does not itself mean ownership, affordability, or permission to share it further.

Examples:

- Employees have access to the recordings after each session.
- Do you have access to the original report?

## `can/cannot afford + noun/to + verb`

Describe whether money, time, or another cost is manageable. Cannot afford to can express that the consequences would be unacceptable, even when money is not involved.

Examples:

- I cannot afford a larger apartment.
- We cannot afford to delay the launch again.

## `work well for + person/purpose`

Say something suits a person or purpose. The expression concerns suitability, not whether the person works well. A solution can work well for one person and poorly for another.

Examples:

- The flexible schedule works well for parents with young children.
- This approach works well for small teams, but it becomes difficult to coordinate at scale.

## `turn into + result · turn + object + into + result`

Describe transformation. Turn into makes the changing thing the subject; turn something into names the agent causing the change. The result is what the thing becomes.

Examples:

- The conversation turned into a debate.
- We turned the empty room into a workspace.

## `shift toward/away from + noun/V-ing`

Describe a change in direction, attention, or emphasis without implying a complete transformation. Toward names the emerging focus; away from names what receives less attention.

Examples:

- My role is shifting toward planning and mentoring.
- The discussion shifted away from assigning blame and toward finding a solution.

## `stand clear of + hazard · stay clear of + person/thing/problem`

Stand clear tells someone to move or stay out of a hazard’s immediate path, especially in announcements. Stay clear can refer to physical avoidance or avoiding involvement. They overlap around physical hazards; stay clear also extends naturally to situations such as gossip.

Examples:

- Please stand clear of the closing doors.
- Stay clear of the edge of the platform.
- I try to stay clear of office gossip.

## `get + benefit/amount + out of + experience`

Use this to describe the benefit, enjoyment, or value gained from an activity or experience. Get the most out of emphasizes gaining as much as possible.

- I got a lot out of the design review.
- What do you hope to get out of this course?
- I want to get as much out of the trip as I can.

## `Me too · Me neither · So do I/So am I · Neither do I/Neither am I`

Say that the same thing is true for you. Use me too after a positive statement and me neither after a negative one; these are the most common casual replies. So/Neither + helper + I is slightly fuller and repeats the helper from the other person’s sentence: do for ordinary present verbs, am for be, did for the past, and have or can when they used those helpers. In casual AmE, me either is also common after a negative.

Examples:

- A: I’m exhausted after that flight. B: Me too. / So am I.
- A: I don’t really enjoy networking events. B: Me neither. / Neither do I.
- A: I’ve never been to Chicago. B: Me neither. / Neither have I.

## `How about you? · What about you? · And you?`

Pass the turn back after you answer a question so the conversation keeps going. How about you and what about you are interchangeable here. And you? is shorter and fits simple exchanges such as greetings.

Examples:

- I’m from Taipei, but I’ve been living in Seattle for two years. How about you?
- I usually work from home on Fridays. What about you?
- A: How’s it going? B: Pretty good, thanks. And you?

## `Right. · I see. · That makes sense. · Fair enough. · Exactly. · Totally.`

Show that you are following or agreeing while someone explains something. Right and I see signal that you understand, not necessarily that you agree. That makes sense says the explanation sounds logical to you. Fair enough accepts someone’s reason even if you might have chosen differently. Exactly and totally show strong agreement; totally is casual.

Examples:

- A: So the cache only refreshes every ten minutes. B: Right. That explains the outdated numbers.
- A: We’re waiting for legal approval before the launch. B: I see. That makes sense. / Fair enough. Let’s not rush it.
- A: The real issue is that nobody owns the onboarding process. B: Exactly. / Totally.

## `Really? · Seriously? · No way! · You’re kidding! · Oh, nice! · Oh no!`

React briefly to someone’s news to show interest and invite them to continue. Really? shows interest or mild surprise. Seriously?, No way!, and You’re kidding! show stronger surprise or disbelief; You’re kidding! often reacts to something annoying or hard to believe. Oh, nice! responds to good news, and Oh no! responds to bad news. Your tone matters: a flat Really? can sound doubtful.

Examples:

- A: I finally got an offer from that startup. B: No way! That’s amazing. / Oh, nice! When do you start?
- A: They moved the launch up by two weeks. B: Seriously? Do we even have time to test it? / You’re kidding! Do we even have time to test it?
- A: My connecting flight got canceled. B: Oh no! Were you able to rebook it? / Really? What happened?

## `Congrats on + noun/V-ing · That’s great news! · Good for you!`

Respond warmly to someone’s good news or achievement. Congrats is the casual form of congratulations; both take on + noun/V-ing. That’s great news! reacts to the information itself. Good for you! praises the person’s own effort or decision; said in a flat tone, it can sound sarcastic.

Examples:

- Congrats on the promotion! / Congratulations on passing your certification exam!
- A: We’re expecting our first baby in March. B: That’s great news!
- A: I finally signed up for a half marathon. B: Good for you!

## `Well, + reply · So, + new topic/question/summary`

Start your turn in a way that signals its purpose. Well begins a reply that needs a moment of thought, a qualification, or a soft disagreement. So opens a new topic, introduces a question, or sums up what has been decided.

Examples:

- A: Did you like the redesigned app? B: Well, it looks better, but it feels slower.
- So, how was your first week at the new job?
- So, to recap: Maria will handle the slides, and I’ll draft the summary.

## `I mean, + clarification/correction · ..., you know?`

Use small spoken markers to adjust what you just said. I mean adds a clarification, a qualification, or a quick correction. You know? at the end checks that the listener shares your point. Use them occasionally; adding them to every sentence makes you sound hesitant. For a deliberate repair of a misunderstanding, use a fuller frame such as What I mean is.

Examples:

- The movie was good. I mean, it wasn’t amazing, but I enjoyed it.
- Let’s meet on Tuesday. I mean Wednesday; I have a dentist appointment on Tuesday.
- It’s hard to exercise regularly when you work such long hours, you know?

## `kind of/sort of + adjective/verb · a bit/a little + adjective · a bit of a + noun`

Soften a description or opinion so it sounds less direct. Kind of and sort of are interchangeable and casual; they can come before an adjective or a verb. A bit and a little often soften a negative or uncomfortable adjective; a bit is especially common in conversation. A bit of a + noun softens a negative noun such as problem, mess, or challenge.

Examples:

- The new office is kind of far from the train station. / The new office is sort of far from the train station.
- I sort of expected the meeting to run long, so I didn’t book anything afterward.
- The instructions were a bit confusing. / The instructions were a little confusing. / The new process has been a bit of a headache.

## `..., or something · ..., and stuff (like that) · ..., or whatever`

Keep a description general when the exact details do not matter. Or something means or something similar; and stuff (like that) means and similar things. Or whatever also leaves the choice open but can sound dismissive, as if the details are unimportant. All three are casual; avoid them in interviews and formal writing.

Examples:

- Let’s grab coffee or something after the workshop.
- I spent the weekend cleaning, doing laundry, and stuff like that.
- Bring chips, drinks, or whatever; we’ll have plenty of food anyway.

## `I was like, "..." · subject + was like, "..." · I’m like / he’s like, "..."`

Report what someone said, thought, or how they reacted in a casual story. Be like does not promise the exact words; it often captures an attitude. Storytellers commonly use the past form, but they also switch to the present to make a story vivid. Use said in professional contexts or when the exact words matter.

Examples:

- My manager asked me to present in five minutes, and I was like, “Wait, today?”
- She looked at the bill and was like, “Did we really order all this?”
- So I’m explaining the bug, and he’s like, “Oh, I already fixed that.”

## `There’s no way + clause · There’s no way to + verb`

Say that something is impossible or that you strongly reject it. There’s no way + clause expresses strong disbelief or refusal. There’s no way to + verb says that no method exists. Ask whether a method exists with Is there any way to + verb?

Examples:

- There’s no way we can finish all of this by Friday.
- There’s no way I’m paying forty dollars for parking.
- A: Is there any way to recover the deleted file? B: Unfortunately, there’s no way to recover it now.

## `It’s up to + person (to + verb) · I’ll leave it up to you`

Say who has the right or responsibility to decide. It’s up to you hands a decision to the listener. It’s up to + person + to + verb names who has to act. I’ll leave it up to you is a polite way to let someone else choose.

Examples:

- A: Should we eat in or go out? B: It’s up to you. I’m fine either way.
- It’s up to each team to decide how often they meet.
- I don’t have a strong preference, so I’ll leave it up to you.

## `When it comes to + noun/V-ing, + clause · As for + noun, + clause · As far as + noun + is concerned, + clause`

Introduce the topic your next statement is about. When it comes to is very common in speech and sets up a general statement about a topic. As for moves to the next person or item, often after you have discussed another one. As far as + noun + is concerned limits the statement to that topic and sounds neutral to slightly formal; as far as I am concerned instead means in my opinion. In terms of is different: it narrows an evaluation or comparison to one aspect rather than introducing a topic.

Examples:

- When it comes to remote work, clear communication matters more than the tools you use.
- The design is finished. As for the backend, we’re still waiting on the security review.
- As far as cost is concerned, both options are about the same.

## `regarding/concerning/with regard to + noun · in the case of + noun`

Name the topic in professional or written English. Regarding and concerning mean about and often follow nouns such as questions, information, or concerns. With regard to is more formal and often begins a sentence. In casual conversation, about is usually more natural. In the case of introduces the particular case a statement applies to, often in contrast with other cases.

Examples:

- Do you have any questions regarding the new schedule? / Do you have any questions concerning the new schedule?
- With regard to your refund request, we have forwarded it to our billing team.
- In the case of small teams, a lightweight review process usually works better.

## `By the way, + clause · Oh, before I forget, + clause · That reminds me, + clause`

Bring up something new or unrelated without a smooth transition. By the way adds a side point. Before I forget flags something you might otherwise forget to mention. That reminds me says that something just said made you think of it. Unlike speaking of, these do not need a related topic.

Examples:

- By the way, did you get a chance to look at my pull request?
- Oh, before I forget, the client moved the call to Thursday.
- A: I’m flying to Denver next week. B: That reminds me, I still need to book my flight home for Thanksgiving.

## `It turns out (that) + clause · X turned out to be + adjective/noun · end up + V-ing / with + noun / in + place`

Talk about how things developed. Turn out reports a fact or result that you discovered, often different from what you expected. End up describes where a process finally led, often without a plan. Both are common in stories and status updates.

Examples:

- It turns out the bug was in the configuration file, not the code. / The new hire turned out to be a great fit.
- We ended up rewriting the whole module.
- I meant to stay for one year, but I ended up in Seattle for five. / We ended up with more volunteers than we needed.

## `Not really · I’m not really into/not a big fan of + noun/V-ing · I’m more of a + noun + person · not … at all`

Answer with the right strength. Not really is a soft no. Not really into and not a big fan of express mild dislike politely; I’m more of a ___ person names the preference you have instead. Not ... at all is strong: it emphasizes zero.

Examples:

- A: Do you play video games? B: Not really, but my brother does.
- I’m not a big fan of open-plan offices. / I’m not really into team sports; I’m more of a hiking person.
- I didn’t understand the second half of the lecture at all.

## `You did? / Do you? / Have you? · You did what? · Like what? · How so? · What makes you say that?`

Show interest and invite the speaker to say more. A short response question repeats the helper from the other person’s sentence with a rising tone: did for a past verb, do for a present verb, and have for a perfect. In an echo question such as You did what?, a question word replaces the surprising part. Like what? asks for examples; How so? and What makes you say that? ask for reasons.

Examples:

- A: I grew up in Alaska. B: You did? What was that like? / A: I’ve never seen snow. B: You haven’t?
- A: I quit my job yesterday. B: You did what?
- A: The interview was kind of weird. B: Like what? / How so? / What makes you say that?

## `No problem · No worries · Don’t worry about it · Anytime · Never mind`

Brush off a small problem, thanks, or an apology. No problem and no worries reply to thanks or a small apology; no worries is casual. Don’t worry about it reassures someone who apologizes or offers to fix something. Anytime replies to thanks and says you are happy to help again. Never mind cancels your own question or request, or says that a small issue no longer matters.

Examples:

- A: Thanks for covering my shift. B: No problem. / Anytime.
- A: Sorry I’m late. B: Don’t worry about it; we just started. / No worries.
- Where did you put the… never mind, I found it.

## `Don’t you think + clause? · Isn’t it + adjective? · Wouldn’t it be better to + verb?`

Ask a negative question when you expect or want the listener to agree. It sounds more involved than a plain statement and can soften a suggestion. Answer about the facts, not the negative form of the question: yes means the positive statement is true.

Examples:

- Don’t you think we should test it before the demo?
- Wouldn’t it be better to ask the client first?
- A: Isn’t it cold in here? B: Yes, it is. / No, it’s fine. — Yes means it is cold.

## `That must be + adjective · That sounds + adjective · No wonder + clause`

React to someone’s situation by drawing a reasonable conclusion. That must be + adjective imagines how the situation feels. That sounds + adjective reacts to what you have just heard. No wonder + clause says that a fact explains something you noticed.

Examples:

- A: I’m working night shifts this month. B: That must be exhausting.
- A: We’re moving to Lisbon in the spring. B: That sounds exciting!
- A: I only slept four hours last night. B: No wonder you look tired.

## `At least + clause`

Point out the positive side of a disappointing situation. At least usually starts the clause or follows but. With a number, at least instead sets a minimum.

Examples:

- The flight was delayed, but at least we got free snacks.
- We lost the bid. At least we learned what the client really wants.
- A: It rained the whole weekend. B: Well, at least you got some rest.

## `You know what? + comment/decision · Guess what? + news · Did you hear about + noun?`

Get attention before saying something. You know what? introduces a comment or a sudden decision. Guess what? announces news, usually good or surprising. Did you hear about introduces news about someone or something the listener may know.

Examples:

- You know what? Let’s just order pizza tonight.
- Guess what? I passed my driving test.
- Did you hear about the layoffs at Dana’s company?

## `So far, + present perfect · So far, so good`

Report progress up to now. So far normally takes the present perfect because the period continues. So far, so good says that everything has gone well up to this point, but the process is not finished.

Examples:

- So far, we’ve migrated about half of the services.
- I haven’t had any problems with the new laptop so far.
- A: How’s the new job? B: So far, so good.

## `Keep in mind (that) + clause · keep + noun + in mind`

Remind someone of a fact that should affect a decision or plan. Keep in mind is more common than bear in mind in American English. With a short noun, put it between keep and in mind.

Examples:

- Keep in mind that the API has a rate limit.
- When you choose a neighborhood, keep the commute in mind.
- Keep in mind that hotel prices go up in the summer.

## `go ahead and + verb · Go ahead.`

Say that you are about to do something, or tell someone they can start. I’ll go ahead and + verb announces an action you are taking without more discussion. Go ahead and + verb tells someone to proceed; Go ahead alone gives permission.

Examples:

- I’ll go ahead and send the invite to everyone.
- Go ahead and start without me; I’m running ten minutes late.
- A: Can I borrow your charger? B: Sure, go ahead.

## `I doubt (that) + clause · I doubt it · I’m not so sure about that · I don’t think that’s right`

Respond with skepticism. I doubt + clause says you think something is probably not true or will probably not happen; because doubt already carries the negative meaning, the clause is usually positive. I doubt it is the short reply. I’m not so sure about that politely questions someone’s claim; I don’t think that’s right is more direct and suits a factual mistake.

Examples:

- I doubt they’ll finish the redesign by Friday. / A: Will it stop raining soon? B: I doubt it.
- A: Remote workers are less productive. B: I’m not so sure about that.
- I don’t think that’s right. The logs show that the cache was already warm.

## `after all`

After all has two common uses. At the end of a clause, it means despite what was expected or planned. At the start of a clause, it gives a reason that the listener should remember.

Examples:

- We didn’t need the reservation after all; the restaurant was half empty.
- I decided to take the job after all.
- Let him choose the restaurant. After all, it’s his birthday.

## `I’m afraid + clause · I’m sorry, but + clause · I don’t want to worry you, but + clause`

Soften unwelcome news, a refusal, or a disagreement. I’m afraid here means I am sorry to say, not that you feel fear. I’m sorry, but is direct and polite. I don’t want to worry you, but prepares the listener for a possible problem.

Examples:

- I’m afraid I can’t make it on Friday.
- I’m sorry, but this ticket isn’t valid on the express train.
- I don’t want to worry you, but the client hasn’t replied since Friday.

## `these days + present · lately/recently + present perfect · the other day + past · at the time + past`

Choose the time phrase and the tense together. These days describes the current situation, often in contrast with the past. Lately and recently often take the present perfect for a recent period; recently can also take the simple past for one recent event. The other day means a few days ago. At the time means then, when the event happened, often when your view has changed since.

Examples:

- These days, most of our meetings are remote.
- I’ve been sleeping badly lately. / I recently started a new job.
- I ran into Jake the other day. / At the time, I didn’t realize how important that decision was.

## `At this point, + clause · In the meantime, + clause · at some point`

Place an action within a process. At this point means at the current stage, often to say what is now possible or sensible. In the meantime means during the period before something else happens. At some point means at a time that is not yet known.

Examples:

- At this point, rolling back is probably the safest option.
- The parts won’t arrive until Monday. In the meantime, we can test the software.
- We’ll need to replace the server at some point.

## `In a way/In a sense, + clause · If you think about it, + clause`

Present a claim as partly true or as one way of looking at things. In a way and in a sense limit the claim. If you think about it invites the listener to notice a point they may not have considered.

Examples:

- In a way, the outage helped; it exposed gaps in our monitoring. / In a sense, the outage helped.
- If you think about it, most of our delays come from waiting for approvals.
- Working from home saves money in a way, but my electricity bill has gone up.

## `Then again, + clause · On the contrary, + clause`

Then again adds a second thought that balances or weakens what you just said. On the contrary rejects the previous statement and asserts the opposite; it is not a general contrast marker. When both sides are true, use on the other hand instead.

Examples:

- The apartment is expensive. Then again, it’s right next to the station.
- A: Was the class too easy? B: On the contrary, it was really demanding.
- Living downtown is convenient. On the other hand, it’s noisy. — Both statements are true, so on the contrary does not fit.

## `In theory, + clause; in practice, + clause`

Contrast what should happen according to a plan, rule, or design with what really happens.

Examples:

- In theory, the cache should fix the problem; in practice, it only speeds up reads.
- In theory, everyone has flexible hours. In practice, most people work nine to six.

## `Other than that, + clause · other than + noun`

Make an exception. Other than that follows a mentioned exception and says that everything else is fine or unchanged. Other than + noun means except.

Examples:

- The hotel was a little noisy. Other than that, it was perfect.
- Other than a few typos, the report looks good.
- I don’t know anyone there other than Maya.

## `The best/worst part was (when) + clause · The good thing about + noun + is (that) + clause · One of the things I like about + noun + is (that) + clause`

Highlight one part of an experience or one feature of something. The best/worst part was describes a moment. The good thing about names an advantage. One of the things I like about presents one of several reasons; the verb agrees with one, so use is.

Examples:

- The best part was when the whole crowd started singing.
- The good thing about this neighborhood is that everything is within walking distance.
- One of the things I like about remote work is that I can focus without interruptions.

## `What a/an (+ adjective) + noun! · How + adjective! · absolutely/totally + extreme adjective · couldn’t be + comparative`

Express a strong reaction. What a + noun and How + adjective are exclamations. With extreme adjectives such as exhausted, amazing, or freezing, absolutely or totally is the natural intensifier; very usually goes with ordinary adjectives such as tired. Couldn’t be better/happier means as good or as happy as possible.

Examples:

- What a great idea! / How nice of you to drive us!
- I was absolutely exhausted after the flight. / I was very tired after the flight.
- A: How’s the new job? B: Honestly, it couldn’t be better.

## `I wouldn’t say + X, just + Y · not exactly + adjective/noun`

Tone down a judgment. I wouldn’t say X, just Y rejects a strong word and offers a milder, more accurate one. Not exactly is ironic understatement: it politely or humorously means not at all.

Examples:

- I wouldn’t say she’s unfriendly, just a little shy.
- I wouldn’t say the trip was a disaster, just a bit disorganized.
- Three meetings back to back isn’t exactly my idea of a productive day.

## `Some people (might) argue that + clause, but + clause · It could be argued that + clause`

Introduce a view you do not fully share before you respond to it. Some people argue presents an opposing view in conversation and essays. It could be argued is more formal and impersonal.

Examples:

- Some people might argue that remote work hurts collaboration, but our data shows the opposite.
- Some people argue that tourism damages small towns, but it also creates jobs.
- It could be argued that the policy came too late to make a difference.

## `…, let alone + parallel item`

Say that if the first, easier thing is not true, the second, harder thing is even less true. Use it after a negative or limiting statement, and keep the two items parallel.

Examples:

- I barely have time for lunch, let alone the gym.
- He can’t boil an egg, let alone cook a full dinner.
- Most people haven’t heard of the company, let alone used its products.

## `Let’s move on to + topic · That’s a separate issue · Let’s not get into + topic · Let’s not go there`

Steer a conversation or meeting. Let’s move on to changes to the next item. That’s a separate issue says a point is not relevant right now. Let’s not get into + topic and the casual Let’s not go there avoid a sensitive or time-consuming subject.

Examples:

- We’re running short on time, so let’s move on to the budget.
- That’s a separate issue; let’s discuss it after the meeting.
- Let’s not get into politics at dinner. / A: What about your ex? B: Let’s not go there.

## `Sorry to bother you, but + clause · Do you have a minute? · Can I have a word (with you)?`

Approach someone who may be busy. Sorry to bother you, but apologizes for the interruption before a request. Do you have a minute? checks whether someone is available; Got a sec? is casual. Can I have a word (with you)? asks for a short private talk and can sound serious.

Examples:

- Sorry to bother you, but do you know where the printer paper is?
- Do you have a minute? I have a question about the schedule.
- Can I have a word with you after the meeting?

## `Can I speak to + person? · Can I leave a message? · Could you tell + person + I called? · This is + name + returning your call`

Handle a phone call. Can I speak to asks for the person you want. On the phone, identify yourself with This is + name, not I am + name. Can I leave a message? and Could you tell + person + I called? are useful when the person is unavailable. Returning your call says that you are calling back.

Examples:

- Hi, can I speak to Ms. Rivera, please? / This is Daniel Chen returning your call from this morning.
- A: She’s in a meeting right now. B: Can I leave a message?
- Could you tell him I called? My number is 555-0142.

## `feel the same way (about + noun / as + person)`

Say that you share another person’s opinion or emotional reaction. About introduces the topic; as introduces the person or comparison clause. A comparison clause can also follow directly: the same way he did. This expresses a shared feeling, not merely understanding why someone feels it.

Examples:

- I feel the same way about having fewer meetings; uninterrupted time helps me concentrate.
- She felt the same way as I did after the interview. / I’d probably feel the same way he did in that situation.

## `That way, + clause`

Refer back to an action or suggestion and explain how it makes a result possible. That way often introduces a consequence with can, will, could, or would; it does not require a hypothetical condition.

Examples:

- Let’s record the decisions. That way, everyone can check what we agreed on.
- I’ll take an earlier train. That way, I’ll have time to find the meeting room.
- I’d practice with a partner. That way, I could get immediate feedback.

## `I wouldn't be surprised if + clause`

Say that an outcome seems plausible or expected. A past-form verb after if can describe a future possibility, not just past time. Wouldn’t here softens the expectation; it does not mean the outcome is impossible.

Examples:

- I wouldn’t be surprised if the deadline slipped; we still have several unresolved issues.
- I wouldn’t be surprised if she accepted the offer; the role suits her experience.

## `far from + adjective`

Say that something is not at all, or not nearly, in a particular state. Far from perfect means there is still a substantial gap from perfection. This is an evaluation, rather than physical distance from a place.

Examples:

- The proposed solution is far from ideal, but it keeps the service running.
- It is far from clear whether the new policy will reduce commuting time.

## `at once · in one go · at a time`

<!-- overview-card -->

Describe timing or the size of a batch. At once can mean immediately or simultaneously; context distinguishes them. In one go means in a single continuous effort. At a time means per occasion or batch; one, ten, or another quantity supplies the batch size.

Examples:

- If the alarm sounds, leave the building at once. — Immediately.
- I cannot review three proposals at once. — Simultaneously.
- We completed the migration in one go instead of splitting it over several evenings.
- The tool processes ten files at a time; I review one result at a time.

## `at work · off work · have a day off`

<!-- overview-card -->

Describe work status or a break from work. At work means working or at your workplace. Off work means not working during a stated period, including scheduled time away or illness. A day off is a day when you do not have to work; it does not mean leaving your job.

Examples:

- I’m at work right now; I’ll call you after the meeting.
- She is off work this week while she recovers from the flu.
- I have a day off on Friday, so I’m planning a short trip.
