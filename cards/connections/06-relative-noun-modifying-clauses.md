# 06 Relative and Noun-Modifying Clauses

## C06-001

<!-- card
item: REL-001
type: context
status: validated
-->

### Front

A review panel received twelve proposals. Combine the statements so the
relative clause identifies the one proposal that received funding:

One proposal addressed both housing supply and public transit. It received
funding.

### Back

**The proposal that addressed both housing supply and public transit received
funding.**

A defining relative clause identifies which member of a larger group is
meant. Removing the clause here would change the reference from one particular
proposal to an unspecified proposal.

**Also OK:** The proposal **which** addressed both housing supply and public
transit received funding. **Which** is a fully standard defining alternative
in contemporary AmE; a preference for **that** is a style choice, not a
universal grammar rule.

## C06-002

<!-- card
item: REL-002
type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Combine each set of facts with a relative clause.

1. A company has ten analysts, but only four completed the security training.
   Only those four may access the database.
2. A smaller company has three analysts. All three completed the training.
   They may access the database.

### Back

1. **The analysts who completed the security training may access the
   database.**
2. **The analysts, who completed the security training, may access the
   database.**

Without commas, the defining clause selects four analysts from a larger group.
With commas, the non-defining clause adds information about all three already
identified analysts. The punctuation reflects a real change in reference.

## C06-003

<!-- card

-->

### Front

Add a subject relative clause about an already identified person:

Dr. Chen will lead the inquiry. She developed the original safety protocol.

### Back

**Dr. Chen, who developed the original safety protocol, will lead the
inquiry.**

In `who + verb`, **who** is the subject of the relative clause. It cannot be
omitted. Because this clause is non-defining, **that** is not available.

### Front

Complete each thing-relative. Do not omit the relative-clause subject.

1. The Aurora satellite, ___ monitors coastal erosion, failed to transmit
   data overnight.
2. Select the sensor ___ detects the smallest changes in air pressure.

### Back

1. **The Aurora satellite, which monitors coastal erosion, failed to transmit
   data overnight.**
2. **Select the sensor which detects the smallest changes in air pressure.**

In both sentences, **which** performs the relative-clause verb, so it is a
subject relative and cannot be omitted.

**Also OK for sentence 2:** Select the sensor **that** detects the smallest
changes in air pressure.

### Front

Complete the defining subject relatives with the same marker: one modifies a
person, and one modifies a thing.

1. The nurse ___ noticed the dosage error immediately alerted the physician.
2. We need the software patch ___ prevents the crash.

### Back

1. **The nurse that noticed the dosage error immediately alerted the
   physician.**
2. **We need the software patch that prevents the crash.**

In a defining relative, **that** can be the expressed subject for a person or
a thing. A subject marker cannot be omitted.

**Also OK:** **who** for the nurse and **which** for the software patch.

## C06-006

<!-- card
item: REL-006
type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Complete the same defining object relative in the requested register.

1. neutral conversation: The consultant ___ we hired starts Monday.
2. deliberately formal report: The consultant ___ we hired starts Monday.

### Back

1. **The consultant who we hired starts Monday.**
2. **The consultant whom we hired starts Monday.**

The following subject **we** shows that the relative pronoun is the object of
**hired**. **Who** is normal in neutral AmE; **whom** is chiefly formal in this
unfronted position.

In this defining relative, **that** or no pronoun is also grammatical:
`the consultant that we hired` / `the consultant we hired`.

Complete the defining thing-object relative with every standard neutral
marker, including omission:

### Back

**The council adopted the resilience plan which local researchers had

-->

### Front

Use no relative pronoun where omission is licensed; otherwise use **that**.

1. The report ___ the auditors released identified three control failures.
2. The report ___ identified three control failures was released on Friday.

### Back

1. **The report the auditors released identified three control failures.**
2. **The report that identified three control failures was released on
   Friday.**

In sentence 1, **the auditors** is the visible subject and the omitted relative
marker is the object of **released**. In sentence 2, the marker itself is the
subject of **identified**, so it cannot be omitted.

**Also OK for sentence 2:** **which** instead of **that**.

### Front

Write a neutral version and a deliberately formal version of the database
relative. Preserve **in** in both.

The institute maintains a database. Researchers store genomic records in it.

### Back

1. **The institute maintains a database which researchers store genomic
   records in.**
2. **The institute maintains a database in which researchers store genomic
   records.**

Neutral standard English can strand **in** at the end of the relative clause.
Formal writing may front it as `in which`; fronting requires **which**, never
**that**.

**Also OK for neutral sentence 1:** `a database that researchers store genomic
records in`, `a database researchers store genomic records in`, or `a database
where researchers store genomic records`.

Complete the defining time relative:

I clearly remember the winter ___ the river froze for six consecutive weeks.

### Back

**I clearly remember the winter when the river froze for six consecutive

## C06-014

<!-- card

-->

### Front

These completed defining relatives are all standard:

- `the date when the revised standard took effect`
- `the date that the revised standard took effect`
- `the date the revised standard took effect`

Which form specifically packages the time relation as a relative adverb? Then
write the deliberately formal version with **on** fronted.

### Back

The relative adverb is **when**:
**The agency announced the date when the revised standard took effect.**

The deliberately formal version is:
**The agency announced the date on which the revised standard took effect.**

Defining time relatives also permit **that** or zero here. Formal writing can
front a meaningful preposition: `on which` for a date or day.

## C06-015

<!-- card
item: REL-015
type: context
status: validated
-->

### Front

Complete the defining reason relative with the standard neutral possibilities,
then give the formal version with a fronted preposition:

The report explains the reason ___ many rural clinics struggle to retain
specialists.

### Back

**The report explains the reason why many rural clinics struggle to retain
specialists.**

**Also OK:** `the reason that many rural clinics struggle`, `the reason many
rural clinics struggle`, and, formally, `the reason for which many rural
clinics struggle`.

`reason + why + finite clause` gives the explanation associated with a reason.
Unlike **because**, **why** introduces a relative clause modifying **reason**.
**Because** introduces a reason clause and cannot simply replace these markers
inside this noun phrase.

## C06-016

<!-- card
item: REL-016
type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Express the same defining relationship in two registers.

1. neutral: the community organizer / I spoke with her / proposed the program
2. formal: the same organizer, with the preposition fronted

### Back

1. **The community organizer who I spoke with proposed the program.**
2. **The community organizer with whom I spoke proposed the program.**

Neutral relative clauses commonly strand the preposition:
`who + subject + verb + preposition`. Formal writing may front it:
`preposition + whom + subject + verb`.

Because sentence 1 is defining, **that** or zero is also possible:
`the organizer that I spoke with` / `the organizer I spoke with`.

The same contrast works with other prepositions: `the consultant who we sent
the draft to` is neutral standard English; `the consultant to whom we sent the
draft` is deliberately formal.

### Front

Join the statements with one non-defining relative that refers to every member
of the four-filter set:

The laboratory tested four low-cost water filters. Every filter removed more
than 95 percent of the contaminant.

### Back

**The laboratory tested four low-cost water filters, all of which removed more
than 95 percent of the contaminant.**

`all of which` refers to every member of a recoverable plural set. **All of
them** would begin a new independent clause; it cannot replace **all of which**
after this comma.

## C06-030

<!-- card
item: REL-030
type: context
status: validated
-->

### Front

Combine each pair with a to-infinitive noun modifier.

1. Priya arrived first. She was the first person.
2. One policy was the most effective. It emerged from the independent review.
3. Marcus met every technical requirement. He was the only applicant.

### Back

1. **Priya was the first person to arrive.**
2. **This was the most effective policy to emerge from the independent
   review.**
3. **Marcus was the only applicant to meet every technical requirement.**

To-infinitives commonly modify nouns selected by ordinals and superlatives,
and nouns selected by words such as **first**, **last**, **next**, and
**only**. Here the noun is the understood subject of the infinitive action:
the person arrives, the policy emerges, and the applicant meets the
requirements.

### Front

Use to-infinitive modifiers to complete both requests.

1. The new employee needs a knowledgeable person whom she can ask about
   payroll.
2. We have a long train journey ahead and need some food for the journey.

### Back

1. **The new employee needs someone to ask about payroll.**
2. **We need something to eat during the long train journey.**

`someone/something + to-infinitive` describes a needed, available, possible,

## C06-033

### Front

Use a to-infinitive modifier for each noun.

1. The waiting room needs a comfortable chair. Patients can sit on it.
2. Before Friday, the team still has several tasks. It must finish them.
3. The next applicant will be interviewed at 2:00 p.m.

### Back

1. **The waiting room needs a comfortable chair to sit on.**
2. **The team still has several tasks to finish before Friday.**
3. **The next applicant to be interviewed is scheduled for 2:00 p.m.**

finish **the tasks**. Sentence 3 uses overt passive `to be + past participle`
because the applicant receives the action.

## C06-037

<!-- card
item: REL-041
type: context

-->

### Front

Turn the second sentence into a comma-delimited relative comment on the entire
first statement:

The council postponed the vote for a third time. This frustrated local
residents.

### Back

**The council postponed the vote for a third time, which frustrated local
residents.**

Here, sentence-relative **which** refers to the whole preceding proposition—the
repeated postponement—not merely to the nearest noun **time** or **vote**.
**That** cannot introduce this comma-delimited construction.

Other natural whole-clause paraphrases include `which means another delay is
likely` and `which is why residents protested`.
