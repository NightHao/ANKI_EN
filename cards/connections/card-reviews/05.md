# Independent card audit — Module 05 Conjunctions and Clause Linkers

Scope: all 74 cards in `05-conjunction-clause-linkers.md`, checked against the
complete Module 05 rows in `coverage.tsv`, `CONNECTION_DECK_PLAN.md`,
`CARD_AUTHORING.md`, and `inventory-reviews/05-07.md`. The review tested
natural contemporary AmE, exact card/item/type mapping, one retrieval decision,
the answer space of every printed sentence, Back and `Also OK:` accuracy,
IELTS-7/C1 usefulness, false exclusivity, duplicate families, reduced-clause
subject control, and one-home routing. This is a review only; no source,
inventory, builder, or status was changed.

## Verdict

**Do not validate Module 05 yet.** Structural mapping and lint are exact, and
the reduced-clause cards generally handle subject control well. However, only
**38 of 74 current cards are safe to validate unchanged** under the master
plan's strict semantic and closed-answer standard.

The principal blockers are:

- C05-017 repeats the exact ambiguity the plan warns about: **although
  receiving** is grammatical alongside **despite receiving**;
- several context Fronts print the target linker in the instruction, leaving
  no retrieval decision;
- many ordinary linker blanks accept common alternatives that the Back does
  not acknowledge;
- C05-033's `if` sentence asks the learner to save a precautionary copy only
  after the network is already unavailable;
- the `so that` / `in order that` cards do not close their modal choices;
- C05-055 incorrectly implies that contemporary AmE **like** cannot introduce
  a clause;
- **by contrast** and **in contrast** are both standard sentence connectors,
  so C05-060/061 falsely force one;
- the manifest's blanket exclusion of reduced **even though** conflicts with
  an authoritative grammar reference; and
- the requested reduced grid still has no home for `while + predicative NP`
  or `if + predicative NP`.

## Must-fix cards and inventory decisions

### M1 — C05-017 / LINK-017 has the plan's explicitly forbidden open Front

Sentence 2 is:

> ___ receiving hundreds of objections, the council approved the plan.

Both **Despite receiving ...** and **Although receiving ...** are grammatical,
and this deck intentionally teaches the latter in C05-018. `In spite of` is a
third accepted answer. The `V-ing` label does not distinguish a preposition
complement from a reduced concessive clause.

Replace sentence 2 with a `V-ing` clause that has its own overt subject, which
blocks the reduced-`although` analysis:

> 2. ___ hundreds of residents objecting, the council approved the plan.
>    (`V-ing` clause with its own expressed subject)

Back:

> 2. **Despite hundreds of residents objecting, the council approved the
> plan.**
>
> **Also OK:** **In spite of hundreds of residents objecting, ...**

This retains the intended `despite/in spite of + NP/V-ing` versus
`although + finite clause` decision.

### M2 — C05-002 / LINK-002 uses an awkward learner model

**Because of being unable to reach the village safely** is grammatical but
heavy and unidiomatic compared with a finite clause or a possessive/NP
alternative. Do not make an IELTS-7 learner retrieve this wording.

Use:

> 3. The clinic canceled its outreach visit ___ the only access road being
>    flooded. (`V-ing` clause)

Back:

> 3. **The clinic canceled its outreach visit because of the only access road
> being flooded.**

This also makes the embedded subject explicit and avoids the current shift
from **clinic** to **clinic staff**.

### M3 — C05-016 / LINK-016 and C05-030 / LINK-031 are reciprocal duplicates

Both cards retrieve the same factual **even though** versus hypothetical
**even if** decision with reservoir/bridge substitutions. A learner cannot
know one without knowing the other. Merge the inventory rows into one contrast
decision and retain one card, preferably C05-016. Remove C05-030 after the
inventory merge.

Safe retained Front:

> 1. Even ___ the reservoir is currently full, restrictions remain in place.
>    (known fact)
> 2. Even ___ the reservoir becomes full next month, restrictions will remain
>    in place. (hypothetical case)

This retrieves **though/if** without pretending plain **although/if** are
ungrammatical in the original unrestricted blanks.

### M4 — C05-003, C05-004, C05-024, C05-028, C05-032, C05-038, C05-040,
C05-044, C05-045, and C05-056 reveal the target instead of testing it

These Fronts say “with **since/as/while/if/provided/providing/when/until/once/as
if/as though**,” then ask the learner to copy that expression into a blank.
That is not one retrieval decision.

This criticism does **not** apply to cards such as C05-018–022, C05-046,
C05-049–052, and C05-065–074. Their linker remains printed because the actual
decision is how to reduce a finite clause while preserving subject control.
It also does not apply to C05-010/C05-059, whose decision is punctuation, or
C05-014, whose decision is the licensed position of **though**.

Use completed-sentence recognition/meaning contrasts where synonyms make a
blank inherently open. Concrete replacements:

- **C05-003:** print **Since all applicants already have the timetable ...**
  and **The archive has been closed since March.** Ask:
  “Which **since** introduces a background reason, and which marks a starting
  time?” The Back answers **1 reason; 2 starting time**.
- **C05-004:** keep only the causal item and print:
  “**As the laboratory already has suitable equipment, no additional
  purchase is necessary.** What relation does initial **as** express here?”
  Back: **a background reason**, with **because** for foregrounded reason.
- **C05-024:** print its two completed **while** sentences and ask which is
  contrast and which is simultaneity.
- **C05-028:** make the open-condition decision explicit against certainty:
  “The review may or may not confirm the concern. ___ it does, the agency will
  withdraw the product.” Back **If**; `when` would present confirmation as
  expected/certain.
- **C05-032:** print one sentence with **provided that** and one with
  **providing that**, then ask whether both are standard and what register they
  share. Do not give the forms and the register on the Front.
- **C05-038:** contrast uncertain **if** with expected/repeated **when**, or
  convert it to a completed `when + finite clause` recognition card.
- **C05-040:** print the completed **as** sentence and ask whether the intended
  relation is time or reason. Use a context without plausible causation, e.g.
  **As the orchestra tuned its instruments, audience members found their
  seats.**
- **C05-044:** remove “with **until**” and add **Also OK: till** as the
  conversational variant.
- **C05-045:** print **Once the vaccine has passed ...** and ask what
  additional threshold/completion meaning **once** adds over general
  **when**.
- **C05-056:** print both completed forms and ask for their relationship and
  register. The Back should say that both are standard, **as if** is much more
  common, and **as though** can sound more formal.

### M6 — C05-009 / LINK-009 and C05-011 / LINK-011 have unrestricted lexical
alternatives

C05-009's sentence accepts **given**, **based on**, and **in view of** as well
as **in light of**. C05-011 sentence 1 accepts **therefore**, **consequently**,
and **accordingly**; sentence 2 accepts **because of**, **due to**, and
**owing to** as well as **as a result of**.

Use structural retrieval rather than synonym guessing:

- **C05-009 Front:**  
  `decision/assessment + in light of + relevant new information`  
  “What does this frame imply?”
  
  Back: it frames a decision as responsive to the information. Add:
  **Also standard:** `in view of + NP`; **given + NP** is a shorter neutral
  alternative.
- **C05-011 Front:**

  > Add the missing punctuation or **of**.
  >
  > 1. The river exceeded the threshold ___ As a result ___ the footbridge
  >    was closed.
  > 2. The footbridge was closed as a result ___ the river exceeding the
  >    threshold.

  Back: **`. As a result,`** and **`as a result of`**.

This makes the decision connector punctuation versus prepositional **of**, not
which result synonym the author happened to choose.

### M7 — C05-001, C05-013, C05-025, C05-029, C05-031, and C05-047 omit
ordinary alternatives

- **C05-001 / LINK-001:** direct answer to **why** strongly favors
  **because**, but **since** and **as** remain grammatical. Keep **because** as
  the foregrounded answer and add: “**Since/as** are possible if the reason is
  backgrounded; they are not the best direct answer to the printed **why**
  question.”
- **C05-013 / LINK-013:** the Back lists **though**, but **even though** and
  concessive **while** also fit the exact sentence. Convert it to a completed
  core-pattern recognition card or list those meaning/register alternatives;
  do not present the blank as uniquely **although**.
- **C05-025 / LINK-026:** **While private tenants receive ...** is a normal
  alternative to **Whereas ...**. Add `Also OK: while`, while preserving
  **whereas** as the more explicitly analytical/formal choice.
- **C05-029 / LINK-030:** sentence 2 also permits **when** if withdrawal is
  treated as expected. Add “**when** is possible only with that changed
  assumption,” or state on the Front that withdrawal is an open possibility.
- **C05-031 / LINK-032:** **so long as** and **on condition that** exactly fit
  the three-word blank. Replace the blank with a bounded comparison:
  “Which two variants are standard for the condition meaning:
  **as long as / so long as**?” Back: both; **so long as** is a little more
  informal. Cambridge explicitly records this overlap.
- **C05-047 / LINK-048:** **when** and **once** fit the printed sentence, but
  are less specific about immediacy. Add an explicit scale:
  “Choose the linker that guarantees the earliest follow-up,” and state on the

### M8 — C05-026 / LINK-027 has an incorrect word count

**Regardless of whether** is three words, not four. Change “a four-word
phrase” to “a three-word phrase.” If **or not** is added, the result is five
words, not four.

The grammar itself is sound: **whether**, not **if**, is required after the
preposition **of**.

### M9 — C05-033 / LINK-034 has a semantically impossible `if` instruction

The current second sentence says:

> Save a local copy if the network is unavailable when you need the file.

At that point the learner cannot obtain the network file to save it locally.
Use an action that can actually occur after the condition becomes true:

> 1. Save a local copy in case the network goes down.
> 2. Use the local copy if the network is unavailable when you need the file.

Back:

> **In case** motivates an earlier precaution; **if** states the later
> condition under which the prepared response applies.

This preserves the high-value distinction confirmed by Cambridge's `In case
(of)` grammar entry.

### M10 — C05-037 / LINK-038 gives the conventional formula a false
shared-subject explanation

**Unless stated otherwise** is idiomatic, but its natural expansion is
“unless something/the instructions state otherwise,” not “unless the
interviews are stated otherwise.” The Back's “affected subject is recoverable”
invites the learner to apply the ordinary passive shared-subject rule where a
fixed elliptical formula is doing the work.

Use:

> **Unless otherwise stated** is a conventional passive formula meaning
> “unless another rule or instruction says otherwise.” Do not infer that
> **interviews** is the thing “stated.”

`Unless stated otherwise` is also standard; choose one order as the main
chunk and list the other under `Also OK:`.

### M11 — C05-043 / LINK-044 has arbitrary lexical-answer blanks

Before **opening/accessing/examining/handling** the files and after
**reviewing/checking/examining/closing** them are all plausible. The card is
supposed to retrieve the `before/after + V-ing` form, not guess the author's
verbs.

Supply lemmas:

> 1. Before ___ the confidential files, Priya checked the access permissions.
>    (**open**)
> 2. After ___ the confidential files, Priya deleted the temporary copies.
>    (**review**)

Then **opening/reviewing** are objectively gradeable.

### M12 — C05-053 / LINK-054 and C05-054 / LINK-055 do not close the modal
choice

In C05-053, **could understand** and **can understand** are both natural:
**could** backshifts with the past translation event; **can** is natural if
the translated form remains available now. In C05-054, **may**, **can**, and a
plain present form can occur after **in order that**. Cambridge states that
purpose **so that/in order that** often take modals, not that one modal is
forced; it also gives `in order that ... can ...` and present-simple examples.

Concrete repairs:

- **C05-053:** make the situation wholly past:
  “For last year's completed field trial, the team translated the consent form
  so that participants ___ understand it during enrollment. (**can**)”
  Back **could**, or retain the current sentence and add
  `Also OK: can understand it` with the continuing-availability reading.
- **C05-054:** supply the modal in the Front and retrieve the finite-clause
  boundary:

  > The agency publishes anonymized records ___ independent researchers may
  > verify its conclusions. (formal purpose linker)

  Back **in order that**. Add:
  `Also OK: so that ... can/may verify`; `in order that ... can verify` is
  also grammatical.

### M13 — C05-055 / LINK-056 falsely excludes `like + clause`

Contemporary informal AmE permits:

> Jonah looked **like he had not rested at all**.

The Back's statement “Unlike `like + NP`, **as if** introduces a clause” is
therefore false as an exclusivity claim. Cambridge explicitly records
clausal **like** as an informal alternative to **as if**.

Use:

> `as if/as though + finite clause` are neutral-to-formal comparison linkers.
> Informal AmE also commonly uses `like + finite clause`.

Add **as though** and informal **like** under `Also OK:` for the exact printed
sentence, or turn the card into a register contrast rather than a unique blank.

### M14 — C05-060 / LINK-061 and C05-061 / LINK-062 force a nonexistent
`by/in contrast` distinction

Merriam-Webster heads the expression **by/in contrast**, and current Cambridge
American examples use both as sentence connectors. Therefore:

- C05-060's two-word blank accepts **By contrast** and **In contrast**.
- C05-061 sentence 1 accepts both.
- C05-061 sentence 2 also accepts **Unlike**, **Compared with**, and
  **Compared to**, although those are different lexical frames.

Use a form-conversion card:

> Convert the sentence connector to the NP-complement frame, preserving the
> words **contrast** and **coastal region**.
>
> The coastal region invested heavily in flood defenses. **By/In contrast,**
> the inland region prioritized wildfire prevention.
>
> → **In contrast to the coastal region, the inland region prioritized
> wildfire prevention.**

Back:

> Both **by contrast** and **in contrast** can be sentence connectors.
> `in contrast to/with + NP` supplies an explicit comparison standard.

Do not claim **by contrast** is the sole connector.

### M15 — C05-062 / LINK-063 is not uniquely `contrary to`

Given a claim whose content is contradicted, **Despite the manufacturer's
claim, ...** is also grammatical, although it profiles non-prevention rather
than direct contradiction. **In contrast to the manufacturer's claim** is
possible, if less pointed.

Print the target phrase and retrieve the semantic distinction:

> **Contrary to the manufacturer's claim, the device cannot operate
> continuously for twelve hours.**
>
> Does **contrary to** merely compare two cases, or assert a conflict?

Back: it asserts that reality conflicts with the claim; **in contrast to**
normally compares, and **despite** means the claim did not prevent the result.

### M16 — C05-063 / LINK-064 and C05-064 / LINK-BY-THE-TIME remain open

C05-063 sentence 1 also accepts **In the event of a fire**; sentence 2 can take
**if** under a conditional reading. Make it a direct shape transformation:

> Change only the complement shape:
>
> **In case of a fire**, use the stairs. (`NP`)
>
> Keep a flashlight nearby **in case the power fails**. (finite clause)

Ask what disappears before the finite clause: **of**.

C05-064 sentence 1 also accepts **Before the inspection team arrived**;
sentence 2 accepts **before noon** with the stricter “earlier than noon”
meaning. Keep **by the time/by** as the deadline targets but add:

> **Also grammatical, with a stricter meaning:** **Before the inspection team
> arrived ... / before noon.** `By` includes the deadline; `before` excludes
> it.

### M18 — C05-065 / LINK-THOUGH-VING and C05-073 / LINK-WHEN-PRED-NP
overstate neutral usage

Both are grammatical, but the current Backs and inventory register labels are
too neutral for production guidance:

- Cambridge labels `although/though + -ing` as formal. Revise C05-065's
  register from `conversational/neutral` to **formal/compact**. A more idiomatic
  model is:
  **Though working two part-time jobs, Amir maintained an excellent academic
  record.**
- **When a medical student, Dr. Chen ...** is grammatical but markedly more
  compressed/formal than **When she was a medical student ...** and competes
  in natural prose with **As a medical student ...**. State that register
  explicitly; do not teach the reduction as the neutral default.

## Authoritative checks used for disputed claims

- Cambridge, [Clauses: finite and
  non-finite](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/clauses-finite-and-non-finite):
  non-finite clauses commonly share the main-clause subject and occur after
  linkers including **after, although, though,** and **if**.
- Cambridge, [Although or
  though?](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/although-or-though):
  `although/though + -ing` is formal; **although, though, and even though** can
  introduce reduced verbless clauses; sentence-final **though** is especially
  spoken.
- Cambridge, [As if and as
  though](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/as-if-):
  both are standard; informal **like + clause** can serve a similar function.
- Cambridge, [As long as and so long
  as](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/as-long-as-and-so-long-as):
  both can mean `provided/providing/on condition that`, with **so long as** a
  little more informal.
- Cambridge, [In case
  (of)](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/in-case-of.):
  **in case** motivates preparation for a possible event; it does not simply
  mean **if**; `in case of + NP` is the prepositional form.
- Cambridge, [So that or in order
  that?](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/so-that-or-in-order-that):
  both express purpose and often occur with several modals; **so that** is far
  more common and **in order that** more formal.
- Merriam-Webster, [by/in
  contrast](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/by%2Fin%20contrast):
  both forms are established for comparison, defeating a unique **by
  contrast** blank.
- CDC, [“However” Does Not Equal
  “But”](https://www.cdc.gov/nceh/clearwriting/writing-tips/2025/however-does-not-equal-but.html):
  **but** is a coordinator; conjunctive-adverb **however** takes a semicolon
  (or sentence boundary) before it and a comma after it in the audited use.
- Cambridge, [thanks
  to](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/dictionary/english/thanks-to):
  the idiom means “because of” and is explicitly illustrated with a
  disapproving negative-result use, supporting C05-007's ironic example.

These are descriptive grammar/dictionary sources. Their examples were used to
check claims; no example was copied into a proposed card.
