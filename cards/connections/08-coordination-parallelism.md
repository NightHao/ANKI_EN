# 08 Coordination and Parallelism

## C08-001

<!-- card
item: COORD-AND-ADDITION
type: chunk
status: validated
-->

### Front

Retrieve the coordinator frame for adding one parallel unit to another:
`X ___ Y`.

### Back

Use **X and Y** to add parallel words, phrases, or clauses.

- The role involves **research and policy analysis**.
- We interviewed residents **and reviewed the survey data**.
- The pilot reduced waiting times, **and it improved staff morale**.

## C08-002

<!-- card
item: COORD-BUT-CONTRAST
type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Use **but**, **although**, and **however** once each. Complete each contrast
with the structure indicated.

1. The initial trial was small, ___ its findings were encouraging.
   (coordinator joining two independent clauses)
2. ___ the initial trial was small, its findings were encouraging.
   (subordinator)
3. The initial trial was small; ___, its findings were encouraging.
   (conjunctive adverb)

### Back

1. The initial trial was small, **but its findings were encouraging**.
2. **Although the initial trial was small, its findings were encouraging**.
3. The initial trial was small; **however, its findings were encouraging**.

Use `clause, but clause`, `although + clause, clause`, or
`clause; however, clause`. The three markers express contrast but require
different clause structures and punctuation.

## C08-004

<!-- card
item: COORD-NOR-NEGATIVE-ADDITION
type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Complete the negative additions.

1. The device did not overheat, ___ did it lose power.
   (a second independent clause)
2. The device showed ___ overheating ___ power loss.
   (two phrase-level alternatives)

### Back

1. The device did not overheat, **nor did it lose power**.
2. The device showed **neither overheating nor power loss**.

After a negative clause, clause-level **nor** introduces another negative
independent clause and triggers subject–auxiliary inversion. Use
`neither X nor Y` to coordinate two excluded phrases.

## C08-005

<!-- card
item: COORD-YET-UNEXPECTED-CONTRAST
type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Use **yet** and **although** once each. Complete each contrast with the
requested structure.

1. The treatment is inexpensive, ___ few clinics offer it.
   (coordinator emphasizing an unexpected contrast)
2. ___ the treatment is inexpensive, few clinics offer it.
   (concessive subordinator)

### Back

1. The treatment is inexpensive, **yet few clinics offer it**.
2. **Although the treatment is inexpensive, few clinics offer it**.

Use `clause, yet clause` to coordinate an unexpected contrast. Use
`although + clause` to make one clause subordinate.

## C08-006

<!-- card
item: COORD-SO-RESULT
type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Complete the cause–result and purpose structures.

1. The river exceeded the warning level, ___ officials closed the footbridge.
   (actual result)
2. Officials closed the footbridge ___ pedestrians would remain safe.
   (intended purpose)

### Back

1. The river exceeded the warning level, **so officials closed the
   footbridge**.
2. Officials closed the footbridge **so that pedestrians would remain safe**.

Use coordinator `so` between independent clauses for a result. Use
`so that + clause` for purpose. In sentence 2, **so** alone is also common in
informal AmE, but **so that** makes the intended-purpose reading explicit.

**Also OK for sentence 2:** Officials closed the footbridge **so pedestrians
would remain safe**.

## C08-008

<!-- card
item: COORD-SHARED-PREDICATE-NO-COMMA
type: contrast

-->

### Front

Supply punctuation for the two structures.

1. The new sensor records air quality ___ and sends an alert when pollution
   rises.
2. The new sensor records air quality ___ and the dashboard displays the
   results.

### Back

1. The new sensor **records air quality and sends an alert** when pollution
   rises.
2. The new sensor records air quality**, and the dashboard displays the
   results**.

Do not normally insert a comma between two short predicates sharing one
subject. Use a comma when **and** joins two independent clauses.

### Back

## C08-011

-->

### Front

Complete the second coordinated infinitive with a form of **improve**, as
compactly as natural:

The initiative aims to reduce food waste and ___ access to affordable meals.

### Back

The initiative aims **to reduce food waste and improve access to affordable
meals**.

One **to** may govern two coordinated infinitives:

improve clarity when the infinitives are long, heavily modified, or contrasted.

The revised eligibility rule applies ___ first-generation students and

### Back

## C08-014

<!-- card
item: COORD-REPEATED-PREPOSITION-SCOPE
type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Use **past**, **through**, and **along** once each to complete the descriptions
of two different routes.

1. The trail runs ___ the forest and the lake.
   (it passes both landmarks without entering either one)
2. The trail runs ___ the forest and ___ the lake.
   (it passes inside the forest, then follows the lakeside)

### Back

1. The trail runs **past the forest and the lake**.
2. The trail runs **through the forest and along the lake**.

In sentence 1, shared **past** governs both noun phrases. In sentence 2,
repeated preposition slots mark two distinct spatial relations and prevent the
second phrase from falling under the first preposition's scope.

## C08-015

<!-- card
item: COORD-SHARED-AUXILIARY
type: context
status: validated
-->

### Front

Complete the sentence with a form of **flag** without unnecessarily repeating
the modal:

The revised software can detect duplicate records and ___ them for review.

### Back

The revised software **can detect duplicate records and flag them for review**.

One modal can govern coordinated bare verb phrases:
`can detect ... and (can) flag ...`. Repeating **can** is grammatical, but it
is unnecessary when the same modality clearly applies to both verbs.

## C08-016

<!-- card
item: COORD-REPEATED-AUXILIARY-CONTRAST
type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Complete both policy statements using **may** and, where needed, **not**.

1. Staff ___ access the archive and download approved files.
   (both actions are permitted)
2. Staff ___ access the archive but ___ download confidential files.
   (permission for one action; prohibition for the other)

### Back

1. Staff **may access the archive and download approved files**.
2. Staff **may access the archive but may not download confidential files**.

A shared auxiliary gives both coordinated verbs the same modality. Repeat the
auxiliary when the second unit has different polarity or modality.

## C08-017

<!-- card
item: COORD-SHARED-THAT
type: context
status: validated
-->

### Front

Complete the coordinated content clauses, omitting the second marker if the
sentence remains clear:

The review concluded that the dosage was safe and ___ no further monitoring
was necessary.

### Back

The review concluded **that the dosage was safe and no further monitoring was
necessary**.

The reporting verb **concluded** selects both content clauses, so the second
**that** may be omitted. Repeating it is also correct—and often clearer with
long clauses: `concluded that ... and that ...`.

## C08-020

<!-- card
item: COORD-BOTH-SUBJECT-AGREEMENT
type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Choose the verb for each subject structure.

1. Both the physician and the pharmacist ___ available.
2. The physician, as well as the pharmacist, ___ available.

### Back

1. Both the physician and the pharmacist **are available**.
2. The physician, as well as the pharmacist, **is available**.

`Both NP and NP` forms a compound subject and normally takes a plural verb.
An **as well as** supplement does not make the first singular head plural.

## C08-023

<!-- card
item: COORD-EITHER-SUBJECT-AGREEMENT
type: context
status: validated
-->

### Front

Complete the sentence in standard AmE:

Either the clinic or the pharmacy ___ open on Sundays.

### Back

Either the clinic or the pharmacy **is open on Sundays**.

With an `either...or` subject, agreement usually follows the nearer subject;
here both alternatives are singular, so **is** is uncontroversial. With
mixed-number alternatives, recasting is often smoother than relying on
proximity: **Either the clinic will open, or the pharmacies will.**

## C08-031

<!-- card

type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Complete the two kinds of alternatives.

1. ___ online ___ in person, all participants receive the same materials.
   (the outcome applies under either alternative)
2. Participants may attend ___ online ___ in person.
   (a direct choice between two options)

### Back

1. **Whether online or in person, all participants receive the same
   materials.**
2. Participants may attend **either online or in person**.

`Whether X or Y` can leave the choice open while stating what holds under both
alternatives. `Either X or Y` directly presents a choice. Both pairs introduce
parallel units.

## C08-035

<!-- card
item: COORD-RATHER-THAN-NP
type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Use **rather than** and **as opposed to** once each to complete the matched
noun-phrase alternatives.

1. The shortage reflects a distribution problem ___ a production failure.
   (preference/correction)
2. The shortage reflects a distribution problem ___ a production failure.
   (neutral contrast)

### Back

1. The shortage reflects **a distribution problem rather than a production
   failure**.
2. The shortage reflects **a distribution problem as opposed to a production
   failure**.

`NP rather than NP` presents the first noun phrase as the preferred or more
accurate alternative. `NP as opposed to NP` makes a more neutral explicit
contrast.

## C08-039

<!-- card
item: COORD-AS-WELL-AS-ADDITION
type: contrast
status: validated
-->

### Front

Use **as well as** to complete the additions so that the emphasized head comes
first.

1. The director, ___ the regional managers, will attend.
   (the director is the head; managers are supplementary)
2. The regional managers, ___ the director, will attend.
   (the managers are the head; director is supplementary)

### Back

1. The director, **as well as the regional managers, will attend**.
2. The regional managers, **as well as the director, will attend**.

`X as well as Y` is not fully symmetric: X remains the grammatical and
informational head, while Y is supplementary. Reversing X and Y changes that
emphasis. Plain `X and Y` coordinates equal members.

-->

### Front

Choose the verb for each subject structure.

1. The lead researcher, as well as two assistants, ___ attending the hearing.
2. The lead researcher and two assistants ___ attending the hearing.

### Back

1. The lead researcher, as well as two assistants, **is attending the

## C08-042

<!-- card
item: COORD-AND-OR-DEFERRED
type: context
status: validated
-->

### Front

Interpret the requirement accurately:

> Applicants must submit a passport and/or a national identity card.

Does this sentence require every applicant to submit both documents?

### Back

**No.** Here, `X and/or Y` means **X, Y, or both**. An applicant may submit
either document individually or submit both.

The notation is compact and common in some legal, administrative, and
technical material. In ordinary prose, make the inclusive meaning explicit:

> Applicants must submit **a passport, a national identity card, or both**.
