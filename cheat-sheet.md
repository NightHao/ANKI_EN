# English Sentence Building: Quick Rules

Use this sheet for production. The chapters explain the reasons and exceptions.

## 1. Build the main clause first

```text
SUBJECT + COMPLETE FINITE VERB PHRASE (+ OBJECT/COMPLEMENT)
```

> Mia **opened** the window.  
> Mia **is cooking** dinner.  
> Mia **has been waiting** for an hour.  
> The room **should have been cleaned**.

Group the bold words as one verb phrase.

### Finite or non-finite?

| Can carry the clause's tense/modal meaning | Cannot do so alone |
|---|---|
| `writes`, `wrote` | `writing`, `written` |
| `is writing` | `to write` |
| `has written` | `having written` |
| `was written` | `being written` |
| `can write` | `to be written` |

An `-ing` or past-participle form may appear inside a complete finite phrase:

```text
is writing
has written
was written
```

Imperatives are a common special case:

> Close the door.

The base verb heads a complete clause with understood subject `you`.

## 2. Give each clause its own tense

Do not make tenses match mechanically.

> The woman who **called** yesterday **lives** next door.

Choose for each clause:

1. When is it true?
2. Is it complete, ongoing, repeated, or earlier than another event?
3. Does its result matter now?

Useful timeline:

```text
EARLIER PAST ------ LATER PAST ------ NOW ------ FUTURE
```

Future time clause:

> I will call when I **arrive**.  
> I do not know when I **will arrive**.  

The first is a time condition; the second is an embedded question.

## 3. Relative-pronoun omission

### Subject relative: keep it

> the woman **who called me**  
> the bus **that stops here**

```text
who/that = performer of the verb
```

Do not simply write `the woman called me ...` for this active meaning.

### Object relative: may omit it

> the woman **who I called**  
> the woman **I called**

> the food **that Maya prepared**  
> the food **Maya prepared**

```text
another subject + verb + ___
Maya prepared ___
```

### Do not omit

- non-restrictive relative: `My aunt, who lives nearby, ...`
- `whose`;
- relative word after a fronted preposition: `to whom`;
- a subject relative by simple deletion.

## 4. Relative-clause reduction

Reduction is optional. Keep the full clause if important meaning disappears.

| Meaning | Full form | Common reduced form |
|---|---|---|
| noun performs ongoing/action description | `the child who is waiting` | `the child waiting` |
| noun receives completed action | `the food that was prepared` | `the food prepared` |
| noun receives ongoing action | `the car that is being repaired` | `the car being repaired` |
| noun has a state | `the seats that are available` | `the seats available` |
| noun has a location | `the keys that are on the table` | `the keys on the table` |
| task, purpose, expected action | `the clothes that need washing` | `the clothes to wash / to be washed` |

### Voice rule

```text
noun performs action → V-ing
noun receives action → past participle
noun receives ongoing action → being + past participle
```

> a woman carrying a bag  
> a bag carried by a woman  
> a bag being carried upstairs

### Reduction may lose

- tense;
- progressive or perfect viewpoint;
- exact modal meaning (`must`, `can`, `need`);
- a clear clause boundary.

If the difference matters, keep the full relative clause.

## 5. Modifier placement

```text
short + familiar → before noun
details/complements → after noun
```

> a sleeping baby  
> a baby sleeping upstairs

> a broken chair  
> a chair broken during the move

> prepared food  
> food prepared by my sister

Avoid:

> the prepared food by my sister

Use:

> the food prepared by my sister  
> the food my sister prepared  
> my sister's home-cooked food

Compound modifiers:

> home-cooked food  
> AI-generated image  
> store-bought bread

## 6. Active or passive?

### Prefer active when the person matters

> the mistake he made

This is usually lighter than:

> the mistake made by him

### Use passive when

- the performer is unknown;
- the performer is unimportant or obvious;
- the affected thing is already the topic;
- you are classifying results.

> a wallet left on the bus  
> the medicine prescribed yesterday  
> the photos taken by Mia

### Verb-type rule

```text
transitive verb + object → passive often possible
intransitive verb, no object → normal passive not possible
```

> Someone opened the door. → The door was opened.  
> An accident happened. → not `An accident was happened.`

Advanced exception: some established verb + preposition combinations allow a prepositional passive:

> Someone slept in this bed. → This bed has been slept in.

The preposition stays. Do not invent these combinations freely.

Both types can use active `-ing`:

> a sleeping baby  
> an event happening nearby

Do not say:

> a happened event  
> an occurred problem

## 7. `being`, `having`, and infinitives

| Form | Core meaning | Safe example |
|---|---|---|
| `being reviewed` | ongoing passive | `the form being reviewed` |
| `having reviewed` | earlier active action | `Having reviewed it, I replied.` |
| `having been reviewed` | earlier passive action | formal; usually use a full clause |
| `to review` | purpose/task/active complement | `I plan to review it.` |
| `to be reviewed` | intended/required passive | `the form to be reviewed` |
| `to have reviewed` | earlier active action | `She seems to have reviewed it.` |
| `to have been reviewed` | earlier passive action | `It seems to have been reviewed.` |

Safe default:

- Avoid `having ...` directly as an ordinary noun modifier.
- Use `after + full clause` in conversation.
- Do not replace `has to be` with `to be` if exact necessity matters.

## 8. Reduced adverbial clauses

Reduce only when the understood subject matches the main subject.

> While **I was walking**, **I** called Maya.  
> While walking, **I** called Maya.

> After **we ate**, **we** left.  
> After eating, **we** left.

### Dangling warning

> **Wrong:** While walking home, the rain started.  
> **Right:** While walking home, I noticed the rain starting.  
> **Right:** While I was walking home, it started to rain.

Common patterns:

- `when possible`;
- `if necessary`;
- `while waiting`;
- `after eating`;
- `before leaving`;
- `once opened`;
- `although tired`;
- `unless otherwise stated`.

## 9. `during` versus `while`

```text
during + noun phrase
while + full clause
while + V-ing with matching subject
```

> during dinner  
> while we were eating dinner  
> while eating dinner

Avoid ordinary `during + action phrase`:

> not normally `during cooking dinner`  
> use `while cooking dinner` or `during dinner preparation`

## 10. Existential `there`

> There **is** one problem.  
> There **are** two problems.

Use it to introduce existence or presence:

> There is someone outside.

Use a direct subject for a status update:

> One person is still waiting.

Relative rules do not change after `there`:

> There is one issue **that needs attention**. — subject; keep `that`  
> There is one issue **we need to discuss**. — object; omission allowed

## 11. Direct and embedded questions

### Direct question

```text
question word + auxiliary + subject + main verb
```

> How **does it work**?  
> Why **did she leave**?

Subject question: no added `do`.

> Who called?  
> What happened?

### Embedded question

Use statement order:

```text
question word + subject + verb
```

> Could you explain **how it works**?  
> I do not know **why she left**.

Not:

> `Could you explain how does it work?`

Question words normally stay because they carry meaning:

> I know **why** she left.  
> I know she left. — different meaning

## 12. Naturalness check

Ask four separate questions:

1. Is it grammatical?
2. Does it express my meaning?
3. Is it a common word combination?
4. Would a speaker normally choose it here?

Examples:

| Expression | Judgment | Recommended |
|---|---|---|
| `a sleeping baby` | natural | keep |
| `events happening now` | natural | keep |
| `happening events` | unnatural for neutral meaning | `ongoing events` |
| `a happened event` | ungrammatical | `an event that happened` |
| `the error made by him` | grammatical but heavy | `the error he made` |
| `one part is still missing` | natural | keep |
| `there is one lacking part` | unnatural | `one part is missing` |

### Rewrite ladder

If uncertain, move toward a fuller structure:

```text
short modifier
→ modifier after noun
→ full relative clause
→ active clause
→ two simple sentences
```

## 13. Ten-second production check

1. What is my main subject?
2. What is my complete finite verb phrase?
3. Am I describing a noun or another event?
4. Who performs the added action?
5. Who receives it?
6. Is it ongoing, completed, required, intended, or earlier?
7. Would a full clause preserve important meaning?
8. Is the modifier short or detailed?
9. Is the word combination common?
10. Would active wording or two sentences be clearer?

Clear full clauses are always a safe option. Reduction is a tool, not a target.
