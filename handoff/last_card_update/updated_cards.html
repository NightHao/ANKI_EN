2. feel the same way (about + noun / as + person)

EN::Patterns · New

Front

feel the same way (about + noun / as + person)

Back

Say that you share another person’s opinion or emotional reaction. About introduces the topic; as introduces the person or comparison clause. A comparison clause can also follow directly: the same way he did. This expresses a shared feeling, not merely understanding why someone feels it.

Examples:

• I feel the same way about having fewer meetings; uninterrupted time helps me concentrate.
• She felt the same way as I did after the interview. / I’d probably feel the same way he did in that situation.

9. That way, + clause

EN::Patterns · New

Front

That way, + clause

Back

Refer back to an action or suggestion and explain how it makes a result possible. That way often introduces a consequence with can, will, could, or would; it does not require a hypothetical condition.

Examples:

• Let’s record the decisions. That way, everyone can check what we agreed on.
• I’ll take an earlier train. That way, I’ll have time to find the meeting room.
• I’d practice with a partner. That way, I could get immediate feedback.

10. I wouldn't be surprised if + clause

EN::Patterns · New

Front

I wouldn't be surprised if + clause

Back

Say that an outcome seems plausible or expected. A past-form verb after if can describe a future possibility, not just past time. Wouldn’t here softens the expectation; it does not mean the outcome is impossible.

Examples:

• I wouldn’t be surprised if the deadline slipped; we still have several unresolved issues.
• I wouldn’t be surprised if she accepted the offer; the role suits her experience.

humble

ADJECTIVE

1. Make someone feel less proud or more aware of their limits.

Everyday situations: what you can say

SituationNatural sentencesWhat to notice
Someone has achieved something without boasting.“She is modest about her success.” / “She remains humble despite her success.”Both are natural. Modest often highlights how someone presents achievements; humble can emphasize their attitude toward themselves.
You describe a small amount or limited change.“We made a modest improvement.”Modest is the ordinary choice for a limited amount; humble does not usually describe the size of an improvement.
You describe simple circumstances.“They lived in a modest home.” / “They lived in a humble home.”Both can describe simplicity. Humble can draw more attention to low status or lack of luxury.

Difference / overlap

Modest and humble overlap when describing someone who does not boast. Neither word automatically means lacking confidence. Keep their other senses distinct: a modest increase; humble beginnings; an experience that humbled someone.

humble: remain/stay humble; humble beginnings; a humble home; be humbled by + experience

66. In my experience, + clause · As far as I know, + clause · From what I have heard, + clause · Apparently, + clause

EN::Patterns · Revised

Front

In my experience, + clause · As far as I know, + clause · From what I have heard, + clause · Apparently, + clause

Back

Patterns on this card:
In my experience, + clause · As far as I know, + clause · From what I have heard, + clause · Apparently, + clause

Identify the basis or limits of a claim. Experience means firsthand experience; as far as I know allows for missing information. The related expression as far as I can tell limits a judgment to available observations; it overlaps with as far as I know when those observations are the basis of your knowledge. From what I have heard identifies a report. Apparently presents something as seeming true from reports or evidence, without guaranteeing it.

Examples:

• In my experience, written decisions reduce misunderstandings across time zones.
• As far as I know, the office is still open on Fridays.
• As far as I can tell, the rollout succeeded; the error rate is back to normal.
• From what I have heard, the new manager is very approachable.
• Apparently, the venue has changed; I just received a new invitation.

67. I am sure/certain/convinced/confident (that) + clause

EN::Patterns · Revised

Front

I am sure/certain/convinced/confident (that) + clause

Back

State a strong belief or assurance. Sure and certain are close alternatives; convinced stresses that you hold a belief firmly. Confident often stresses assurance about an outcome or ability. Context allows substantial overlap; the wording does not prove the claim. Pretty sure softens sure: you feel fairly confident while leaving room for doubt.

Examples:

• I am sure this is the right address. / I am certain this is the right address. / I am pretty sure this is the right address.
• I am convinced that the old process needs to change.
• I am confident that we can finish the migration this month.

68. simple past · had + past participle · had been + V-ing

EN::Patterns · Revised

Front

simple past · had + past participle · had been + V-ing

Back

Patterns on this card:
simple past · had + past participle · had been + V-ing

Use the simple past for the main events in a past account. Use the past perfect when you need to look back from one past moment to an earlier event. Do not use the past perfect merely because an event happened a long time ago.

Use had been + V-ing to look back at an activity continuing up to a past moment. Had just/barely + past participle + when places an action shortly before another event; barely emphasizes how little time passed. In I’d just sat down, I’d means I had; in I’d ask, it means I would.

Examples:

• I had just sent the proposal when the client called. / I sent the proposal and then called the client.
• I’d barely fallen asleep when the alarm went off.
• She ate before the meeting. / She was not hungry because she had eaten before the meeting.
• After I finished the report, I sent it. / By the time I sent the report, I had checked every figure.
• I had been waiting for an hour when the clinic finally called my name.
